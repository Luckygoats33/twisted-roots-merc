Caulking a Bathtub So It Does Not Go Black

Old caulk out, surface bone dry, tub full of water, one continuous bead. Skip any of those and you will redo it in a year.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The joint where a tub meets tile moves. The tub flexes down when it fills and springs back when it drains, and any rigid material in that joint cracks. Which is why it is caulked rather than grouted, and why the caulk has a service life. When it goes black or splits, water gets behind the tile, and in a coastal bathroom that never fully dries, that is where trouble starts.

What you need

  • Kitchen and bath silicone with a mildew inhibitor
  • A caulk gun
  • A utility knife and a caulk removal tool or a stiff plastic scraper
  • Denatured alcohol or mineral spirits, and rags
  • Painter's tape, optional but it helps

All the old caulk has to come out

New silicone will not bond to old silicone. Not partly, not mostly. If you lay a fresh bead over the old one you have made a decorative strip that will peel off in sheets, and you have sealed mildew inside where it will keep growing and show through.

  1. Score along both edges of the old bead with a utility knife. Cut into the caulk, not into the tub finish or the tile glaze.
  2. Pull the bead out in a strip if it will come. Silicone often will.
  3. Scrape the residue with a plastic scraper. Get every trace, including the thin film that is left behind.
  4. Wipe the joint down with denatured alcohol on a rag. This removes soap film and silicone residue, both of which stop adhesion.
  5. If there is black mildew in the joint or on the substrate, clean it with a bleach solution, rinse, and then let it dry completely.

The tub gets filled first

This is the step that separates a bead that lasts from one that splits in three months. Fill the tub with water before you caulk. The weight pulls the tub down to its loaded position and opens the joint to its widest. Caulk it in that state, let it cure fully, then drain. Now the joint closes slightly and the caulk goes into gentle compression rather than being stretched every bath.

Get in the tub yourself if it is a fiberglass or acrylic unit. Same principle, and those flex more than cast iron.

Laying the bead

  1. Make sure everything is dry. Not damp. Silicone will not adhere to a wet surface, and a bathroom on this coast holds moisture for a long time. Run the fan, or leave it overnight with a fan on it.
  2. Cut the nozzle at a 45 degree angle, small. Start smaller than you think. You can always cut more off, and the classic mistake is a hole big enough to lay a bead you then have to clean up.
  3. Tape both sides of the joint if you want a crisp line, leaving a consistent gap.
  4. Push the bead rather than pull it, keeping steady pressure and moving at a steady speed. Do the whole run in one pass if you can. Stops and starts are where beads go lumpy.
  5. Tool it immediately with a wet finger or a caulk tool. One firm pass. Do not go back over it repeatedly, which drags the surface and leaves ridges.
  6. Pull the tape while the caulk is still wet, at a shallow angle away from the bead.
  7. Leave it alone. Silicone needs a full cure before the tub gets used. Twenty-four hours minimum, longer in a damp room.

Silicone or latex

Property100 percent siliconeSiliconized latex
WaterproofFullyGood, not as good
FlexibilityExcellent, long termFair, hardens with age
PaintableNoYes
CleanupSolventWater
Removal laterHarderEasier

For a tub or a shower, silicone. The paintability of latex is irrelevant in a wet joint and the flexibility is what you are buying. For a painted trim joint elsewhere in the house, latex.

Why it goes black here

Mildew grows on soap residue and body oils sitting on the caulk surface in a warm damp room. The inhibitor in bath silicone slows it. What actually stops it is ventilation. If your bathroom has no fan, or the fan vents into the attic rather than outside, you will be recaulking on a schedule no matter what you buy. Wiping the joint dry after a shower helps more than any product.
