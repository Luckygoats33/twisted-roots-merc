A Caulk Gun That Will Not Stop Oozing

It is not the gun, usually. It is the pressure still in the tube and a rod that has nowhere to go.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

You finish a bead, set the gun down, and come back to a puddle of sealant on the floor. The tube is still under pressure from the plunger, and it keeps pushing until the pressure equalizes. Most guns have a way to stop this and most people do not know it is there.

Release the pressure, every time

On a ratcheting gun, there is a lever or plate at the back. Squeeze or lift it and pull the rod back a half inch. That relieves the pressure on the tube instantly and the ooze stops. Do it at the end of every bead, not just at the end of the job.

On a smooth rod, or dripless, gun the mechanism releases automatically when you let go of the trigger. Those are worth the extra few dollars precisely because nobody remembers to release a ratchet gun in the middle of a run.

The other habit worth building: after each bead, tip the nozzle up before you set the gun down, and set it on a scrap of cardboard rather than on the work.

Nozzle discipline

  1. Cut the nozzle small, at 45 degrees. Start smaller than the gap. You can always enlarge it, and you cannot go back.
  2. Break the inner seal. There is a foil or plastic membrane inside the tube behind the threads. Use the wire on the gun, or a long nail. If you have to squeeze hard to get flow, you have not broken it and the pressure is building up behind it, which is what causes a sudden gush.
  3. Wipe the nozzle after every stop.
  4. Between sessions, push a nail into the nozzle and leave it there as a plug, or cap it with tape. Cured sealant in the nozzle is easier to prevent than to remove.

Getting a bead that does not need cleaning up

Push the gun rather than pull it, with the nozzle angled into the joint. Pushing forces the material into the gap. Pulling lays it on the surface.

Move at a steady speed matched to your trigger pressure. The most common cause of a lumpy bead is stopping to reposition, so plan the run and do it in one pass where you can. On a long run, stop at a corner rather than in the middle of a straight line.

Tool it once. One firm pass with a wet finger, a caulk tool or the back of a plastic spoon. Repeated passes drag the surface and pull material out of the joint.

Common problems

ProblemCause
Keeps oozing after you stopRod pressure not released
Sudden gush when you startInner seal not fully broken
Bead comes out lumpyNozzle partly blocked, or tube nearly empty
Thin string instead of a beadNozzle cut too small
Way too much materialNozzle cut too far back
Rod slips, no pressureWorn ratchet plate, replace the gun
Very hard to squeezeCold sealant. Warm the tube indoors first

That last one comes up all winter here. Sealant thickens in the cold and a tube that has been in the truck overnight is genuinely hard work. Bring tubes inside the evening before, and remember most sealants also need a minimum application temperature to cure at all.

Buying a gun once

The cheapest guns have a thin pressed steel frame that flexes, a ratchet that slips, and a rod that bends. For occasional use they work. If you do more than a couple of tubes a year, a smooth-rod dripless gun with a decent thrust ratio is one of the better small purchases in a house. High thrust ratio matters when you are pushing thick exterior sealant on a cold day.

Keep it clean. Wipe the plunger and the barrel when you finish, and pull the rod fully back for storage. A gun that has been left with cured sealant down the barrel never works properly again.
