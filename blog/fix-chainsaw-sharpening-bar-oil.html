Chainsaw Chains, Files and Bar Oil

A dull chain makes sawdust instead of chips, works the engine, and is the reason most chainsaw injuries happen.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Everybody out here owns a chainsaw and a fair number of them are running dull. A sharp chain pulls itself into the wood and throws chips. A dull one has to be forced, it makes fine dust, it wanders in the cut, and it grabs. Sharpening is a ten minute skill and it changes how the saw behaves entirely.

What you need

  • A round file kit matched to your chain pitch, with a guide
  • A flat file and a depth gauge tool
  • Bar and chain oil, and a scraper for the bar groove
  • Gloves

Signs your chain is dull

  • Fine dust instead of chips
  • You have to lean on the saw
  • The saw pulls to one side in the cut
  • Smoke from the cut on softwood
  • The chain rattles or grabs instead of feeding smoothly

A chain also goes dull instantly the moment it touches dirt, a rock or a fence staple. One brush with the ground in a bucked log does more damage than an hour of clean cutting, which is why you keep the tip out of the soil and buck up on a support.

Filing a chain

Get the file size right. It is stamped on the chain or in the manual, and using the wrong diameter changes the cutting geometry.

  1. Set the brake, or clamp the bar in a vise. The chain should not move while you file.
  2. Start with the shortest or most damaged cutter, and mark it with a marker so you know when you have gone all the way around.
  3. Set the file in the cutter's gullet. The guide sits on the top plate and shows the correct angle, usually 30 degrees, marked on the guide.
  4. Push the file forward only, on the stroke away from you. Lift on the return. Filing on the back stroke dulls the file and rounds the edge.
  5. Same number of strokes on every cutter. Three or four is usually enough on a chain that is merely dull. Count them and stick to the count, because uneven cutters make the saw cut in an arc.
  6. Do every cutter facing one direction, then turn around and do the other side.

The depth gauges, which is the step people skip

In front of each cutter is a small hump called a raker or depth gauge, and it controls how big a bite the cutter takes. Each time you file the cutter back, the cutter gets slightly shorter, so the rakers become relatively taller and the chain takes a smaller bite. Sharpen a chain five or six times without touching the rakers and it will be perfectly sharp and cut like nothing.

Lay the depth gauge tool over the chain, and if a raker protrudes through the slot, take it down flush with a few strokes of the flat file. Then round the front edge back over so it does not catch. Do this every few sharpenings.

Bar oil, and why not to substitute

ProductVerdict
Bar and chain oilCorrect. Tacky additive keeps it on the bar at speed
Used motor oilNo. Thin, full of grit and metal, and it goes into the ground
New motor oilWorks badly. Flings off, no tack
Vegetable oilWorks, goes gummy in storage

The tackifier is the entire point. Bar oil is formulated to cling to a chain moving at speed instead of being thrown off in the first second. Run a saw dry and the bar rails and chain will blue from heat quickly and the chain stretches permanently.

You should use roughly a tank of bar oil per tank of fuel. If the oil tank is still full when the fuel runs out, the oiler is blocked or the bar groove is packed. Clean the groove with a thin scraper, clear the oil hole in the bar, and check the sprocket at the nose spins freely.

Bar maintenance and tension

Flip the bar over every few sharpenings so it wears evenly. Deburr the rails with a flat file if they have mushroomed. Check tension cold: the chain should pull away from the bar a small amount and snap back, and the drive links should not come out of the groove. A chain tensioned hot and left tight will bind when it cools.

When to stop and buy a new chain

When the cutters are filed back to the witness mark, when teeth are broken, when it has been sharpened at wildly uneven angles, or when it will not hold tension because it is stretched. Chains are consumables. Keep a spare sharp chain in the truck instead of trying to file one in the rain during an outage.
