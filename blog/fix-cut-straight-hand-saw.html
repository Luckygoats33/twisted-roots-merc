Cutting a Straight Line With a Hand Saw

No power, no extension cord, no noise. The technique is old and it takes about one board to learn.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A hand saw is the tool that still works when the power is out, when the cut is somewhere a cord will not reach, or when you need one board shortened and hauling a circular saw out is more trouble than the cut. People assume a hand saw cannot cut straight. It cuts exactly as straight as it is started and guided.

What you need

  • A sharp hand saw, 15 inch is a good general size
  • A square and a sharp pencil
  • Something to hold the work solidly
  • A knife, for a fine cut

Mark it properly

Use a square, not your eye, and carry the line around all the faces you can see. For a crosscut on a board, mark the top and both edges. Now you have a line to check against as you go, and you will see immediately if the cut is wandering out of plumb.

Pencil for rough work. For a cut that has to be clean, score the line with a utility knife first. The knife severs the surface fibers so the saw cannot tear them, and it also gives the saw teeth a groove to sit in when starting.

Support matters more than the saw

Work that flexes or vibrates will not cut cleanly and will bind the blade. Put the board on two sawhorses, or clamp it to a bench with the waste hanging free. Support the offcut, or the last inch of the cut will splinter as it drops. Kneel on it if that is what you have. A stable board is most of the job.

Starting the cut, which is where straightness is decided

  1. Position the saw on the waste side of the line. The kerf takes an eighth of an inch, and cutting on the wrong side of the line makes the piece short by exactly that.
  2. Use the thumb of your free hand as a guide, knuckle against the blade, well away from the teeth.
  3. Draw the saw backwards two or three times to score a starting groove. Backwards. It will not catch.
  4. Begin with short, light forward strokes at a low angle. No downward pressure at all yet.
  5. Once you have a groove an eighth of an inch deep, move your guiding hand away and lengthen the stroke.

The stroke

Use the whole blade. Short choppy strokes in the middle six inches wear that section out and are slower. Let the weight of the saw do the cutting on the push and lift slightly on the return.

Grip lightly, with your index finger pointed along the side of the handle. A light grip lets the saw track its own kerf. A death grip forces the blade off line.

Line up your forearm, elbow and shoulder in the plane of the cut, and keep them there. If your elbow drifts out, so does the blade. Most people who cannot cut straight are moving their body, not their saw.

Angle to the workUse for
About 45 degreesGeneral crosscutting, good balance of speed and control
About 60 degreesRipping along the grain, faster
Low, 20 to 30 degreesStarting a cut, and finishing cleanly in thin material

If it wanders

Do not try to steer it back by twisting the blade. That binds it and makes the wander worse. Instead, back the saw out, restart the cut in the correct place, and take light strokes until the new kerf is established. On a long cut, stop every few inches and sight down the blade against your line on the edge of the board.

A saw that consistently drifts one way regardless of technique has uneven set on its teeth, which is a sharpening problem. Most modern hardpoint saws are not sharpenable and are simply replaced when dull, and they are not expensive.

Crosscut and rip

Crosscut teeth are filed like small knives to sever fibers across the grain. Rip teeth are filed like small chisels to pare along the grain. A crosscut saw ripping a long board is slow and hot work. A general purpose hardpoint saw handles both adequately, and that is what most people should own.

Finish the last inch slowly and support the offcut with your free hand, or the piece breaks off and takes a splinter of your good face with it.

Hand saws, squares, clamps and utility knives are on the Fix It wall, and the lumber to practice on is out in the yard.
