Door Sweeps and the Gap at the Bottom

The biggest single air leak in most houses is the half inch of daylight under the back door. It is a twenty minute fix.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Air moves through a house because warm air rises and leaves at the top, pulling cold air in at the bottom. That makes the gap under an exterior door one of the most productive things you can seal. It is also the place that mice come in, and where wind-driven rain gets across the threshold in a storm.

What you need

  • A door sweep sized to your door width
  • A hacksaw or snips to cut it to length
  • Screwdriver, drill and small bit for pilot holes
  • Tape measure and a pencil

Measure the gap and the door

Close the door and look at the bottom. A quarter inch is normal. Half an inch or more, and you can see daylight, is a real leak. Measure the door width, not the frame, and measure the gap at both ends and the middle, because floors settle and the gap is often uneven.

Also look at the threshold. Many have an adjustable insert with screws along the top. Turning those screws raises or lowers the seal strip, and quite often the entire problem is that somebody has never touched them. Try that before you buy anything. Raise it until the door closes with light drag and no daylight.

For an exterior door on this coast, screw-on aluminum with a vinyl bulb, mounted on the inside face. A brush-type sweep sheds wind but not water, so if driving rain is your issue you want a bulb rather than bristles.

Fitting it

  1. Hold the sweep against the closed door and mark the length. Cut with a hacksaw. Aluminum cuts easily. File the burr off.
  2. Position it so the bulb or vinyl just touches the threshold with the door closed. Just touches. It should compress slightly, not drag hard.
  3. Most sweeps have slotted holes so you can adjust up and down. Mark the top of each slot, drill small pilot holes, and start the screws in the middle of the slots.
  4. Snug the screws, then close the door and check. Adjust down until light stops coming through, then stop.
  5. Tighten fully. Open and close it twenty times to be sure it is not going to catch on the threshold and tear off.

A sweep set too low is worse than one set slightly high. It drags, it wears through in a season, and it makes the door hard enough to close that somebody starts slamming it.

The coastal considerations

Water is the other half of this. A sweep on the inside face keeps air out. It does not keep water from running across a worn threshold. If you get water inside the door in a storm, look at the threshold itself: the cap may be worn flat, or the sill under it may be rotten, in which case you have a bigger job. Poke it with a screwdriver, because a rotting sill under an exterior door is common here and it does not improve on its own.

Aluminum sweeps corrode where salt air gets at them. Stainless screws rather than the plated ones that come in the packet will still be turnable when you want to adjust it in a few years.

Mice come in here

A quarter inch gap is enough for a mouse. If you are sealing the bottom of a door specifically for that, a bulb sweep plus a threshold that meets it properly closes the route, and there is more on the general strategy in the post on sealing gaps. Foam and brush sweeps do not stop them. They chew through both.
