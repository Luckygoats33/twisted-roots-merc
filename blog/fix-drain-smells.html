Fix It

When a Drain Smells, and What It Is Telling You

Sewer gas in the house means a trap is dry, a vent is blocked, or something is growing. Each has a different fix.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Every drain in a house has a U-shaped trap holding a few inches of water. That water is a plug, and it is the only thing between your living room and the sewer or septic system. When a room smells, the question is always the same: is a trap empty, is the venting failing, or is something living in the drain.

Work through it in this order

  • Run every fixture, including the ones nobody uses
  • Smell the drain directly, and then smell the room
  • Check the toilet and the water heater pan
  • Look for a floor drain you have forgotten about

The dry trap, which is most of them

A trap that does not get used evaporates. A guest bathroom, a basement floor drain, a laundry sink, an unused shower. Six weeks is enough on a warm dry line. The fix is thirty seconds: run water down it. A cup is enough to refill a sink trap, more for a floor drain.

For a fixture that genuinely never gets used, pour a little mineral oil or cooking oil in after refilling. It floats on the water and slows evaporation for months. Cheap trick, works well in a vacation place or a guest bath.

The rotten smell that is only that one sink

If the smell comes only from one sink, gets stronger when you run hot water, and smells more like a wet dog than a sewer, it is biofilm. A layer of soap, grease and bacteria coating the inside of the tailpiece and the trap. It is entirely harmless and entirely disgusting.

  1. Take the P-trap off, empty it, and scrub the inside with a bottle brush and hot soapy water.
  2. Get up into the tailpiece and down into the wall arm with the brush.
  3. On a kitchen sink, pull the strainer basket and clean under the flange. That gap collects a lot.
  4. Reassemble, then use an enzyme drain maintainer once a month to keep it from coming back. Enzymes eat the film, and they will not hurt a septic system.

Do not attack this with bleach. It kills the smell for a day and does nothing to the film, and on a septic system you are working against yourself.

When it is the vent

Plumbing vents run up through the roof and let air into the system so water can flow. When one is blocked, a draining fixture pulls a vacuum and siphons water out of nearby traps. The tell is a gurgle. If flushing the toilet makes the tub gurgle, or the sink burbles when the washer drains, air is being pulled through a trap and that trap will end up dry.

Around here vents block with two things: a bird or wasp nest at the top, and moss. A vent pipe on a north-facing coastal roof grows a genuinely impressive amount of moss and it can close the opening off. If you are safe on the roof, look. If you are not, hire it out, because a slick mossy roof over a fifteen dollar problem is a bad trade.

The one that is not plumbing

If the smell is around the base of the toilet rather than in a drain, and it is worse right after a flush, that is a failed wax ring letting gas and a little water out under the base. It does not go away and it is rotting your subfloor while you consider it. That is a wax ring job.

And if the whole house smells of sewer gas, particularly when the wind is up, stop investigating and get a plumber out. A cracked vent or drain line inside a wall is not a thing to live with while you experiment.

Traps, brushes, enzyme maintainer and wax rings are all on the Fix It wall.
