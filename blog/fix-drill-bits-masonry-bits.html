Drill Bits: Which One, and Why the Concrete One Will Not Work

Twist bits cut. Masonry bits crush. Using either in the other's job wastes the bit and gets you nowhere.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A drill bit set is one of the more confusing things on a hardware wall because the differences are metallurgical and the bits all look broadly similar. The practical version is short: what the bit is made of decides what it will cut, and how the tip is shaped decides how it does it.

The types you will actually use

Twist, high speed steel118 degree pointWood, plastic, thin metal

The most common mistake is grabbing a masonry bit for wood or metal because it looks tough. The carbide tip is wider than the shank and it is not sharp in the cutting sense. It will burn a ragged hole in wood and will not touch steel.

Masonry drilling is percussion, not cutting

A masonry bit works by pulverizing. The carbide tip chips the material and the flutes carry the dust out. That requires impact, which is why a hammer drill matters. On a standard drill in concrete you will spin for ten minutes and produce a shallow dent and a hot bit.

  1. Use hammer mode, or borrow a hammer drill. A cordless impact driver is not the same thing. Impact drivers hammer rotationally, hammer drills hammer along the axis.
  2. Moderate speed, firm steady pressure. Leaning harder does not go faster and it will overheat the carbide.
  3. Pull the bit out every half inch to clear dust. A flute packed with dust stops cutting.
  4. If you hit rebar, stop. A masonry bit will not go through steel. Move over or use a rebar-cutting bit.
  5. Blow the hole out before setting an anchor. Concrete screws will not hold in a hole full of dust, and that is the number one reason they strip.

Wear eye protection and a dust mask. Concrete dust contains silica and it is genuinely bad to breathe.

Speed, and why bits die

The general rule is inverse to the hole size: small bits fast, big bits slow. Hardwood and metal want slower than softwood. Almost every burned bit was run too fast with too little pressure, which polishes the edge instead of cutting.

In metal, use cutting oil, run slow, and use firm pressure so the bit takes a curl rather than skating. If you are producing dust rather than spirals, you are rubbing rather than cutting and the bit is dulling with every second.

Start with a center punch on metal and a brad point or a small pilot on wood, or the bit walks. A bit that skates across a smooth surface leaves a scratch you cannot remove.

Drilling tile without cracking it

Put a strip of masking tape over the spot so the tip does not wander. Use a spear point carbide bit or a diamond hole saw, no hammer mode, low speed, light pressure, and keep it wet with a sponge or a little dam of putty holding water. Hammer mode on glazed tile cracks it, every time. Once through the tile, change to a masonry bit for the substrate behind it.

What to buy

A good general twist set covering the common sizes, a separate small masonry set, and a spade bit or two. Beyond that, buy individual sizes as jobs demand them. The two sizes everybody loses are the ones for the anchors they use most, so buy spares of those specifically.

Sharpening twist bits by hand is a real skill and mostly not worth learning for the money involved. Masonry bits can be touched up on a grinder if you keep the angles, and a dull carbide tip with rounded corners is the sign.

Bit sets, masonry bits, concrete screws, safety glasses and dust masks are all on the Fix It wall. If you are drilling into wood near an edge, read the pilot hole post first.
