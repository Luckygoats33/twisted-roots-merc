Drywall Anchors, and Which Ones Actually Hold

Most anchors fail because somebody picked one rated in pounds without reading how those pounds are meant to hang.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The single best drywall anchor is a stud. Nothing sold in a bag holds like a screw driven into wood. Everything below is what to do when the thing you want to hang refuses to line up with framing, which is most of the time.

What you need

  • A stud finder, or a small nail and some patience
  • Anchors suited to the weight and to how it hangs
  • A drill and a bit that matches the anchor, not a bigger one
  • A screwdriver, and a level

Find the stud first, seriously

Studs are usually 16 inches apart, sometimes 24. Find one, measure across, and mark. If your fixture has two mounting holes, quite often one lands on a stud and only the other needs an anchor, and that halves the problem. Failing electronics, tap along the wall and listen, then confirm with a finish nail through where the fixture will cover.

In older houses out here you may be into lath and plaster instead. Anchors behave differently in plaster: it is harder, more brittle, and it crumbles. Drill, do not hammer, and toggle-type anchors are usually the right answer.

The types, and where each earns its keep

AnchorReal capacity in half inch drywallBest for
Plastic expansion, the ribbed cone5 to 10 lbAlmost nothing. Light picture hooks
Self-drilling threaded, plastic or metal20 to 35 lbTowel bars, small shelves, curtain rods
Molly bolt, sleeve type25 to 50 lbAnything that will be pulled on, coat hooks
Toggle bolt, spring wings50 to 100 lbHeavy shelving, cabinets, grab rails
Strap toggleSimilar to toggle, easier to fitSame jobs, and removable fixtures
Screw into a studFar more than the drywallEverything, if you can reach one

The plastic conical anchor that comes free in the box with a curtain rod is the worst product on this list. It relies on friction against the crumbly gypsum core and it pulls out. If a fixture came with them, throw them away and buy something else.

Shear load and pull-out are different

This is the part the packaging does not explain. A picture hanging flat against a wall puts a shear load on the anchor, straight down. A coat hook with a heavy wet jacket on it puts a pull-out load, levering the anchor straight out of the wall. The same anchor might handle 40 pounds of the first and 10 pounds of the second.

So look at the geometry. Anything that sticks out from the wall multiplies the force at the top fastener. Shelves, hooks, TV mounts, grab bars. For those, either hit a stud or use a toggle, and use more fasteners than you think you need.

Two things that must not go into drywall anchors

Grab bars in a bathroom. Those need to be into blocking or studs, because the entire point of a grab bar is that it holds when somebody falls onto it. An anchor that fails at that moment is worse than no bar at all.

And anything heavy over a bed, a crib or a desk. If it would hurt somebody when it comes down, it goes into wood.

Installing them so they work

  1. Drill the exact size the anchor calls for. Oversize hole, useless anchor. If you have no drill, self-drilling threaded anchors go in with a screwdriver.
  2. Set the anchor flush with the wall surface, not sunk below it and not proud.
  3. Drive the screw until it is snug, then stop. Over-tightening spins the anchor in the hole and destroys the gypsum around it.
  4. If it does spin, do not keep going. Pull it, patch the hole, and move over an inch.

Removing them later leaves a hole worth patching properly rather than smearing over, and that is covered in the small hole post.
