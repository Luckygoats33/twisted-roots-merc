Fix It

Expanding Foam, and the Places It Must Not Go

It is genuinely useful and it is genuinely destructive in the wrong gap. Knowing the difference takes about four minutes.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A can of expanding foam is the most satisfying product on the shelf. It goes into a gap as a bead and turns into something solid that seals air, water and mice. It is also a material that exerts real force as it cures, sticks permanently to skin, hair and paint, and cannot be reversed. It rewards planning more than almost anything else in the store.

What you need

  • The right can. Gaps and cracks, or windows and doors, and they are not the same
  • Disposable gloves, and eye protection
  • A drop cloth, because it will go where you did not want it
  • A utility knife for trimming after cure

The two kinds, and why it matters

Gaps and cracksWindow and door
ExpansionHigh, up to three timesLow, minimal
Cured textureRigidSofter, stays flexible
Use in a window jambNo. It will bow the frameYes, this is what it is for
Use in a large voidYesWasteful

The classic disaster is using the high expansion kind around a new window or door. The foam cures, pushes the jamb inward, and the sash no longer opens or the door binds forever. The low expansion version is formulated to be weaker than the frame. Read the can, every time.

Where it belongs

  • Around pipe and cable penetrations through the rim joist or the crawlspace wall
  • Sealing the gap where a dryer vent or a hose bib passes through siding
  • Filling the void behind trim on a drafty exterior wall
  • Sealing rodent entry points, in combination with steel wool or hardware cloth
  • Around a window or door jamb, low expansion only

Where it does not belong

Anywhere near heat. Not around a chimney or flue, not around a wood stove pipe, not touching recessed light cans that are not rated for it. Standard foam is combustible. There is a fire block foam, usually orange, for those locations, and that is the only thing that should go there.

Behind electrical boxes and around wiring in bulk. A small bead where a cable passes through a plate is fine. Encasing wiring in foam is not, because it changes how the conductor sheds heat.

Structurally. Foam is not a shim, not a bond, and not a load path. Filling a rotten section with foam so it feels solid is a way of hiding a problem that is still progressing.

Where water needs to drain. Do not foam the bottom of a window, the weep holes, or the gap under siding. Same rule as with exterior sealant, and for the same reason: on a wet coast, water gets in, and it has to get out.

Anywhere exposed to sun. Cured foam degrades quickly in sunlight, going yellow and crumbly. Trim it and cover it with caulk, trim or paint.

Using a can without regret

  1. Shake it hard for a full minute.
  2. Hold the can upside down while dispensing. That is how the propellant works, and holding it upright gives you spitting and a wasted can.
  3. Fill about a third to a half of the gap. It expands. Everyone overfills the first time.
  4. Work bottom to top in a vertical gap.
  5. A light mist of water on the surrounding surfaces helps it cure, since it cures with moisture from the air. Out here that is rarely in short supply.
  6. Leave it alone until fully cured, usually several hours to a day in damp cool weather.
  7. Trim flush with a serrated knife or a utility blade, then cover or paint.

Once you open a can

A straw-type can is generally single use. The foam sets in the straw and the nozzle. If you have a lot of sealing to do, a gun-type applicator with a screw-on can is a better buy, because the can can be left attached with the valve closed for weeks and you get far better control of the bead.

Get it on your hands and it stays there. It does not wash off, it wears off. Gloves are cheap and this is the single piece of advice people ignore and then regret for four days.
