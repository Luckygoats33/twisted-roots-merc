Extension Cords: Gauge, Length and Why the Cheap One Gets Hot

A cord too thin for the load drops voltage, cooks the tool and warms the cord. The chart is short and worth knowing.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

An extension cord is a length of wire, and wire has resistance. Push too much current through wire that is too thin over too long a distance and two things happen: the voltage at the far end sags, and the energy that went missing turns into heat in the cord. That is why a cheap cord running a saw feels warm, and why the saw sounds like it is struggling.

The two numbers on the tag

  • Gauge, written as AWG. Lower number means thicker wire
  • Length. Every extra foot adds resistance

The chart

Load25 ft50 ft100 ft
Up to 7 amps, lights, drill, small tools16 gauge16 gauge14 gauge
7 to 10 amps, circular saw, shop vac16 gauge14 gauge12 gauge
10 to 15 amps, table saw, heater, compressor14 gauge12 gauge12 gauge, and think about it
Generator to house loads12 gauge12 gauge10 gauge

The pattern is simple: when in doubt, go thicker. A 12 gauge cord costs more, weighs more and coils worse, and it will run anything you own without complaint. The only cord most people regret buying is a thin one.

Reading the jacket

Printed on the cord itself you will find letters. The ones that matter: S for flexible service cord, J for junior, meaning 300 volt rated rather than 600, T for thermoplastic, O for oil resistant, and W for outdoor rated. So SJTW is a flexible, 300 volt, thermoplastic, weather rated cord, which is the ordinary orange outdoor cord.

The W is the one to check. A cord without it is indoor only. Indoor cords have jackets that stiffen and crack in cold and are not built to sit in wet grass. On this coast, everything that goes outside should be W rated, full stop.

Rules that are not fussiness

  1. Uncoil it fully before you run a heavy load. A coiled cord is an inductor and a heat trap, and a coiled cord under load can melt its own jacket while sitting on the ground looking innocent.
  2. Do not daisy chain cords. Two fifty foot cords is a hundred foot run, with an extra connection sitting in the wet.
  3. Never run one under a rug or through a doorway that closes on it. Crushed insulation is a fire.
  4. One heavy tool per cord. A cord is not a power strip.
  5. Keep the connection out of puddles. Prop it on a block, or use a cord protector.
  6. Retire a cord with a nicked jacket, a bent prong, a missing ground pin, or a plug that is warm after use. There is no repair worth making on a damaged cord end that carries fifteen amps.

Generator cords

Powering things off a generator during an outage is where cord choice stops being academic. Long runs from a generator sitting safely outside to loads inside the house are exactly the situation where undersized cord causes trouble, and a refrigerator compressor running on sagging voltage can be damaged by it.

Use 12 gauge minimum, 10 gauge for long runs, keep the generator well away from the house and never inside a garage even with the door open, and use a proper generator cord for a large draw. If you want the generator to feed the house circuits rather than individual cords, that requires a transfer switch or interlock installed by an electrician. Backfeeding a dryer outlet is illegal, it energizes the line outside, and it can kill somebody working on the pole.

Storage, which decides how long they last

Coil over and under rather than around your elbow, hang them off a nail rather than kinking them into a bucket, and keep them out of the sun. A cord stored dry in a shed will outlive several stored in the bed of a truck.
