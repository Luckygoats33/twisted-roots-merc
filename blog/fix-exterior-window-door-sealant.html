Fix It

Sealing Around Windows and Doors on the Outside

Where the sealant goes matters as much as which one you buy, and there are places outside that must be left open.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The joint between a window or door frame and the siding is the most common water entry point on a house, and on this coast it gets tested by wind-driven rain from a direction that no window was ever designed for. Sealing it is a genuinely valuable afternoon. Sealing the wrong parts of it traps water inside the wall, which is worse than leaving it alone.

What you need

  • Exterior window and door sealant, an elastomeric or polyurethane type
  • Caulk gun
  • Utility knife and a scraper
  • Backer rod for gaps wider than a quarter inch
  • Rags and a dry day, which is the hard part

Where to seal, and where absolutely not

Seal the sides and the top of a window or door frame where it meets the siding or trim. Seal any gap where two pieces of trim meet. Seal around the top and sides of the nailing flange area if it is exposed.

Do not seal the bottom. Leave the underside of the window sill and the bottom of the frame open. That gap is a drain. Any water that does get into the wall assembly, and on this coast some always will, has to have a way out at the bottom. Caulk it and you have built a bathtub inside your wall.

Also leave weep holes clear. Vinyl and aluminum windows have small slots along the bottom of the exterior frame and they exist to drain the track. People caulk them shut every year because they look like a gap. They are not a gap.

Sides of the frame to sidingYes
Top of the frame, under a drip capYes, above the cap only
Bottom of the sillNo, leave it open

Vertical siding joints and butt jointsYes
Under the bottom course of sidingNo

Choosing the tube

Exterior work needs a sealant that stays flexible, sticks to dissimilar materials and survives sun. Polyurethane and high-grade elastomeric sealants do all three and carry long warranties. Ordinary painter's acrylic latex caulk is fine for filling a trim joint that will be painted immediately and is protected from weather, and it is a poor choice for a gap that moves and gets rained on.

Silicone sticks to glass and metal beautifully and cannot be painted, so it is right for glass-to-frame and wrong for anything you want to disappear under paint.

Getting it to stick

  1. Remove the old failed sealant. Every bit. New over old is the same mistake as in a bathroom.
  2. Scrape and brush the joint clean. Loose paint, dust, moss and cobwebs all prevent adhesion, and out here there is moss in places you would not expect.
  3. Dry. The surface has to be dry and the forecast has to give you enough hours without rain for a skin to form. Check the tube, but plan on several hours minimum.
  4. Temperature matters. Most sealants want above 40 degrees. A cold damp morning is not the time.
  5. Push backer rod into any gap wider than a quarter inch. Sealant should bond to two sides of a joint, not three. Backer rod gives it a defined depth and lets it stretch properly. Filling a deep gap solid with sealant makes a bead that tears.
  6. Bead in, tool it once with a wet finger or a spoon, and leave it.

The bigger picture

Caulk is the last line of defense, not the first. The first line is flashing: metal or membrane that directs water out and over the layer below it. If a window leaks and you are on your third round of caulk, the flashing above it is missing or was installed wrong, and no amount of sealant will fix a reverse lap. That is when it stops being a Saturday job.

Signs it is past caulk: staining on the interior below the window, soft trim, paint bubbling on the wall under the sill, or a leak that appears only in wind-driven rain from one particular direction. All of those want somebody to open it up and look.

Exterior sealant, backer rod, caulk guns and scrapers are on the Fix It wall. If the draft you are chasing is around the door rather than the frame, that is weatherstripping.
