Fix It

Frozen Pipes on the Coast, and What to Do When One Bursts

We do not get deep cold out here, which is exactly why coastal houses are built with pipes in places that freeze.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The Oregon Coast rarely gets brutally cold, and that is the problem. Because hard freezes are uncommon, houses out here were built with water lines in crawlspaces, in exterior walls and along uninsulated garage ceilings, in places that would never be allowed somewhere with real winters. Then a cold snap comes through with an east wind behind it and half the town discovers where their pipes run.

Before it gets cold

  • Foam pipe insulation on anything in the crawlspace or the garage
  • Hose bib covers, and hoses disconnected
  • Crawlspace vents closed for the cold spell
  • Know where the main shutoff is

Preventing it, which is the entire strategy

Pipes burst because water expands as it freezes, and the ice plug is usually not where the pipe splits. The plug forms, pressure builds between the plug and a closed faucet, and the pipe fails at the weak point. Which is why leaving a faucet dripping works. It is not the moving water keeping things warm. It is that an open faucet gives the pressure somewhere to go.

During a cold snap: open the cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls so house heat gets to the pipes, run a slow drip on the fixture furthest from where the water comes in, and keep the heat on even in rooms nobody is using. If you are leaving for a week in cold weather, shut the water off at the main and drain the system. That is the only version that is actually safe.

If it is frozen but not yet burst

  1. Open the affected faucet. Both handles. You want an escape route for water as it melts.
  2. Find the frozen section. It will be the cold-to-the-touch stretch, usually where the pipe passes through an uninsulated space.
  3. Warm it slowly. A hair dryer, a heat lamp at a distance, or towels soaked in hot water. Start at the faucet end and work back toward the frozen middle, so meltwater has somewhere to run.
  4. Never use a torch or an open flame. People burn houses down doing this every single winter, and a propane torch on a frozen pipe can also flash the trapped water to steam and rupture the pipe in your hand.
  5. Keep going until full pressure returns at the faucet. Partial flow means part of the plug is still there.

If it has already burst

  1. Shut the water off at the main. Do it first, before you look at anything.
  2. Open the lowest faucet in the house to drain the pipes down.
  3. Kill power to anything the water is getting near. If water is anywhere near the panel, near outlets or near a furnace, shut the relevant breakers off, and if you cannot do that safely, stay out and call.
  4. Get the water off the floor and get air moving. Standing water in a crawlspace on this coast becomes a mold problem within days.
  5. Now look at the pipe. A split in an accessible section of copper or PEX is a genuine push-fit coupling repair. Cut past the damage on both sides, because the split usually runs further than it looks.

Afterwards

Once it is fixed, insulate the pipe that froze. It will freeze again in the same place the next time the temperature drops, because nothing about that location changed. Foam sleeve is inexpensive and it goes on in minutes with a utility knife. Heat tape is an option for the worst spot, but it wants a proper outdoor-rated outlet and it should never be wrapped over itself.

Pipe insulation, push-fit couplings, hose bib covers and heat tape come out onto the Fix It wall before the first cold snap, and there is more in storm supplies. If you are not certain where to shut the water off, that is the first thing to fix.
