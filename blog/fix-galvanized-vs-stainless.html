Galvanized or Stainless: Why It Matters More Here

Salt air, treated lumber and constant damp will eat the wrong fastener in a couple of seasons and stain your siding on the way.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Inland, an ordinary zinc plated screw outdoors is a compromise that mostly works. Within a few miles of salt water it is a mistake you will see within two years: rust streaks down the siding, screw heads that crumble when you put a driver in them, and connections that have quietly lost strength.

Eric picks the fastener stock here on this basis, which is why there are fewer choices on the wall than at a big store and why the cheap ones are missing.

The four grades you will meet

TypeCoating or materialWhere it belongs
Zinc plated, brightThin electroplated zincIndoors, dry only
Hot dip galvanizedThick zinc, dipped moltenOutdoors, treated lumber, general exterior
Coated exterior screwProprietary polymer or ceramicDecks and fences, check the rating
Stainless 304Solid stainlessCoastal exterior, cedar, anywhere visible
Stainless 316Marine grade, adds molybdenumDirect salt spray, docks, boats

Hot dip galvanized is thick and rough looking, and that roughness is the point. Electroplated zinc is smooth, shiny and thin, and it is essentially a decorative finish outdoors. If it looks like a bright silver screw with a slick finish, it is indoor hardware.

The treated lumber problem

Modern pressure treated lumber is preserved with copper compounds. Copper in contact with zinc, in the presence of moisture, sets up a galvanic cell and the zinc corrodes preferentially. That is basic chemistry and it is the reason a plated screw in a treated post can be gone in a couple of years while the wood is fine.

So for treated lumber the choice is hot dip galvanized at minimum, or stainless. Manufacturers state which their product is rated for, and lumber suppliers put it on the tag. It is worth reading.

The tannin problem

Cedar and redwood contain tannins that react with iron and steel, leaving black stains around every fastener. Even galvanized will do it once the coating wears. If you are building anything from cedar that you want to look good, stainless is not optional, and this is the one place where paying three times as much for a box of screws is obviously the right call.

Mixing metals

Do not mix. A stainless screw through an aluminum flashing, or a galvanized bracket held with stainless bolts, creates a galvanic pair where the less noble metal corrodes. In practice: use stainless fasteners with stainless hardware, galvanized with galvanized, and keep aluminum away from both unless there is an isolating washer or coating between them.

This comes up constantly with gutters, roof flashing and post bases. If you are fastening a galvanized post base, buy the fasteners the manufacturer specifies. They have thought about it.

JobFastener

Handling the difference

Stainless screws are softer than hardened steel screws. They snap more easily and they strip more easily, so drill pilot holes in hardwood and in anything near an edge, use a star drive head where you can, and slow the drill down. A snapped stainless screw buried in a deck board is an irritating afternoon.

Galvanized coating is thick enough to affect fit. A hot dip galvanized bolt may not run smoothly through a nut that is not also galvanized, and the coating gets damaged when you drive it hard. A damaged coating rusts at that spot. Cold galvanizing compound in a spray can is a real product and touching up cut ends of galvanized material is worth doing.

Stainless and hot dip galvanized fasteners, gate hardware and post brackets are on the Fix It wall, with treated lumber and posts out in the yard.
