Fix It

What a GFCI Actually Does, and Why Yours Keeps Tripping

It is not a breaker and it is not there to protect the wiring. It is there to protect you, and it trips for good reasons.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A GFCI is the outlet with the two little buttons. Most people know it as the thing that makes the bathroom outlet stop working. It is worth knowing what it is measuring, because once you understand that, its behavior stops being random and starts being a diagnosis.

The thing it is measuring

Electricity that goes out on the hot wire is supposed to come back on the neutral. A GFCI compares the two continuously. If more current is leaving than returning, even by a few thousandths of an amp, the missing current is going somewhere else. It is going to ground. And the most common way that happens is through a person standing on a wet floor.

So it trips in a fraction of a second at a leak far below what it takes to hurt somebody. It is not protecting the wire from overload. That is the breaker in the panel, and a breaker will happily let fifteen amps run through your chest all day without noticing. The GFCI is protecting you specifically, which is why the code puts them wherever there is water: bathrooms, kitchen counters, laundry, garages, crawlspaces, and outdoors.

Why it keeps tripping

PatternMost likely cause
Trips only when one appliance runsThat appliance has a fault. Stop using it
Trips in wet weather, outdoorsMoisture in an outdoor box or a cord end
Trips randomly, several outlets deadDownstream wiring fault, or a shared neutral
Trips immediately on reset, nothing plugged inWiring fault or a failed GFCI
Trips with a motor load, freezer or pumpMotor leakage, common as they age
Will not reset, no power at allNo power reaching LINE, check the breaker

The important thing here is that a nuisance trip and a real fault look identical from the outside. Treat every trip as real until you have found the cause. Never solve a repeatedly tripping GFCI by replacing it with a regular outlet. That is not fixing anything, it is removing the smoke alarm.

Working out which one it is

  1. Unplug everything on that circuit. Everything, including chargers and the coffee maker.
  2. Press reset. If it holds, plug things back in one at a time, using each one for a bit before adding the next.
  3. If it trips with one particular appliance, that appliance has a ground fault. Old freezers, well pumps, space heaters and anything that has been damp are the usual suspects.
  4. If it trips with nothing plugged in, the fault is in the wiring or the device itself.
  5. Remember that one GFCI often protects a string of ordinary outlets downstream. The dead outlet in the garage may be protected by a GFCI in the bathroom, or in the panel. Go press every reset button you can find.

Coastal reality

Salt air and constant damp are hard on outdoor electrical. An outdoor box with a cracked gasket or a cover that no longer closes will trip its GFCI every time it rains sideways, which out here is often. Same with an extension cord whose end has been sitting in a puddle. Before you assume the device has failed, look at every outdoor connection on the circuit and dry it out.

In-use covers, the bubble kind that close over a plugged-in cord, are the right fix for anything that stays plugged in outside. A flat flip-lid cover only protects an unused outlet.

Testing and replacing

Press TEST monthly. The button should trip it. If pressing TEST does nothing, the device has failed and it must be replaced, even if the outlet still supplies power. A GFCI that no longer trips is worse than no GFCI, because everyone assumes it is working.

They do not last forever. Fifteen years is a fair working life, less in a damp bathroom or an outdoor box. Replacing one is the same job as any outlet with one extra rule: LINE is the incoming power, LOAD feeds everything downstream. Reversed, it will not reset. The wiring detail is in the outlet post.

When to hand it over

Call an electrician if a GFCI trips with nothing plugged in and new hardware does not fix it, if it trips at the same time as a breaker, if you find scorching anywhere on the circuit, or if a whole string of outlets is dead and you cannot find a reset. Intermittent ground faults inside a wall are exactly the kind of thing that is worth paying somebody to trace, and it is usually a short visit.
