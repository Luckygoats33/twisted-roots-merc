Fix It

Hose Bibs, and the Two Dollar Habit That Saves the Wall

A garden hose left connected through a freeze is how a perfectly good outdoor faucet ends up leaking inside the house.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The outdoor faucet is the most exposed water connection on a house and the one people think about least. It sticks through the wall into weather, it is usually the oldest fixture on the building, and the single most common cause of a burst pipe on this coast is a hose left screwed onto it in October and forgotten.

Before cold weather

  • Disconnect every hose. All of them. Drain and coil
  • Insulated hose bib cover on each spigot
  • Find the interior shutoff for the bib, if there is one
  • Foam sleeve on the pipe where it runs through the crawlspace

Why a connected hose is the problem

Most outdoor faucets on newer houses are frost-free. The valve seat is not at the handle, it is twelve inches back inside the heated wall, and the long stem lets the water in the exposed section drain out every time you shut it off. That is a good design and it works perfectly, right up until somebody leaves a hose attached. The hose holds a column of water in the barrel that cannot drain, the water freezes, and the ice splits the pipe. Because the split is inside the wall, you will not know until spring when you turn the water on and it comes out of the ceiling below.

So the rule is simple and it has no exceptions. Hoses come off before the first freeze. Even on a warm day in the middle of winter, put it back off when you are done.

Frost-free or ordinary, and how to tell

PropertyFrost-freeOrdinary
Barrel length8 to 14 in from the wall to the handleShort, 3 to 4 in
Behavior after shutoffDribbles for a few seconds then stopsStops immediately
HandleRound, often with a set screwRound or lever
Freeze risk with hose offLow, if installed with a slight downward pitchHigh

If you have the short kind, and plenty of older places here do, you want an interior shutoff. Find the valve on the pipe feeding it, close it for winter, and open the outdoor faucet to drain the stub. If there is no interior valve, adding one is a reasonable plumber call and it retires the whole problem.

Replacing a bib that is already damaged

  1. Shut the water off, at the interior valve if there is one, otherwise at the main.
  2. Open the bib and let it drain. Open a lower faucet in the house too.
  3. Most bibs thread into a fitting. Hold the pipe steady inside with a second wrench while you back the bib out, or you will twist the pipe in the wall and turn one repair into two.
  4. Clean the male threads on the new bib, wrap two to three turns of thread seal tape clockwise.
  5. Thread it in by hand first, then wrench it snug with the outlet pointing down. Pointing down matters, because that is how a frost-free bib drains.
  6. Turn the water on slowly and check inside the crawlspace as well as outside. A bad seal shows up on the inside.

Where it stops being a homeowner job

If the bib is soldered rather than threaded, if the pipe behind it is galvanized and flaking, or if you cannot see or reach the connection inside, stop. Twisting on old pipe inside a wall is how a fifteen dollar part becomes an opened wall. A push-fit shutoff and a short repair are doable in an accessible crawlspace, but anything sealed up is a plumber.

The rest of the year

Leaks at the handle are usually just the packing nut. Snug it a quarter turn with the water on and see if the weep stops. A drip from the spout with the handle off is the washer or the seat, and on a frost-free bib that washer is on the far end of a long stem you unscrew and pull out whole. Take it in and match it.

Hose bib covers, thread seal tape, pipe insulation and replacement hoses are all on the Fix It wall. If a pipe has already let go, go to the frozen and burst pipe post.
