A Leaking Garden Hose Is Almost Never the Hose

Three failure points, two of them fixed with a washer. Here is how to find which one is soaking your boot.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A hose that sprays at the faucet, sprays at the nozzle, or leaks somewhere along its length is three different problems. Two of them cost under a dollar to fix. Most hoses get thrown away long before they need to be, usually because the brass end got crushed when somebody drove over it.

What you need

  • Hose washers, the rubber ones. Buy a bag, they vanish
  • A hose repair kit for the end you are replacing
  • A sharp utility knife
  • Channel-lock pliers or a small adjustable wrench

Leaking where it meets the faucet

That is the washer, nine times out of ten. Unscrew the hose, look inside the female coupling. There should be a flat rubber ring sitting in there. If it is missing, flattened hard, cracked, or has swelled out of shape, replace it. Pull the old one out with a small screwdriver or a bent paperclip and press the new one flat into the seat.

If a new washer still leaks, look at the coupling itself. If the hex end is oval instead of round, somebody stepped on it or drove over it and it no longer pulls up square. You can sometimes true it up carefully with pliers. If the threads are chewed, cut the end off and put a repair fitting on.

Leaking at the nozzle

Same washer, on the other end. Nozzles also have their own internal seals that go, and a metal nozzle with a worn valve is worth replacing rather than repairing. If the leak is only when the nozzle is closed and the hose is pressurized, note that a shut-off nozzle leaves the whole hose under pressure all day, which is what makes hoses bulge and split. Turn the faucet off at the wall when you are done, not just the nozzle.

A split in the middle of the hose

  1. Find the split. Pressurize it and walk the length, or look for the section that bulges.
  2. Cut out the damaged section cleanly with a utility knife. Cut square. Go an inch past the damage on both sides.
  3. Pick your fitting. A mender joins two cut ends back together. A male or female repair end replaces a crushed coupling.
  4. Warm the hose end in hot water for a minute if it is stiff. Cold rubber does not want to go over a barb.
  5. Push the barbed insert all the way in, then tighten the clamp or the compression collar evenly.
  6. Test at full pressure before you coil it up.

Why hoses die out here

Not from cold, mostly. From sun on the exposed half of a coil, and from being left pressurized. A hose lying in the yard all summer goes brittle on the top surface only, which is why the splits are always on one side. Coiling it out of direct sun, or hanging it, roughly doubles its life.

The other coastal killer is leaving a hose full of water through a freeze. Water expands, the hose bulges permanently, and the following summer it splits at the bulge. Drain hoses before winter. Walk the length holding it up, or just drape it over a fence rail for an afternoon.

The hose that is worth keeping

Rubber hoses outlast vinyl ones by years and they stay flexible in the cold. They are heavier and they cost more and they are the last hose you will buy for a while. If you are the kind of person who runs a hose to a stock tank or a garden all summer, buy the heavy one once.

Hoses, repair kits, washers and metal nozzles are all on the Fix It wall. If the leak is at the wall rather than the hose, that is the hose bib post.
