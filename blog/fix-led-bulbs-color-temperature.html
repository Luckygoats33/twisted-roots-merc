LED Bulbs: Lumens, Kelvin, and Why Your New Bulbs Look Wrong

Watts stopped meaning anything. Here is what the numbers on the box actually tell you, and which ones matter.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

People come in unhappy about new bulbs more than about almost anything else on the shelf. The light is too blue, or too dim, or it flickers on the dimmer, or it died in a year when the box promised twenty. All of that comes down to three numbers, and once you can read them the problem goes away.

Brightness is lumens, not watts

Watts measure power consumed. With incandescent bulbs that correlated closely enough with brightness that everyone used it as shorthand. LEDs broke the shorthand, because a 9 watt LED and a 60 watt incandescent put out about the same light.

40 W4505 to 6
60 W8008 to 9

Buy by lumens. The equivalent wattage printed in big letters on the front is marketing, and manufacturers are generous with it.

Color temperature is the one people notice

Kelvin describes whether the light is warm and yellow or cold and blue. Low numbers are warm.

KelvinLooks likeGood for
2200 to 2400 KCandle, amberAccent, porch, mood
2700 KOld incandescentLiving rooms, bedrooms, most of a house
3000 KSlightly crisper warm whiteKitchens, bathrooms
4000 KNeutral, faintly clinicalShops, garages, task lighting
5000 K and upBlue daylightWorkbench, outbuildings

If you replaced bulbs and the room suddenly feels like a waiting room, you bought 4000 K or higher. Go back to 2700 K for living space. On this coast, where it is gray and half dark for a good part of the year, plenty of people genuinely prefer 3000 K or 4000 K indoors because it feels brighter. That is a legitimate preference, not a mistake. What you should not do is mix temperatures within one room. Two bulbs at different Kelvin in the same fixture look broken.

There is a fourth number worth knowing: CRI, color rendering index. Higher is better and 90-plus is worth paying for in a kitchen or anywhere you look at food, paint or fabric. Cheap bulbs at CRI 80 make everything look slightly gray.

Dimmers, and why the flicker

Most LED bulbs are not dimmable, and putting a non-dimmable bulb on a dimmer gives you buzzing, flicker, and a short life. Check the box for the word dimmable.

Even dimmable LEDs can misbehave on an old dimmer, because dimmers designed for incandescent loads expect a much larger minimum load than a few LED bulbs present. The symptoms are flicker at low settings, a glow when off, or a range where nothing happens. The fix is an LED-rated dimmer, which is a straightforward swap covered in the switch post.

Bases and shapes

The common household screw base is E26, called medium. Candelabra is E12, the little one. Then there are shapes: A19 is the standard pear, BR30 and PAR38 are the wide flood cans, GU10 and MR16 are the small pin-base spots. Take the old bulb with you, or photograph the top of it next to a coin for scale.

Why LEDs die early

An LED bulb is a small circuit board plus diodes, and the part that fails is almost always the driver electronics, killed by heat. So a bulb rated for twenty thousand hours in open air will not get there jammed into a sealed recessed can or a tight enclosed fixture. If it is going into an enclosed fixture, buy one that says enclosed rated on the box. That single line on the packaging is the difference between years and months.

The other early killer is damp. A porch or outbuilding fixture that lets moisture in kills LEDs quickly, and out here that is most of them. Wet-rated fixtures and a bulb suited to the location save you a ladder trip every few months.
