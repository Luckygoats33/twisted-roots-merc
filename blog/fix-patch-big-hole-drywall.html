Fix It

Patching a Hole You Can Put Your Fist Through

Once the hole is bigger than a fist, filler alone will not hold. You need something behind it, and there are two good ways.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Somewhere around three or four inches across, a drywall hole stops being a filling job and becomes a patching job. There is no longer enough surrounding material to bridge, so you have to put backing behind the hole and a piece of drywall in it. Two methods work well. Which one you use depends on how tidy you want to be.

What you need

  • A scrap of drywall the same thickness, usually half inch
  • Joint compound, and mesh or paper tape
  • A drywall saw or a sharp utility knife
  • A wide taping knife, six inch minimum, plus a smaller one
  • Drywall screws, and a piece of scrap wood for backing
  • Primer and paint

Before you cut, look inside

Put a flashlight in the hole. You are checking for wires, pipes and ducts before you start sawing. Holes near a switch, an outlet or a bathroom wall very often have something running through them. If there is a cable in there, cut by hand with a utility knife and stay well away from it.

Method one: the California patch

Best for holes up to about eight inches, and it needs no backing wood.

  1. Cut the hole into a clean square or rectangle.
  2. Cut a piece of new drywall two inches larger than the hole in both directions.
  3. On the back of that piece, score the paper one inch in from each edge, all the way around, and snap the gypsum off. Peel the front paper away from the removed strips so you are left with a plug the size of the hole, with a one inch paper flange all around the front face.
  4. Butter the paper flange with joint compound, press the plug into the hole and the flange flat onto the wall.
  5. Smooth it down, let it dry, then two more thin coats feathered wide.

The paper flange is doing exactly what drywall tape does, so there is no separate taping step. It is a clean method and it is fast.

Method two: backing and a plug

Better for larger holes, and for anywhere the wall gets bumped.

  1. Cut the hole square. If a stud is nearby, extend the cut to the center of the stud so you have something solid to screw to.
  2. Cut two pieces of scrap wood a few inches longer than the hole. Slide each one in behind the drywall, hold it, and run screws through the existing wall into the wood at top and bottom.
  3. Cut a drywall plug to fit the hole with a small gap, and screw it to the backing.
  4. Tape all four seams. Mesh tape then a setting compound, or paper tape bedded in all-purpose compound.
  5. Three coats, each wider than the last, feathered out eight to twelve inches.

The coats are the whole job

CoatKnifeAim
First, bedding4 to 6 inFill the seam, bed the tape, squeeze out excess
Second8 inCover the tape, widen the ramp
Third10 to 12 inVery thin, feather the edges into nothing

The mistake everybody makes is trying to make coat one look finished. It will not. Its job is to bed the tape. All the appearance happens on coat three, and it is a skim so thin you can see the coat underneath through it. A patch shows because of a visible ridge, not because of a lump, so spend your effort feathering the outer edge rather than filling the middle.

Texture and paint

If your walls have texture, a smooth patch will read as a patch no matter how flat it is. Spray texture in a can matches most orange peel or knockdown, and it is worth practicing on cardboard first because everyone lays it on too heavy. For knockdown, spray it, wait until it loses its gloss, and then flatten it lightly with a wide knife.

Prime the whole patch and the feathered area before painting. In a coastal house give each coat of compound real drying time, overnight if the weather is wet, and run a fan. Trying to finish a three-coat patch in one day here is how you get a patch that cracks along the tape a month later.

Joint compound, tape, knives, drywall screws and primer are all on the Fix It wall, and there is scrap material and backing wood out in the yard.
