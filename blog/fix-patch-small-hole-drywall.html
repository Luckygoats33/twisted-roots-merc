Fix It

Patching a Small Hole in Drywall So It Disappears

Nail holes, anchor holes and doorknob dents. The materials cost seven dollars and the technique is all in the last coat.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A small hole is anything you can cover with a playing card. Nail holes, screw holes, the crater left by an anchor you pulled out, the round dent from a doorknob. All of these are the same repair, and the reason patches show is almost never the filler. It is that people build the patch up proud of the wall and then sand it, which cuts through the surrounding paper and makes a bigger problem.

What you need

  • Spackle for the small stuff, joint compound for anything wider than a coin
  • A flexible putty knife, wider than the hole. A three inch is a good all-rounder
  • Fine sandpaper, 150 to 220 grit
  • A damp sponge
  • Primer, then your wall paint

Prep is two minutes and it decides everything

  1. Run the edge of the putty knife over the hole to knock down any raised paper or crumbs. If a nail hole has pushed the paper outward into a little volcano, press it back flat or cut the burr off with a utility knife.
  2. For an anchor hole, do not just fill over a hole with a ragged crushed rim. Push the loose material inward slightly with the corner of the knife so the surface is at or below flush.
  3. Wipe the dust off. Spackle does not stick well to dust.

Filling

Load a small amount of spackle onto the knife and press it into the hole with a firm pass, holding the blade at a shallow angle. Then come back across at ninety degrees to that first pass with the blade almost flat, scraping the excess off. You want the filler in the hole and essentially nothing on the wall.

Lightweight spackle barely shrinks and one coat usually does. Regular joint compound shrinks as it dries, so expect a small dimple and plan on a second thin coat. Two thin coats always beat one thick one. A thick coat cracks as it dries and takes a day to cure in coastal humidity.

Let it dry properly. In a damp house that can be several hours even for a small patch, and the surface will look done long before the middle is. Poke it. If it is cool to the touch it is still wet.

Sanding, gently

Use 150 or 220 grit and a light touch, and stop as soon as it is flush. The paper facing on drywall is what holds paint. If you sand into it you get a fuzzy area that will show through paint as a rough patch forever, and the fix for that is priming and skimming a wider area.

The better method in a finished room is a damp sponge rather than sandpaper. Wipe the patch with a barely damp sponge in a circular motion and the excess compound rolls off without dust and without touching the paper. It takes an extra minute and it does not put gypsum dust over everything in the room.

Priming, which is the step people skip

Fresh spackle and joint compound are porous. Paint over them without primer and the patch soaks up more paint than the wall around it, so it dries with a different sheen. You will see it from across the room in raking light, particularly on anything above flat paint. Prime the patch, let it dry, then paint.

The coastal footnote

Compound dries by evaporation, and in a house that runs at high humidity nine months of the year it dries slowly. Rushing it is the number one cause of a patch that cracks or sinks a week later. Put a fan in the room, crack a window on a dry hour, and be prepared to do the second coat the next day rather than after lunch.

If the hole you are patching came from an anchor that pulled out, read the anchor post before you rehang anything, because the same anchor in the same wall will fail the same way.
