Fix It

Pilot Holes, and Why Your Board Keeps Splitting

A screw is a wedge. Near an end grain edge, in dry fir, it will split the board unless you take the wedge out of the equation.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Driving a screw into wood does two things at once. The threads pull, which is what you want, and the solid shank displaces material, which is what splits the board. Close to an end or an edge there is not enough wood to absorb that displacement, so it opens along the grain. A pilot hole removes the material the shank would have displaced, and the threads still have everything they need to grip.

What you need

  • A drill bit set, and a countersink if you want the head flush
  • The screws you are actually using, to size against
  • Masking tape, for a depth mark

Two holes, not one

A proper pilot for a tapered wood screw is really two diameters. The pilot hole in the receiving piece matches the root diameter of the screw, the solid core without the threads. The clearance hole through the piece being fastened matches the outer thread diameter, so the screw passes through freely and pulls the two pieces together.

Skipping the clearance hole is the most common error. If the threads bite in both pieces, the screw cannot draw them together and you get a gap that no amount of tightening closes. That is why a joint sometimes stays stubbornly open no matter how hard you drive.

Sizing without a chart

Hold the drill bit up in front of the screw against a light. You should see the thread crests sticking out on both sides and the solid core hidden behind the bit. That is your pilot bit. For the clearance hole, the bit should be just wide enough that the screw drops through under its own weight.

Softwood, which is most of what we sell, tolerates a smaller pilot. Hardwood and anything very dry needs closer to the full root diameter, or the screw will snap before it seats.

When you can skip it

Modern deck screws and structural screws have self-drilling tips, auger points or reverse threads under the head, and in the middle of a wide board in soft fir they genuinely do not need a pilot. Where you still need one:

  • Within about two inches of the end of a board
  • Within an inch of an edge
  • Anything hardwood, or old dry material
  • Cedar, which splits readily
  • Any stainless screw, which will snap where a hardened one would drive
  • Trim and anything you want to look good

Other ways to stop a split

Stagger fasteners so two screws are not on the same grain line. Two screws in a row along one grain line make a perforation, and the board splits along it.

Blunt a nail before driving it near an end. Tap the point flat with a hammer. A blunt point crushes its way through rather than wedging the fibers apart, and it splits far less. That is an old trick and it works.

Clamp the piece, or hold it against something solid. Wood that can flex splits more readily than wood that is supported.

Depth, and the tape trick

Wrap masking tape around the bit at the depth you want and stop when the tape reaches the surface. It costs nothing, and it keeps you from drilling out the back of a cabinet door or into the wire behind a stud.

Countersinking matters for appearance and for strength. A screw head that has been forced below the surface without a countersink has crushed the fibers around it and it will not hold as well. A countersink bit, or a larger bit run in a quarter inch, gives the head somewhere to sit.

The coastal note

Wood out here moves with the seasons more than most places, and a screw driven tight into a board that later swells has to go somewhere. Splits that appear a year later on a deck usually started as a screw too close to an end. Keep fasteners a couple of inches back from board ends, use two rather than three across a narrow board, and pre-drill anything at an edge.
