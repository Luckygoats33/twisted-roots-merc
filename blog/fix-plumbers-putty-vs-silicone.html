Plumber's Putty or Silicone: Getting It Right the First Time

They look interchangeable on the shelf. Use the wrong one and the joint either leaks or never comes apart again.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Two products, both used to seal plumbing fixtures, both about eight dollars. They work in completely different ways. Putty seals by staying soft and filling a gap under compression, and it never cures. Silicone seals by curing into rubber that bonds to both surfaces. Choosing wrong does not usually cause a dramatic failure. It causes a slow weep, or a fixture that has to be destroyed to remove.

Quick answer

  • Under the flange of a sink drain, a strainer basket, or a faucet base on a flat surface: putty
  • Anywhere the joint is under water pressure, or on plastic, or where the seal is also the bond: silicone
  • Around a tub, a shower base, or a countertop backsplash: silicone
  • Threads: neither. That is thread seal tape or pipe dope

What putty is for

Plumber's putty is a soft clay. You roll it into a rope, press it under the lip of a fixture, tighten the fixture down, and the excess squeezes out. You wipe the excess off and the remainder stays soft under there for years, filling the irregular gap between a chrome flange and a sink surface that is not quite flat.

Its advantage is that it never hardens, so the fixture comes back apart cleanly when you need it to. Its limit is that it holds back standing water, not pressure. It is a gasket, not a glue.

The two places it should not go: on plastic and on stone. Standard putty contains oils that will stain marble and granite and can craze certain plastics over time. There are stain-free and plastic-safe versions and they are worth buying if either applies.

What silicone is for

Silicone cures into rubber and sticks. It handles movement, it handles constant damp, and it is what you want on a tub surround where the joint flexes when the tub fills. Kitchen and bath silicone with a mildew inhibitor is the version worth having in a coastal bathroom, because in a room that never really dries out, ordinary caulk goes black in a season.

Its drawback is exactly its strength. Silicone bonds. A pop-up drain assembly bedded in silicone comes out with a putty knife, some swearing, and occasionally a broken flange. On a fixture you might reasonably want to service, that matters.

The one that is neither

Threaded connections do not get putty and do not get silicone. Tapered pipe threads seal on the threads themselves and need thread seal tape or pipe joint compound to fill the spiral gap and let the joint tighten without galling. Two to three wraps of tape, wound clockwise so tightening does not unwrap it. On plastic threads, tape rather than paste, and hand tight plus a small nudge, because it is easy to split a plastic fitting.

Compression fittings and the plastic slip joints on a P-trap get nothing at all. They seal on a machined cone or a tapered washer. Adding sealant there is a sign that something is not seated right, and the sealant will not save it.

What goes where

JobUse
Sink strainer basket flangePlumber's putty
Pop-up drain flange, bathroom sinkPutty, or silicone if the manufacturer says so
Faucet base on a flat deckPutty, unless a gasket came with it
Faucet base on stone or plasticStain-free putty or clear silicone
Tub to tile jointKitchen and bath silicone
Toilet base to floorSilicone, and leave the back open
Threaded pipeThread seal tape or pipe dope
Compression or slip jointNothing

Putty, silicone, thread seal tape and a decent caulk gun are all on the Fix It wall. If you are sealing around a tub, the technique matters more than the tube, and that is covered in the bathtub caulking post.
