Fix It

Plunging, Done Right

Most people use the wrong plunger, on a dry seal, with an aggressive first push. All three of those work against you.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Plunging works on hydraulics, not muscle. You are trying to move a column of water back and forth against a soft blockage until it breaks up. Air does not do that. Neither does force. Almost every failed plunging attempt fails for one of three reasons, and all three are easy to correct.

What you need

  • The right plunger for the fixture
  • Enough standing water to cover the cup
  • A rag, for sink and tub overflows
  • Gloves and a bucket, because this is going to splash

Two different plungers, and they are not interchangeable

PlungerShapeUse on
Flange or toilet plungerCup with a soft sleeve that folds outToilets
Cup or sink plungerPlain rubber dome, flat rimSinks, tubs, showers

A toilet drain is a curved horn, not a flat surface. A flat cup plunger cannot seal on it, so all your effort goes into pushing air sideways. The flange folds out of the cup and seats down into the horn. Conversely, using a flange plunger on a flat sink bottom is awkward. If you own one plunger, own a flange one and tuck the flange up inside the cup when you are on a sink.

The sequence

  1. Make sure there is water in the fixture, enough to fully cover the plunger cup. Plunging a dry bowl accomplishes nothing. If the toilet is empty, add water from a bucket.
  2. Block every other opening on the same fixture. On a sink, hold a wet rag hard in the overflow hole. On a double sink, hold a stopper or a rag in the other basin. If air can escape somewhere, your push escapes with it.
  3. Seat the plunger and press down slowly the first time. This is the step people skip. The first push should be gentle, to expel the air trapped in the cup without blowing your clog further down the pipe.
  4. Now go. Fifteen to twenty firm strokes, keeping the seal the whole time. The pull matters as much as the push. You are working the blockage loose from both sides.
  5. Break the seal and see if it drains. Repeat two or three cycles before you decide it is not working.

When to stop plunging

If three good cycles do nothing, stop. More plunging on a hard blockage compacts it. Move to a snake, which grabs and pulls rather than pushes. Also stop immediately if you have already poured chemical drain opener in, because plunging that means splashing caustic liquid at your own face.

Stop and think if a toilet backs up and the tub gurgles, or if plunging one fixture makes another one burble. That is a main line or a vent problem, and pushing on it just moves sewage somewhere you do not want it. On a septic system, several fixtures slow at once is a signal to get the tank looked at before you spend another weekend on the house plumbing.

Two things that help

Hot water, not boiling, poured into a slow kitchen sink before you plunge will soften a grease plug considerably. Boiling water is a bad idea because it can soften PVC joints and crack porcelain.

A dab of petroleum jelly around the plunger rim helps it seal on an awkward tub drain or an old cast iron bowl. It sounds fussy. It works.

Plungers, drain snakes and gloves are all on the Fix It wall, and if plunging has not solved it in ten minutes, read the snake post before you buy anything stronger.
