Fix It

PVC and Irrigation Repair That Holds

Primer, then cement, quarter turn, hold. Most failed PVC joints are somebody skipping one of those four things.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

PVC is genuinely easy to work with and genuinely unforgiving of shortcuts. The joint is not a glue joint, it is a chemical weld: the primer softens both surfaces, the cement melts them together, and they fuse into one piece of plastic. Skip the primer and you have a joint that holds pressure for a season and then weeps underground where you cannot see it.

What you need

  • PVC fittings in the right size and schedule
  • PVC primer and cement, the purple primer and the blue or clear cement
  • A PVC cutter or a fine-tooth saw
  • A rag and a marker
  • Sandpaper or a deburring tool

Getting the fitting right

MarkingWhat it isUse
Schedule 40, whiteStandard pressure PVCIrrigation, sprinklers, cold water supply
Schedule 80, grayThicker wallHigher pressure, rare in a yard
ABS, blackDrain waste ventDrains only, different cement
Poly, flexible blackDrip and low pressureBarbed fittings and clamps, no cement

PVC and ABS are not the same material and the cements are not interchangeable. If you are joining white to black there is a transition cement, but the honest answer in most yard situations is to not mix them. Pipe is sized by its nominal inside diameter, so a half inch pipe measures about seven eighths on the outside. Take a scrap of the old pipe to the store rather than guessing.

Making the joint

  1. Cut square. A ratcheting PVC cutter is the clean way. A saw works if you keep it square, but you must deburr afterwards.
  2. Deburr both inside and outside the cut end. A ridge of plastic will scrape the cement off as the pipe goes in.
  3. Dry fit everything first. Mark the alignment with a marker across the pipe and fitting, because you get about eight seconds to get the orientation right once the cement is on.
  4. Wipe both surfaces clean and dry.
  5. Primer on the outside of the pipe and the inside of the socket. It should look wet and the surface should dull slightly.
  6. Cement on both surfaces while the primer is still wet. A full even coat, not a thick blob.
  7. Push together with a quarter turn twist, line up your marks, and hold for thirty seconds. It will try to push back out. If you let go early the joint backs off and you have a leak.
  8. Wipe the bead of excess off. Let it cure before you pressurize. Give it an hour minimum, longer if it is cold or damp, and it is usually both here.

Repairing a break in an existing line

You cannot spin a coupling onto a fixed line, which is the whole difficulty. Three options: cut out a generous section and put in two couplings and a new length, using a slip coupling or a repair coupling that has no internal stop so you can slide it over; use a compression repair coupling, which needs no cement at all and is a good answer in a wet trench; or cut back to the nearest fitting and rebuild.

Excavate more than you think you need. Working in a hole with six inches of clearance is how you end up with a joint you could not hold together properly.

Wet conditions, which is most of the year

Cement will not weld a wet surface. In a trench that is filling with groundwater, bail it, get a rag in there and dry the pipe, and work fast. If you cannot get it dry, use a compression repair fitting instead of fighting it. There is also a wet-condition cement made for exactly this, and it is worth having on the shelf out here.

Cold slows the cure dramatically. A joint that would set in fifteen minutes in summer wants an hour or more on a damp day, and pressurizing early is the most common reason a fresh repair fails.

Winterizing, so you do not do this again

Irrigation left full through a freeze splits at the fittings. Drain the system in autumn, open the low point drains, and leave a valve cracked so it can breathe. That is a half hour that saves a trench in spring.

PVC fittings, cement and primer are on the Fix It wall, and the shovels and gravel for the trench are out in the yard.
