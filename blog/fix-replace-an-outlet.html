Fix It

Replacing an Outlet

Same job as a switch with one extra decision: which wire is hot, and whether that location legally needs a GFCI.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

An outlet where the plug falls out under its own weight is worn out and it is a fire risk, because a loose blade makes a poor connection and a poor connection makes heat. Replacing one is straightforward. The two things that trip people up are getting hot and neutral on the correct screws, and not realizing that certain locations require a GFCI whether the old one had one or not.

What you need

  • 15A duplex outlet, or a 20A GFCI if the location calls for it
  • Non-contact voltage tester
  • Screwdriver set and a wall plate
  • Wire connectors, and electrical tape

Off, and proven off

  1. Breaker off. Then test the outlet with something plugged in, and test with the non-contact tester.
  2. Plate off, screws out, pull the receptacle forward.
  3. Test every conductor in the box. Two circuits in one box is common, especially in kitchens.
  4. Photograph the wiring before anything comes off.

Which wire goes where

WireScrewSide
Black, hotBrass, gold coloredNarrow slot side
White, neutralSilverWide slot side
Bare or green, groundGreenBottom or top, by the U slot
Red, in a switched or split outletBrass, after breaking the tabOnly if it was that way before

Brass to black is the mnemonic that sticks. Getting hot and neutral reversed does not stop the outlet working, which is exactly why it is dangerous. A lamp wired backwards has its shell live even when switched off. A three dollar plug-in outlet tester will tell you in one second whether you got it right, and one belongs in every house.

Screw terminals, always

Same as with switches: use the screws, not the push-in back holes. Hook clockwise, tighten firmly. If the box has multiple cables and the old outlet was used to pass power through by its own terminals, consider pigtailing instead: join the incoming and outgoing wires with a wire connector plus a short tail to the outlet. Then a failed device does not kill the rest of the circuit.

Wrap a couple of turns of electrical tape around the body of the outlet to cover the side terminals before you push it back into a metal box. Not required, but it costs nothing and it stops a screw head touching the box.

Where a plain outlet is not allowed

GFCI protection is required in the places where water and electricity meet. Broadly: bathrooms, kitchen countertops, laundry areas, garages, unfinished basements, crawlspaces, outdoors, and near a sink generally. If you are replacing an outlet in one of those places and it is not GFCI protected, upgrading it is the right call and it is not much more work.

A GFCI has LINE and LOAD terminals and the difference matters. LINE is the incoming power. LOAD feeds the outlets downstream and gives them protection too. Wire it backwards and it will not reset. The terminals are labeled and there is usually tape over the LOAD screws from the factory, which is a hint that most installations do not use them.

Details on how they work and why they trip are in the GFCI post.

Stop signs

Put it down and call an electrician if you see scorching, melted plastic, or a discolored screw terminal. That outlet has been running hot and the cause may be further up the circuit. Same if the wiring is cloth-covered and brittle, if it is aluminum, if there is no ground and you were planning to install a three-prong outlet anyway, or if the box is metal and floating loose in the wall.

An ungrounded two-wire circuit is the common one out here in older houses. You cannot simply put a three-prong outlet on it and call it grounded. The lawful options are a GFCI with the correct label, or running a proper ground, and which one is right for your situation is worth asking somebody qualified.
