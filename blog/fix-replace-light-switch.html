Replacing a Light Switch

Fifteen minutes, one screwdriver, and one non-negotiable step involving a tester before you touch a single wire.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A switch that has to be jiggled, that buzzes, or that has stopped working is a five dollar part. Replacing one is a genuinely reasonable homeowner job. It is also electrical work, which means the difference between doing it right and nearly right is not cosmetic. If any part of what follows makes you uneasy, that is useful information and there is no shame in making a phone call.

What you need

  • Single pole switch, or three-way if that light is controlled from two places
  • Non-contact voltage tester. Not optional
  • Screwdriver set
  • Wire connectors and a wall plate
  • Electrical tape

Turning it off properly

  1. Find the breaker and switch it off. Not the switch on the wall, the breaker in the panel.
  2. Test at the fixture or the switch with the plate still on. A non-contact tester will chirp near a live wire through the plastic.
  3. Take the plate off and pull the switch out of the box, carefully, without touching the terminals.
  4. Test every wire in the box, not just the ones on the switch. Boxes very often carry more than one circuit, and the one you turned off may not be the one that will bite you.
  5. Only when everything reads dead do you start unscrewing wires.

Testers can fail. If a tester reads dead on a circuit you know is live elsewhere, do not trust it. Prove it on a known live outlet first, every time.

Getting the new one wired the same way

Photograph the old switch before you disconnect anything. Then note which wire goes where.

Switch typeTerminalsKey detail
Single pole2 brass, 1 greenEither brass screw, orientation does not matter for the two hot wires
Three-way2 brass, 1 dark common, 1 greenThe common wire must go on the dark screw
DimmerUsually pigtail leadsCheck it is rated for LED if the bulbs are LED

On a three-way, mark the common wire with tape before you remove it. Get that one wrong and the light works from one switch and not the other, and figuring out why afterwards takes far longer than the tape would have.

Making a connection that lasts

Use the screw terminals, not the little push-in holes on the back. Push-in connections are legal and they are also the single most common cause of a loose connection that heats up years later. Strip about three quarters of an inch, bend a hook, put it clockwise around the screw so tightening pulls the loop closed, and snug it down firmly.

The bare or green ground goes to the green screw. If the box is metal, it also gets bonded to the box. If there is no ground wire at all in an old box, that is worth knowing about, and it is a conversation with an electrician rather than something to ignore.

Fold the wires back into the box in a gentle accordion, not a jam. Screws in, plate on, breaker on, test.

What tells you to stop

Back away and call an electrician if you find any of the following: scorch marks or melted insulation in the box, cloth-insulated wiring that crumbles when you move it, aluminum wire, more wires in the box than you can account for, a box that is loose in the wall, or knob and tube. Also stop if the box is already so full that the new switch will not fit back in without forcing it. Overstuffed boxes are a real hazard and the fix is a bigger box, not more shoving.

There is a fuller list of the stop signs in the post on when to call an electrician, and it is worth reading before you open anything.
