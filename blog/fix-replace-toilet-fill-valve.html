Replacing a Toilet Fill Valve

Twenty minutes, one part, no special tools, and it fixes the toilet that hisses forever or takes four minutes to refill.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The fill valve is the tall part on the left side of the tank. It lets water in after a flush and shuts off when the float tells it to. When it fails it does one of three things: runs forever, refills painfully slowly, or refills with a shriek that you can hear from the other end of the house. All three are the same fix and the same part.

What you need

  • Universal fill valve
  • A small bucket and a rag
  • Channel-lock pliers or an adjustable wrench
  • Ten minutes of patience with the shutoff valve

Doing it

  1. Shut the water off at the angle stop behind the toilet. Flush and hold the lever to drain the tank.
  2. Sponge the last of the water out. There is always more than you expect and it will end up in your shoe.
  3. Put the bucket under the tank and disconnect the supply line from the bottom of the fill valve shank. Hand tight is usually all it was.
  4. From under the tank, unscrew the plastic locknut holding the valve in. It is meant to be hand tight. If somebody put a wrench to it, go gently, because you are twisting on porcelain.
  5. Lift the old valve out. Take the refill tube with it, the little flexible hose that clips to the overflow.
  6. Set the new valve height before you install it. Most twist to extend. The rule is the top of the valve should sit at least an inch above the top of the overflow tube.
  7. Drop it in, put the rubber washer on the outside where the instructions say, and hand tighten the locknut. Snug plus about a quarter turn. No wrench.
  8. Reconnect the supply line. Hand tight, then a quarter turn with the wrench. These are compression or rubber-sealed connections and overtightening is what makes them leak.
  9. Clip the refill tube to the overflow tube using the little angled clip that came in the bag. Do not shove the tube down inside the overflow.
  10. Turn the water on a quarter turn, watch the connections, then open it fully.

The refill tube matters more than it looks

That skinny tube sends a small stream down the overflow to refill the bowl trap while the tank fills. If you push it down into the overflow tube so the end sits below the tank water line, you have built a siphon. The tank will slowly drain into the bowl forever and you will swear the new valve is defective. Clip it so the end sits above the water, aimed down into the overflow, and leave it.

Setting the level and checking your work

Fill the tank, then look. The water should stop about an inch below the top of the overflow tube. Most valves adjust with a screw on top or a clip on the float rod. Lower is safer than higher. If the level is too low the flush gets weak and you will be flushing twice, which defeats the purpose.

When to stop and call somebody

If the angle stop behind the toilet weeps at the packing nut, will not shut off, or snaps, you now have a job that needs the water off at the main and a real repair to the stub-out. On copper with a soldered stop, that is a plumber unless you are comfortable with a push-fit shutoff valve. On old galvanized, call. Galvanized pipe that has been in a coastal wall for decades will crumble at the threads and what started as a toilet repair becomes a wall repair.

Fill valves, supply lines and push-fit shutoffs are all on the Fix It wall. If you have not yet worked out whether the valve or the flapper is your problem, start with the diagnosis post.
