Replacing a Toilet Flapper Without Making It Worse

The cheapest plumbing repair in the house, and the one people most often install slightly wrong so it leaks again in a month.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A flapper is a rubber disc on a hinge. It costs about seven dollars, it takes ten minutes, and there are no tools required unless the old one has fused itself to the flush valve, which happens on well water. This is the single most satisfying repair in a house because the noise that has been bothering you for three weeks just stops.

What you need

  • Universal toilet flapper
  • A rag and a bucket for the half inch of water left in the tank
  • Channel-lock pliers, only if the supply shutoff is stiff

The actual sequence

  1. Turn the water off at the little valve behind the toilet. Turn it gently. If it will not budge, do not lean on it, because a snapped angle stop turns a ten minute job into an afternoon with the main off.
  2. Flush and hold the handle down so the tank empties as far as it will go.
  3. Sponge or rag out what is left in the bottom. You want the flush valve seat dry enough to feel with a fingertip.
  4. Unhook the chain from the flush lever arm. Note which hole it was in.
  5. Pull the old flapper off its ears, or unhook the ring if yours slides over the overflow tube.
  6. Run a finger all the way around the flush valve seat, the plastic ring the flapper sits on. Feel for grit, mineral scale or a nick. Wipe it clean. A new flapper on a rough seat leaks exactly like the old one did.
  7. Fit the new flapper. Ear style clips onto the two pegs. Ring style drops over the overflow tube and slides down.
  8. Hook the chain up with about a half inch of slack. This is the step everybody gets wrong.
  9. Turn the water back on slowly and watch for a minute.

The chain is the whole job

Too much slack and the flapper does not lift far enough, so you get a weak flush and end up holding the handle. Too little slack and the chain holds the flapper a hair off its seat, which is a slow leak that you will not hear but the dye test will find. Half an inch of droop with the flapper closed is the target. If there is a long tail of extra chain, cut it or clip it back, because a loose tail will eventually find its way under the flapper and prop it open. That failure drives people genuinely crazy since it is intermittent.

Sizing, and why universal usually is

Tank typeFlapper sizeNotes
Most residential toilets2 inchWhat a universal flapper fits
Newer high efficiency, taller tank3 inchWider seat, obvious once you look
Old low profile tanks2 inch, often a ballA flapper conversion works fine

Measure the opening the flapper covers, not the flapper. Two inch and three inch are far enough apart that you cannot mistake one for the other once you have seen both. If you are unsure, bring the old one in. Somebody behind the counter has held a lot of flappers.

Things that go wrong

Old flappers can go gummy and leave black rubber residue on your fingers and on the seat. Wipe every trace of it off. That residue is what is left of the rubber breaking down, usually from a chlorine tablet sitting in the tank, and it will chew a new flapper just as fast. If you use in-tank cleaning tablets, this is your sign to stop. They eat flappers, fill valve seals and the tank-to-bowl gaskets.

If the new flapper still leaks after you have cleaned the seat and set the chain, the seat itself is worn. On some toilets you can drop in a replacement seat that glues over the original. On a lot of them it is cheaper and less aggravating to change the whole flush valve, which means pulling the tank off the bowl. That is a bigger job than most people want on a weeknight, but it is not beyond a patient person with two wrenches.

Flappers, fill valves and tank parts are all on the Fix It wall. If the noise you are chasing is a constant trickle rather than an intermittent refill, read how to tell a bad flapper from a bad fill valve before spending money.
