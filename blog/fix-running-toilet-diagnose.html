Fix It

Why Your Toilet Runs, and Which Part Is Actually Bad

A running toilet is almost always one of two cheap parts. Twenty minutes and some food coloring tells you which one to buy.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A toilet that runs costs more than people think. A flapper that seals for eleven hours and then quietly stops sealing will move a few hundred gallons a day. If you are on a well, that is your pump cycling all night to keep up with a two dollar piece of rubber. The repair is cheap either way. The part of it worth getting right is knowing which piece is bad before you drive over here.

What you need

  • A pencil
  • Food coloring, or a dye tablet
  • Twenty minutes when nobody is going to flush

Two parts, two different noises

Nearly every running toilet is one of two failures. The flapper, that rubber disc sitting in the hole at the bottom of the tank, has stopped sealing. Water dribbles out of the tank into the bowl, the tank level drops, and the fill valve wakes up every so often to top it off. That is the toilet that is silent for twenty minutes, hisses for thirty seconds, then goes quiet again. Always loudest at two in the morning.

The other failure is the fill valve itself not shutting off. Water keeps arriving, the level climbs past where it should stop, and the surplus pours down the overflow tube standing up in the middle of the tank. That toilet runs continuously, with a steady sound like a garden hose left a quarter open. It never goes quiet, so you stop hearing it, which is how people end up surprised by a water bill.

The dye test settles it

  1. Take the tank lid off and set it somewhere it cannot get knocked onto the floor. They are porcelain and they do not survive tile.
  2. Drop enough food coloring in the tank to make the water obviously colored. Ten drops is plenty.
  3. Do not flush. Go do something else for twenty minutes.
  4. Come back and look in the bowl. Color in the bowl means the flapper is leaking. Clear water in the bowl means the flapper is fine and your problem is upstream.

That single test rules out half the possibilities and takes no tools. It is the first thing Eric asks people at the counter, and about seven times out of ten it ends with somebody walking out with a universal flapper and nothing else.

Reading the water level

Now look at the water line in the tank. There should be an inch of air between the surface and the top of that overflow tube. Most tanks also have a molded fill line on the porcelain or on the valve body itself. If the water is right at the top of the overflow tube, or trickling into it, the fill valve is either misadjusted or worn out.

Adjustment is free. Modern valves have a screw or a clip on the float that lowers the shutoff point. Turn it down, flush, and watch where it settles. If lowering the float does not stop the flow, or if the valve hisses and shudders when it refills, the valve is done. They wear out from the inside and no amount of fiddling brings them back.

When it is not the tank

If the tank is behaving and there is water on the floor, stop chasing the flapper. Water at the base is a wax ring, a loose closet bolt, or a cracked tank, and those are different jobs with different parts. We wrote them up separately in the post on toilets leaking at the base.

Both replacement parts live on the Fix It wall, and both jobs are genuinely a twenty minute repair with no tools more exotic than a pair of channel-lock pliers. Bring the old flapper in if you want somebody to eyeball the size.
