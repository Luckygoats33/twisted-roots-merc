Fix It

A Slow Sink Drain, Fixed Without Pouring Chemicals Down It

The clog is almost always in the trap or the tailpiece, about eight inches from where you are standing.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A sink that drains slowly is not a mystery. The water is getting past something, so the pipe is not blocked, it is coated. Ninety percent of the time the coating is in the first eighteen inches: the tailpiece coming down from the drain, the P-trap under the cabinet, or the horizontal arm going into the wall. All three of those come apart by hand.

What you need

  • A bucket and an old towel
  • Channel-lock pliers, sometimes
  • A stiff brush or a bit of wire
  • Plumber's putty, if the drain body has to come out

Skip the chemical drain opener

Caustic drain openers work by generating heat and dissolving organic material. Sometimes that clears the clog. Often it just sits on top of the clog, and then you have a trap full of lye that you or a plumber has to open up anyway. On old thin-wall chrome traps or on ABS glued joints, repeated use of the hot stuff does real damage. On a septic system, which most places out this way are, dumping caustics down the drain is working against the bacteria doing your treatment.

The exception nobody argues with is enzyme drain maintainers used monthly on a slow kitchen line. Those are not clog openers, they are slow prevention, and they do not hurt a septic tank.

Pull the trap. It is easier than it looks

  1. Everything out from under the sink. Everything. You will knock over whatever you leave.
  2. Bucket under the trap. Towel under the bucket.
  3. Loosen the two slip nuts on the P-trap by hand. Most are plastic and hand tight. If somebody used a wrench, use the pliers gently and turn the nut, not the pipe.
  4. Drop the trap into the bucket and pour it out. Look at what comes out. Hair and soap is a bathroom sink. Gray grease is a kitchen sink. Grit and sand means something else is going on.
  5. Reach up into the tailpiece and down into the wall arm with a brush or bent wire. The build-up is usually a ring you can feel.
  6. Reassemble. New washers if the old ones are hard. Hand tight only, then a nudge.
  7. Run hot water for a full minute and watch every joint.

If the trap was clean

Then the blockage is further out in the branch line and you need a hand snake, not a plunger. Feed it in through the wall arm rather than down through the sink, because you are already there and the trap is off. Twenty-five feet of snake reaches a lot further than most people expect. There is a whole piece on using a drain snake if you have not done it before.

If two fixtures are slow at once, or a sink backs up when the washer drains, the problem is the main line and not this sink. That is a different scale of job.

The coastal wrinkle

What comes out of the trapWhat it means
Hair and gray soap pasteNormal bathroom sink, clean and move on
Solid grease plugKitchen. Stop putting grease down it
Sand and fine gritWell water or someone rinsing off river gear
Black slime with a smellBiofilm. Clean and see the drain smell post
Rusty flakesGalvanized drain line, on its way out

Houses out here often still have galvanized drain lines behind the wall. They corrode from the inside and the opening narrows year over year, so the sink gets slower gradually instead of clogging suddenly. If you have pulled a clean trap and the sink is still slow, and the pipe going into the wall is steel rather than plastic, you are not looking at a clog. You are looking at a pipe that has silted itself closed, and that is a replacement, not a cleaning.

Putting it back so it does not weep

Slip joints seal with a tapered plastic washer, not with force. The washer goes in with the tapered edge pointing toward the joint. Hand tight is right. If a joint drips after you have snugged it, take it apart and look at the washer instead of tightening further, because cranking on plastic threads splits the nut and then you are driving somewhere for a trap kit.

Traps, washers, snakes, plungers and plumber's putty are all on the Fix It wall.
