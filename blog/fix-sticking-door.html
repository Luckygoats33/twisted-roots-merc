Fix It

A Door That Sticks, Drags or Will Not Latch

Before you plane anything, tighten the top hinge screws. That fixes more doors around here than any other single step.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Doors in coastal houses move. Wood swells in a wet winter, the building settles on damp ground, and hinge screws work loose in framing that has been slightly humid for thirty years. The result is a door that rubs at the top corner, drags on the floor, or swings shut on its own. Almost none of that needs a plane, and reaching for one first is how a door ends up with a permanent gap.

What you need

  • A screwdriver
  • 3 inch screws, long enough to reach the framing
  • Wooden shims or thin cardboard
  • A pencil, and a block plane only as a last resort

Find where it is binding

  1. Close the door slowly and watch. Listen for where it touches.
  2. Run a strip of paper around the gap. Where it will not slide through, that is the contact point.
  3. Look at the gap all the way round. A door should have a consistent gap, about the thickness of a nickel, on the top and both sides.
  4. A gap wide at the top of the latch side and tight at the bottom means the door has dropped. That is hinges, not swelling.
  5. A door that binds all the way down one side in winter and is fine in summer is swelling. That is a finish problem.

The top hinge, which is usually the answer

The top hinge carries most of the door's weight in shear, and it is the one that pulls loose. The short screws that came with the hinge only bite into the jamb, which is thin trim material nailed to the framing. Over years the door sags and the top latch corner starts to rub.

The fix takes ten minutes. Open the door, support it, and remove one screw at a time from the jamb side of the top hinge. Replace each with a 3 inch screw that goes through the jamb and into the framing behind it. Drive it snug, not so hard that it pulls the jamb out of line. Do all of them on that hinge. Very often the door lifts back into place as you tighten.

If the screw holes are stripped and the screw spins, glue wooden matchsticks or a piece of dowel into the hole, let it dry, and drive into that.

Swelling, and why it is a coating problem

Wood swells because it is taking on moisture, and it takes on moisture through unsealed surfaces. On most doors that means the top edge and the bottom edge, which almost nobody paints. Take the door off, check both edges, and if they are bare, prime and paint them. That single step stops a lot of seasonal sticking permanently.

If the door is genuinely too tight and must be trimmed, mark the contact area with pencil, take the door off, and remove material with a block plane or a belt sander in small passes, checking constantly. Plane the hinge edge rather than the latch edge if you can, because the latch edge is bevelled slightly and a shaved latch edge starts to look wrong. Seal and paint any bare wood you expose, immediately, or you have just made a new water entry point.

Other fixes, from least invasive up

SymptomTry first
Rubs at top latch cornerLong screws in the top hinge
Rubs at bottom latch cornerShim behind the bottom hinge, or tighten the top
Latch does not engageAdjust the strike plate
Swings shut on its ownHinge pin tap, or the jamb is out of plumb
Drags on the floorCheck for a raised threshold or new flooring
Binds only in winterSeal the top and bottom edges

Shimming a hinge moves the door in the opposite direction: a thin cardboard shim behind the whole hinge leaf pushes that edge of the door away from the jamb, and a shim behind only the outer half angles it. It sounds crude. It is standard practice and it works.

When the door is not the problem

If the gap at the top of the door is wedge-shaped and the trim above it has separated, the frame is out of square because the house has moved. That is common in older houses out here on saturated ground, particularly on the low side of a building. You can adjust the door to fit the new shape, and that is a reasonable answer. But if it keeps needing adjustment every year, and doors elsewhere in the house are doing it too, get somebody to look at the foundation and the crawlspace rather than at the doors.

Long screws, shims, hinges and planes are on the Fix It wall. If it closes fine but will not catch, go to the strike plate post.
