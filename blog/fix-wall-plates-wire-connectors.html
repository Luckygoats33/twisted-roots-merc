Wall Plates and Wire Connectors: The Small Parts That Cause Big Problems

A cracked cover plate is not cosmetic and a wire nut spun on badly is the most common cause of an outlet that dies for no reason.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

These two items sit in a bin near the register and cost almost nothing, and between them they account for a surprising share of household electrical trouble. A cover plate is the barrier between a person and a live terminal. A wire connector is the joint that either holds for forty years or slowly cooks itself inside a wall.

What you need

  • Wall plate to match the device, standard or oversize
  • Wire connectors in an assortment of sizes
  • A screwdriver, and a small level if you are fussy

Wall plates are not decoration

Replace a cracked one. The crack means somebody leaned on it or the box is out of square, and a broken plate leaves the sides of a device exposed. On a switch that is unpleasant. On an outlet in a garage or a shop, where a metal object could slide down beside it, it is worse.

Sizing catches people out. Standard plates cover the device and about a quarter inch of wall. If the drywall around the box is chewed up, or the box sits crooked, an oversize or jumbo plate covers the mess without any patching. That is a legitimate and very cheap fix for a hole somebody cut too big.

DevicePlate
Duplex outletDuplex, two oval openings
GFCI or a rocker switchDecorator, one rectangular opening
Toggle switchToggle, one narrow slot
Blanked-off boxBlank plate. Required, not optional
OutdoorsWeatherproof, in-use bubble if a cord stays plugged in

An open box with no plate at all, in a garage or a crawlspace, is a live box somebody will eventually put a hand into. A blank plate costs about a dollar. There is no reason for that to exist in a house.

Wire connectors, and the way they actually work

A twist-on wire connector is a tapered spring inside a plastic shell. As you turn it, the spring bites into the conductors and draws them together, and the plastic insulates the result. Done correctly it is a gas-tight connection that will outlast the house. Done carelessly it is a joint that arcs quietly for years.

  1. Strip about three quarters of an inch from each conductor. Same length on all of them.
  2. Hold the wires side by side with the ends flush. Do not pre-twist copper of different sizes together, just line them up.
  3. Push the connector on and turn clockwise, firmly, until the insulation on the wires starts to twist too. That is the signal it is fully engaged.
  4. Tug each wire individually. Every one of them. If a wire pulls out, the connection was never made. Redo it.
  5. No bare copper should show below the skirt of the connector. If it does, your strip length was too long.

Getting the size right

Connectors are rated by how many wires of what size they will accept, and the range is printed on the box. Too big and the spring never grips. Too small and you cannot get it on, or you strip the shell trying. The color coding varies by brand, so read the box rather than trusting the color.

Two more rules that matter. Copper and aluminum do not go in the same ordinary connector, ever. Aluminum branch wiring needs a specific rated connector and antioxidant compound, and honestly it needs an electrician. And never use a connector where a wire is nicked or the strands are broken, because you have already lost part of the conductor and the rest will run hotter.

Where the failures show up

Half the mystery electrical problems in an old house are a bad connector in a box behind a light fixture. The symptom is a light or outlet that works intermittently, sometimes coming back if you thump the wall. That is arcing, it generates heat, and it belongs at the top of the fix-it-now list rather than the get-around-to-it list.

If you open a box and find a connector that is discolored, has melted plastic, or has a copper conductor blackened at the end, do not simply put a fresh connector on and close it up. Cut back to bright clean copper first, and go look at what the circuit was being asked to carry. If the box shows scorching beyond the connector itself, stop and read the post on when to call an electrician.
