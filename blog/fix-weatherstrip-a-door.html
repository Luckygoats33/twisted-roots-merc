Fix It

Weatherstripping a Door That Whistles

Find the gap with a dollar bill, buy the right profile for it, and clean the frame before anything with adhesive touches it.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

An exterior door with a bad seal loses heat continuously, lets wind-driven rain get into the jamb, and makes a noise on a south-westerly that will keep you awake. Weatherstripping is one of the cheapest improvements in a house, and it fails almost entirely for one reason: people stick foam tape to a dirty or damp frame in cold weather and it falls off by spring.

What you need

  • Weatherstripping in a profile that matches your gap
  • A dollar bill or a strip of paper
  • Denatured alcohol and a rag
  • Scissors or snips, a tape measure
  • Small nails or screws if you are using the rigid kind

Find the gap before you buy anything

  1. Close the door on a dollar bill and pull. If it slides out with no drag, that spot is not sealing. Do it every foot around the whole perimeter and mark the loose spots.
  2. On a windy day, run the back of your hand slowly around the frame. You will feel it.
  3. At night, have somebody outside shine a flashlight around the closed door while you watch from inside.
  4. Note whether the gap is even. A gap only at the top or only on the latch side usually means the door is out of adjustment, and no weatherstrip will fix a door that is not closing square. That is a different repair, and it comes first.

The types

If your jamb has a groove with a flexible strip already in it, you have a kerf-in seal and replacement is trivial: pull the old one out, take it in to match, push the new one in. Do not glue foam over the top of a worn kerf seal.

Installing it so it stays

Clean the surface with denatured alcohol and let it dry. Not soapy water, which leaves a film. Then check the temperature. Most adhesives will not bond below about 50 degrees, and on a damp coastal morning the frame carries a film of moisture even when it looks dry. Warm the jamb with a hair dryer if you have to, and do this job on a dry afternoon.

  1. Measure each run and cut, rather than trying to bend one length around a corner.
  2. Peel a few inches of backing at a time and press as you go. Do not pull the whole backing off first.
  3. Press firmly along the entire length once it is placed. Adhesive strips bond under pressure.
  4. Do the hinge side, the latch side, then the header.
  5. Close the door. It should take slightly more effort than before but it should latch without a shove. If it will not latch, your strip is too thick or the strike plate needs moving.

What to expect

Adhesive foam is a season or two out here. Salt air, constant damp and big temperature swings between a heated house and a wet exterior are exactly what kills adhesive. If the door is one you use every day, spend the extra and put on nail-on or kerf-in seal once instead of foam tape four times.

The bottom of the door is a separate product and a separate job, which is a door sweep, covered in the sweep post.
