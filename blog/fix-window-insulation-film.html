Window Film for Single Pane Windows That Bleed Heat

A plastic kit and a hair dryer will not make an old window modern, but it will stop the draft and the condensation.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Old single pane windows lose heat two ways: straight through the glass, and by air leaking around the sash. Interior shrink film addresses both by putting a sealed layer of still air in front of the whole window. It is not glamorous, it costs about fifteen dollars a room, and on a windy night the difference in how a room feels is obvious.

What you need

  • A window insulation film kit, tape included
  • A hair dryer
  • Denatured alcohol and a clean rag
  • Scissors, and a helper for a big window

What it does and does not do

It creates a trapped air space, which is the same principle a double pane window uses. It cuts the draft you feel, it raises the surface temperature on the room side, and because of that it dramatically reduces condensation on the glass. In a coastal house where every window streams with water on a cold morning and the sills grow mildew, that last point is often the real reason to do it.

It will not stop water leaking in around a rotten frame, it will not fix a broken seal in a failed double pane unit, and it does not do much for noise. It also means the window cannot be opened until spring, so do not film the one you use for ventilation, and never film a window that is a required fire escape route in a bedroom.

Doing it so it is invisible

  1. Clean the frame where the tape will go. Denatured alcohol, then dry. Dust and old paint residue are why tape lets go, and a damp coastal window frame is the worst case.
  2. Apply the double sided tape to the frame, not the sash. You want the film to span the whole opening, so the trapped air layer includes the gaps around the sash.
  3. Leave the backing on the tape while you cut the film. Cut it two inches larger than the taped area on all sides.
  4. Peel the backing and start at the top. Press the film onto the top tape, then work down the sides, then the bottom. Keep it roughly taut but do not fight the wrinkles yet.
  5. Now the hair dryer. Medium heat, held a few inches away, moving constantly. The film shrinks and the wrinkles disappear. Work from the middle outward.
  6. Trim the excess with a sharp blade held against the tape edge.

Wrinkles that will not shrink out usually mean the film was pulled unevenly at one corner. Warm that area and it generally resolves. If the film pulls off the tape, the frame was not clean.

Doing it in a damp house

Here is the thing nobody prints on the box. If your windows are running with condensation before you film them, that moisture is in the room air, and sealing film over a cold window does not remove it. It moves it. The moisture will find the next coldest surface, which might be the corner of an exterior wall or the inside of a closet on the north side, and that is where you will get mildew instead.

So film the windows and also address the moisture: run the bathroom fan properly and for longer, use the range hood, do not dry laundry indoors without ventilation, and check that the crawlspace has a ground vapor barrier. Plastic on the crawlspace floor does more for indoor humidity in a coastal house than most people expect.

The alternative worth knowing

For a window you actually need to open in winter, or for a rental where tape on the frame is a problem, magnetic or rigid acrylic interior panels do the same job and come off cleanly. They cost more. They also last many seasons rather than one.

Film kits, weatherstripping, V-strip and mildew cleaner are on the Fix It wall, and the outside half of the problem is covered in the exterior sealant post.
