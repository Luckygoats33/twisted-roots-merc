Looking for Agates After a Storm

Gravel gets turned over in winter, which is when the stones show up — where to look, when to go, and how to actually see them.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Agate hunting is the most reliable good time on this coast in bad weather, which is convenient, because bad weather is what makes it work.

Why winter

Summer beaches are sand. Storm surf strips the sand off and exposes the gravel beds underneath, and it also rolls fresh material up out of the surf. So the beaches that are wide and smooth and boring in August turn into gravel in the winter months, and gravel is where agates are.

The best hunting is generally after a good blow, once the wind has dropped enough to be out there safely — often a day or two behind the storm rather than during it. Look for freshly worked gravel patches, especially near creek mouths and where the beach steepens.

What you are looking for

Agates are translucent. That is the whole identification trick. A wet agate held up against the sky glows and lets light through, where a similar-looking pebble is dead opaque.

They come in clear, white, grey, honey, orange and red, and the surface is often slightly waxy or pitted rather than smooth. Sizes run from lentil to plum, with the small ones being far more common. Jasper — opaque, red or yellow — turns up in the same gravel, and so does petrified wood and the occasional bit of fossil shell.

The classic mistake is picking up wet quartz all afternoon. Hold it to the light. If light does not pass through, put it back.

Where and when to walk

  • Outgoing tide toward low, so you are walking freshly uncovered ground rather than getting cut off.
  • Low sun — early or late — because a low angle lights up translucent stones from behind. Overcast is fine too. Bright overhead sun is the worst light for it.
  • Wet gravel beats dry. Water is what makes them glow; that is why they look dull once they dry in your pocket.
  • Creek and river mouths, where material gets sorted and delivered.
  • The edge of a gravel patch, where the last wave has just sorted the stones by weight.

How to actually see them

Slow down considerably. New people walk at normal walking speed scanning a wide area and find nothing. The people filling a bag are moving at a shuffle, looking at an area about the size of a doormat, with the sun in front of them rather than behind.

Once your eye locks onto the particular glow, it gets much easier and you will spot them from further away. Give it an hour before you decide you are bad at it.

The safety part, which is not optional in winter

You are looking down, on a beach, in the season when the surf is at its worst. That is precisely the profile of the people who get hurt.

Look up regularly. Never turn your back on the ocean. Stay well above the wet line. Do not stand on, sit on, or walk on the seaward side of drift logs, which are lifted and rolled by exactly the kind of water that makes good agate beaches. All of this is spelled out properly in sneaker waves and logs, and it is worth reading before a winter beach walk rather than after.

Know the tide before you go around any headland. A rising tide plus a bluff is how people get stranded.

What to take

Rubber boots, a jacket that is actually waterproof, a hat, gloves, and something to put stones in that will not soak through — an old cloth bag or a plastic tub. A walking stick if the gravel is loose. Nothing else. Fancy equipment does not help; time and low sun do.

Boots, wool socks and a decent hat are on the outdoors shelf, and there is usually something warm coming out of the oven if you stop in on the way over — the bakery rack is thin by nine, so early is better.
