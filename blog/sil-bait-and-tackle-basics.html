Around Siletz

Bait and Tackle Basics for Somebody Who Has Not Done This Before

What the stuff on the tackle shelf is actually for, in plain language, without the catalogue vocabulary.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The tackle aisle is deliberately confusing. Everything has a brand name and a colour name and no explanation of what it does. Here is the plain version, aimed at somebody who wants to go fishing this weekend rather than join a subculture.

The four ways to present something

Nearly every method on a river like this is one of four ideas.

  • Under a float. A bobber holds your bait or jig at a set depth and drifts it downstream at the speed of the current. Easiest to learn, easiest to see what is happening, and it catches everything in this river.
  • On the bottom. Weight gets your bait down and it tumbles along the gravel. Deadly for salmon and steelhead. Costs you weights.
  • Something moving. A spinner or a spoon that you cast and retrieve, or troll behind a boat. The blade flashes and vibrates and fish come to look.
  • Something that dives. A plug that swims down and wobbles when water pushes past it. Mostly a boat method here.

Pick one. Get good at it. A person who fishes a float well outfishes a person who does four things badly, every time.

Bait, briefly

Cured salmon eggs are the traditional bait for salmon and steelhead on this coast, and the curing is half of it — it toughens the eggs so they stay on the hook and it adds colour and scent. Ready-cured bait off the shelf works perfectly well and saves you a fortnight of learning to do it yourself with a smell in your garage.

Sand shrimp are the other classic, often used with eggs. Worms catch cutthroat and just about everything else. Whether bait is allowed at all depends on the stretch of water and the season, and that is Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife territory, so read the current regulations before you buy a tub of anything.

Terminal tackle, decoded

What it is calledWhat it does
SwivelStops your line twisting when a spinner spins. Small and boring and essential.
Snap swivelSame, with a clip so you can change lures without retying.
LeaderA short length of lighter line between your main line and the hook, so a snag costs you a hook rather than everything.
Split shotSmall crimped weights. Add or remove to get the depth right.
Slinky or pencil leadWeight that slides over rocks instead of wedging in them.
Bobber stopA tiny knot on your main line that sets how deep the float fishes.
BeadProtects the knot and adds colour. Sometimes both, sometimes decoration.
JigA weighted hook with hair or rubber on it, usually fished under a float.

Knots: learn two

An improved clinch knot to tie a hook or swivel to line, and a surgeon's knot or blood knot to join two lines. That is genuinely enough for years. Wet the line before you pull a knot tight — friction from a dry knot weakens it, and that is why people lose fish on gear they swear was fine.

Retie after every fish and after any hard snag. The last eighteen inches of line takes all the abuse.

Colour

People will argue about this until closing time. The short version that most people out here work by: bright and big in dirty water, small and natural in clear water. Pink, orange and chartreuse in the green-brown winter water. Something subtler when you can see the bottom.

If it has not produced in a reasonable stretch of good water, change one thing at a time. Changing four things at once teaches you nothing.

What to put in the first box

A spool of main line, a spool of lighter leader, a card of hooks in two sizes, a handful of swivels, split shot, two floats, a few bobber stops, three or four jigs, three or four spinners, and a tub of bait. That is a fishing kit. It fits in a coat pocket and it will catch anything in this river.

All of it is on the outdoors shelf, and if you stand in front of it looking lost somebody will come over, because that is more or less the point of the place.
