Sneaker Waves, Beach Logs and the Two Rules Nobody Should Break

The Oregon coast is not a warm-water beach and it does not behave like one — the specific hazards here, and how people get caught.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

This is the least funny thing on this website. People die on this coast every year, usually visitors, usually doing something that looked completely reasonable, and the two things that get them most often are sneaker waves and logs.

What a sneaker wave actually is

Waves do not arrive evenly. Swell comes in groups, and every so often a group lines up so that one run of water goes very much further up the beach than everything before it. Not a wall of water. Just a surge that runs two or three times further inland than the last twenty waves did, and arrives with no warning and no build-up.

It does not need to be big to be dangerous. Knee-deep water moving fast will take your feet out, and the drag back down a steep beach is strong. Cold water does the rest — the shock of it makes people gasp, and a gasp in water is how drownings happen.

They happen on calm sunny days. That is the part that catches people. The dangerous days are often the ones after a distant storm has sent a long-period swell across, and the surface at the beach can look untroubled.

Rule one: never turn your back on the ocean

That is the whole rule and it is not a saying, it is a procedure. If you are on the sand below the dry line, on rocks, on a jetty, or anywhere the water has visibly been, you keep the ocean in view. Photographs facing inland get taken from up the beach. Children get watched, not glanced at.

Watch where the highest run of water has reached — there is usually a wet line or a wrack line of debris. Stay above it. If the wet line moves up, move up with it.

Rule two: never stand on a beach log

Coastal Oregon beaches are covered in drift logs, and some of them are enormous. They look like furniture. They are not furniture.

A log sitting on wet sand becomes buoyant when water runs under it. It lifts, turns, and comes down. A log that weighs several tons does not need to move far to break a leg or pin somebody, and it moves quickly and silently. People have been killed by logs that had apparently been in the same place for weeks.

So: do not sit on them, do not stand on them, do not let kids climb on them, and do not walk on the seaward side of a big one when water is running around it. Above the high water line, well back, a log is a bench. Anywhere the tide reaches, it is a hazard.

The rest of the list

  • Do not get cut off. Know the tide before you walk around a headland. See reading a tide table.
  • Stay off jetties in swell. Wet rock, no handholds, and water that comes over the top.
  • Do not climb the bluffs. The soil here is loose and undercut and it gives way.
  • Keep dogs leashed near the water. Most of the worst outcomes on this coast start with somebody going in after a dog.
  • Never go in after somebody. Throw something that floats, call for help, keep them in sight. A second person in the water is the standard way one emergency becomes two.
  • Dress for cold water regardless of the air temperature. The ocean here stays cold all summer.

Rip currents are a separate problem

They are, and they have their own piece: how rips work and what to do in one. Worth reading even if you do not swim, because the advice is what you would shout at somebody who is in one.

Why locals seem casual and are not

People who grew up here look relaxed on the beach because the rules are automatic, not because they are ignoring them. Nobody local stands on a log. Nobody local turns their back on a big set. It is the same instinct as not standing behind a horse.

Learn the two rules, and then you can enjoy the place, which is genuinely the point. There is more on the storm-day version of this in storm watching and where not to stand, and a coat that will actually keep the weather out is on the outdoors shelf.
