Blackberries: Free Food and a Full-Time Enemy

The best free harvest of the year comes off a plant that is actively trying to take your property, which is the whole relationship in one sentence.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Everybody out here has two opinions about blackberries and holds both at once. In late summer they are the reason there is a cobbler on the counter. The rest of the year they are the reason a fence line disappeared.

Knowing what you have

Most of what you see along roads, fence lines and old fields is Himalayan blackberry — introduced, enormously vigorous, with thick arching canes, big thorns and broad leaves in groups of five. It is the one that swallows sheds. It also produces the biggest and most abundant fruit, which is exactly why it is still here.

There is also a native trailing blackberry, which runs low along the ground with thin wiry stems and much smaller berries. Those berries are better than the big ones by a distance, and picking a bowl of them takes a very long time.

Both are safely edible, which makes blackberries an unusually low-risk thing to pick — worth saying, since the foraging caution applies to almost everything else.

Picking

Late summer into early autumn, running earlier or later with the year. A berry is ready when it is fully black, dull rather than shiny, and comes off into your hand with no resistance. If you have to pull, leave it — it will be sour and it will be ready in a few days.

The good picking is not on the road side of the bush. It is the second row back and low down, where nobody has bothered to reach.

  • Long sleeves and long trousers, no matter how warm it is. This is not optional and everybody learns it once.
  • Rubber boots, because the good patches are in ditches and wet ground.
  • A container that hangs off you so both hands are free. An old ice cream tub on a belt is the traditional solution.
  • Do not pick right beside a busy road. Traffic film and spray both settle on them.
  • Check whose land it is. Roadside and public ground is generally fine; somebody's field is not.
  • Watch for wasps in the fruit and for a nest in the bramble, which is the actual hazard rather than the thorns.

What to do with them

Eat some, freeze most. Spread them on a tray in a single layer, freeze them solid, then bag them — they stay loose that way and you can take out a cupful instead of chiselling at a block. They keep well and they turn a wet February into something better.

Beyond that: cobbler, jam, syrup, pie, on a scone, in a pancake. There are a couple of things worth doing with a full tub in the recipes, and the bakery tends to reflect what is ripe.

The other half of the relationship

Himalayan blackberry is a serious invasive and it will take ground at a rate that surprises people. The canes arch over, touch the ground and root at the tip, so a single plant walks across a field. It regrows enthusiastically from the crown and from root fragments.

Cutting it down does not kill it. That is the thing everybody discovers in year two.

Actually getting rid of it

  1. Cut it back first so you can reach the base. A brush cutter, loppers or a machete, and heavy gloves.
  2. Then deal with the crown. Dig it out — the whole root ball, which is deeper and more determined than you expect. This is the step that does the work, and it is the step people skip.
  3. Or keep cutting. Repeated cutting through a full growing season, every time it puts up growth, will exhaust a crown eventually. It takes seasons, not weeks.
  4. Grazing works if you have goats and time.
  5. Herbicide is what a lot of people end up using on large infestations. If you go that way, read the label as if it were the law, because it is, and be careful about water — a great deal of ground here drains directly into the river.
  6. Then replant. Bare ground gets recolonised. Something else has to occupy it or the brambles come straight back.

The realistic goal

Not eradication. Control of a boundary. Most people out here end up with a line they defend — the fence, the edge of the field, the twenty feet around the house — and a truce beyond it. The truce produces the cobbler.

Loppers, gloves, brush blades and boots are in the store, and the yard is around back if you need something bigger. We are open early.
