Around Siletz

Cell Dead Zones, and How to Work Around Them

Coverage out here is a function of terrain, and terrain does not improve — so the answer is habits rather than a better phone.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

People assume rural coverage is a matter of one more tower. It is mostly a matter of hills. Radio does not bend around a ridge, and this county is nothing but ridges with roads and rivers in between them, so there are places with excellent signal a mile from places with none at all.

Where it goes away

The pattern is predictable once you see it.

  • In canyons and river bottoms where the walls block line of sight to anything.
  • Under heavy timber. Wet conifer canopy attenuates signal noticeably, which is why a marginal bar disappears when it rains.
  • On the back side of a ridge, in the radio shadow.
  • On forest roads generally, once you are a few turns off a main route.
  • In the middle of the Coast Range crossings, in patches, on every route.
  • Inside metal-roofed buildings, which is a lot of buildings out here.

And it varies by carrier in ways that do not follow a simple better and worse ranking. The carrier that works at your house may be the one that fails on the road you drive daily. Ask neighbours what they use rather than reading a national coverage map, which is drawn optimistically.

The habits that actually solve it

  1. Tell somebody your route and your rough return time. The single most effective safety measure available and it costs nothing.
  2. Download maps for offline use before you leave. Live navigation stops working exactly where you need it. A paper county map in the glovebox is the backup for the backup.
  3. Send a text rather than making a call when signal is marginal. A text is a tiny amount of data that will keep retrying in the background and will often get through when a call cannot hold.
  4. Turn wifi calling on. If you have any internet at home, it turns a house with no bars into a house with a working phone.
  5. Know where your local high spot is. Everybody out here has one — a bend, a gate, a particular pullout where the phone works. Learn yours.
  6. Keep the battery up. A phone hunting for a signal burns through a battery fast. Airplane mode when you know there is nothing preserves it for when you get somewhere there is.
  7. Carry a car charger.

Satellite messengers

For anybody who spends real time on forest roads, up drainages or on the water alone, a satellite messenger or a phone with satellite messaging is a genuinely different level of safety. It is not a gadget. It is the difference between a bad day and an unbounded one when you are somewhere nobody is going to drive past.

Newer phones have emergency satellite features and they are worth knowing how to use before you need them. Read the instructions in your kitchen rather than in a ditch.

At the house

If the house has no signal, wifi calling over whatever internet you have is the standard fix. A signal booster helps where there is a weak outdoor signal to amplify, and does nothing at all where there is none — that distinction is what makes people angry about boosters.

Keep a landline if you have one and it makes sense. In a long outage, a phone that does not depend on your power or your router is a real asset, though many modern services still need power at your end. Know which kind you have before the power goes out.

During an outage

Towers have battery backup and larger sites have generators, so cell service usually works at first and then degrades over hours. That is the pattern people misjudge: it is fine, then it is patchy, then it is gone. Make your important calls early, then switch to texting, and have a battery radio so you can find out what is happening without a network at all. The rest of that thinking is on the storm page.

The upside

There is one, and people who move here eventually admit it. Places where the phone does not work are places where nobody can reach you, and there are not many of those left. The trick is choosing when you are in one rather than finding out.

Batteries, chargers, radios and headlamps are on the shelf inside the door, and if you want a straight answer about which carrier actually works on your road, ask at the counter — somebody standing in the line will have tried all of them. We are here.
