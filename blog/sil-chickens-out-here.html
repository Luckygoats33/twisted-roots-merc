Keeping Chickens Out Here

Wet ground, a long list of predators and a mild winter — what that combination means for a coop that actually works.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Chickens do well on this coast. The winters are mild enough that cold is rarely the problem, and the summers are cool enough that heat rarely is either. What will beat you here is water and predators, in that order for damage and the reverse order for drama.

The predator list is long

Raccoons, coyotes, bobcats, weasels and mink, hawks, owls, the neighbour's dog, and your own dog. Each defeats a different weakness, which is why a coop needs to be right in several ways at once.

  • Hardware cloth, not chicken wire. This is the single most important sentence on this page. Chicken wire keeps chickens in. It does not keep anything out — a raccoon tears through it and a weasel walks through the holes. Half-inch hardware cloth, stapled and screwed with washers, on every opening.
  • Two-step latches. Raccoons open anything simpler. A barrel bolt with a carabiner through it, or a proper locking latch.
  • A buried skirt. Wire laid flat outward from the base for a couple of feet, pinned down, or buried. Diggers follow the wall down; a horizontal skirt stops them because they will not back up and start further out.
  • A covered run. Hawks and owls are constant here. Netting or wire over the top.
  • Shut them in at night. Every night. Automatic doors are worth the money precisely because they remove the human failure.
  • Close every gap. Anything a mind can fit through, a mink can fit through. Check where the roof meets the wall.

The wet is the real work

Eight months of rain turns a chicken run into mud, and mud is where the health problems come from — foot issues, parasites, and a smell that will make you unpopular with yourself.

  1. Site it on the highest, best-drained ground you have. Not the low corner that is already boggy. This decision is permanent and it decides everything.
  2. Roof the run, or at least a good part of it. A dry area to stand in changes everything about winter chicken keeping here.
  3. Build the floor up. Coarse wood chips, or sand over drain rock, so water goes down instead of sitting. Deep litter in the coop, topped up, works well and composts down.
  4. Get the gutter water away. Roof runoff into the run is how a run becomes a pond.
  5. Ventilate high, without draughts low. Damp air inside a coop causes far more trouble here than cold does. Vents up under the eaves, no wind blowing across the roost.

Cold is not your problem, damp is

A dry, well-ventilated coop needs no heat in this climate for standard breeds. Heat lamps are the leading cause of coop fires and they make birds less hardy. Skip them. Give them a wide flat roost so they can cover their feet, keep the bedding dry, and they will be fine.

Water freezing is only an occasional problem here, and it is usually solved with a heated base or just swapping the waterer in the morning.

Birds that suit the coast

Look for cold-hardy, rain-tolerant, calm breeds with practical combs. Heavier dual-purpose birds generally do well in this weather. Very large single combs are more prone to frostbite in a hard snap, which is uncommon here but not unknown up the valley. Ask around locally — the birds that thrive on your road are better evidence than any chart.

Feed and vermin

Store feed in metal cans with tight lids. A plastic bin gets chewed open, and spilled feed is the reason rats arrive. Feed inside or under cover so it is not on wet ground, and do not leave a full feeder out at night if you have a rodent problem — that is dinner service for everything on the predator list.

The same principle as the bin piece: no easy food, no visitors.

The neighbourly parts

Roosters carry a long way in a quiet valley, and whether you may keep one depends on where you are and who is nearby. Check your local rules, and think honestly about your neighbours before you find out the hard way.

Eggs are the best currency in a small town. A dozen handed over the fence buys more goodwill than anything else available, and this is genuinely how half the favours around here get done.

Hardware cloth, latches, staples, feeders, waterers, bedding and the lumber for a coop are all in the store and the yard. Feed is on the shelf, and if we do not carry what your birds are on, tell us — that is how things end up on the shelf permanently. Come and ask.
