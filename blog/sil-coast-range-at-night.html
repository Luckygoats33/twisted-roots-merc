Driving the Coast Range at Night

Over the hill in the dark in winter is the drive people underestimate most, and it has its own set of rules.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The routes over the Coast Range are ordinary roads in daylight in July. In December at nine at night they are something else, and the fact that they are only an hour or so of driving makes people treat them casually.

What changes after dark

Everything you rely on for speed judgement goes away. On a two-lane road through timber there are no lights, no distant reference points and often no reflective edge marking worth much. Your headlights are the whole world, and at a normal speed on a curving road you are outdriving them — the beam does not reach around the corner and you cannot stop within what you can see.

That is the core of it. Everything below is a consequence.

Elevation makes its own weather

It can be raining at the coast, clear in the valley, and snowing or icing on the summit between them. A pass a few hundred feet higher gets different weather to both ends of the drive, and it gets it first.

Freezing on a bridge deck and in shaded cuts happens before it happens anywhere else, because a bridge loses heat from underneath. Black ice on this side of the range is usually thin, local and in the same places every time — the shaded corner, the bridge, the spot where a seep crosses the road.

Check road conditions before you commit. The state runs a road conditions service and it is worth the two minutes, especially if you are coming home late.

The animals

Night in the range means elk and deer, and it means them at the worst possible viewing angle. Scan the verges rather than staring at the middle of the road. Eyeshine at the edge is your warning and it is often the only one you get. If you see one deer, slow immediately, because there is a second one and it is still to cross.

Elk are a different problem entirely and the piece on road wildlife explains why hitting one is not survivable the way hitting a deer often is.

Lights and how to use them

  • High beams whenever nothing is coming. Drop them early for oncoming traffic and for the vehicle you are following, and do not sit on them out of spite.
  • Clean the glass. Headlights, tail lights and windscreen. A film of road grime cuts output enormously and nobody ever notices it happening.
  • Cloudy plastic headlight lenses are worth restoring or replacing. It is the cheapest real safety improvement available to an older vehicle.
  • Fog is a low-beam situation. High beams reflect straight back — see the fog piece.
  • Wipers: replace them in autumn as a matter of routine. Smeared glass plus oncoming headlights is close to blindness.

The fatigue part

The late drive home from the valley after a long day is when people run off these roads. Steady speed, dark, monotonous curves, warm cab. If you are yawning, stop. A pullout and fifteen minutes is not weakness; it is the only actual fix, and coffee is a delay rather than a solution.

Before you go

  1. Fuel above half. There is nothing open in the middle and nothing open at the ends after a certain hour.
  2. Tell somebody the route and roughly when you expect to be back.
  3. Assume no signal for long stretches. Download directions rather than relying on live data, and see dead zones.
  4. Blanket, water, a light, a coat and something to eat in the vehicle. The full kit is here.
  5. If the forecast has snow at elevation, either carry chains or do not go.

If you break down

Get off the road as far as you can, hazards on, and stay in the vehicle unless it is unsafe. You are far more visible inside a car with lights on than standing beside it in dark clothing on a bend. If you must be outside, be uphill of the vehicle and off the pavement entirely.

A cheap headlamp, a blanket and a set of jumper cables cover most bad nights, and all three live in the outdoors aisle. The winter stock list is on the storm page.
