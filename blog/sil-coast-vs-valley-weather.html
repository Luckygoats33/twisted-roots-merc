Around Siletz

Coast Weather Versus Valley Weather, and Why the Forecast Lies

Two entirely different climates separated by a range of hills and about an hour of driving, which is why nobody here trusts a regional forecast.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Somebody moves out here from Salem or Corvallis and spends the first year confused about the weather. It is not that the forecast is bad. It is that they are reading a forecast for a different climate, delivered by a station on the other side of a mountain range.

The mechanism, in one paragraph

Weather here comes off the Pacific, moving east. It hits the Coast Range and has to go up. Rising air cools, cooling air drops its water, and so the western slopes get soaked. By the time that air comes down the eastern side into the Willamette Valley it has already lost much of its moisture and it warms as it descends. That is the whole story: wet on this side, drier on that side, and the hills in between doing the sorting.

The same range works in reverse for temperature. The ocean holds a steady temperature all year and it drags everything near it toward that number. So the coast has a small range — mild winters, cool summers — and the valley has a big one, with real frost and real heat.

The east wind exception

Once in a while the pattern reverses and air flows from inland out to the coast. Air coming down off the hills compresses and warms, so an east wind day here can be the hottest and clearest of the year, with the fog gone and the ocean flat. Those are the days people photograph.

They are also the days with the highest fire risk on this coast, because dry warm wind off the land is exactly what a wet forest does not usually get. A coastal forest is not fireproof. It is just usually wet.

Why the forecast for town is wrong for your house

Even inside Lincoln County the differences are large. A forecast written for a coastal town is a forecast for a beach and a bay. Siletz sits inland up the river valley, which means:

  • Warmer on a summer afternoon than the beach, sometimes by a lot, because the marine air does not always push this far up the valley.
  • Colder on a clear winter night, because cold air pools in valleys and there is no ocean right there to hold the temperature up.
  • Less wind than the exposed coast, but more falling timber, because we are surrounded by tall trees and they are not.
  • More fog sitting in the valley bottoms on a still morning.

Add elevation and it changes again. Snow that does not touch the valley floor will stick on the ridges and on the roads over the hill, which matters if you are driving to the valley. See driving the Coast Range.

How to actually forecast around here

Look at the marine forecast and the wind, not just the rain percentage. Wind is what causes trouble here; rain is just Tuesday. Watch the pressure trend — a fast fall means a real blow coming. Learn what your own place does relative to the nearest official station, because after a couple of seasons you will know that you run three degrees colder or that the fog burns off an hour later than at the beach.

And keep an eye on what the sky is doing, which is old advice but works, because the weather here arrives from a direction you can see. The first blow of the season is the one that catches everyone, and what to have in the house before it turns up is on the storm page.
