A Coastal Garden, and What Will Not Grow Here

The climate is generous about some things and completely unwilling about others, and knowing which is which saves years.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The most useful thing anybody can tell a new gardener on this coast is the list of things to stop trying. Not because it cannot be done with enough plastic and effort, but because there is a long list of things that grow here almost by themselves, and beginners spend three seasons on tomatoes instead.

What the climate is actually offering

A long, mild, wet growing season with a great deal of light but not much heat. Frost is uncommon and rarely hard at low elevation near the coast, so the season is long at both ends. What is missing is warm nights and accumulated heat. Cool summers, marine air, and fog that can sit for days in the middle of what is supposed to be summer.

That combination is close to ideal for leaves, roots and brassicas, and close to hopeless for anything that needs to bake.

Things that grow well with very little argument

  • Kale, chard, collards. They will grow through the winter and be there in February when nothing else is.
  • Lettuce and salad greens. The cool season means they do not bolt the way they do inland.
  • Peas and broad beans. Early, happy, and they like the cool.
  • Brassicas. Broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts. This is their climate.
  • Potatoes. Reliably.
  • Root vegetables. Carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips.
  • Alliums. Leeks in particular, and garlic planted in the autumn.
  • Berries. Blueberries love the acid soil. Raspberries, currants, gooseberries and strawberries all do well.
  • Rhubarb. Plant it once and it becomes a landmark.
  • Apples and plums, with attention to varieties suited to a cool wet climate and to disease resistance.

The three things that decide everything

Drainage. The default problem here is waterlogged soil, not dry soil. Raised beds are not a style choice on this coast, they are the fix. Get the roots up out of the winter water table and half the trouble disappears.

Wind. A coastal garden without a windbreak is a garden of shredded leaves and rocking root balls. Filter the wind rather than blocking it — a solid fence makes turbulence on the lee side. See wind-tolerant planting.

Slugs. Not a nuisance. A primary limiting factor, and the single biggest cause of failed seedlings out here. That gets its own piece, deservedly.

Soil

Expect acidic soil, often heavy, often low in some nutrients because decades of rain have washed them downward. Get a test rather than guessing. Lime where a test says to, particularly for brassicas, and leave the blueberries alone because they want it acid.

Compost and organic matter are the answer to both clay and to sand, and there is no shortage of raw material out here.

What to do first

Build one raised bed in the sunniest, most sheltered spot you have, fill it well, put a slug plan in place, and plant the easy list. Get one good season. Then expand, and then, if you still want tomatoes, build a small tunnel and grow them properly instead of hopefully.

Seeds, starts, soil, slug bait, netting, stakes, twine and fencing are on the shelf here, and there is more on the timing in the growing season. The yard out back has the lumber for beds — pull the truck round.
