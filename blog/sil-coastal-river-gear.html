Gear for a Coastal River, Sorted by What You Actually Need

What to buy first, what to buy later, and what people spend money on that does nothing for them on a river like this.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Fishing gear is one of those categories where you can spend anything at all, and the amount you spend has almost no relationship to how many fish you touch. Here is the order we would buy it in for a river like this one.

The things that actually matter first

Not the rod. Being warm, dry and upright matters first, because cold wet people fish badly for two hours and then go home.

  • Rain gear that actually works. Not a shell that is water resistant. Actual waterproof, with a hood that turns with your head. It rains sideways here and a bad jacket is the same as no jacket.
  • Boots. Knee rubber boots for bank work and mud, wading boots with proper grip if you are going in. Felt versus rubber soles is an argument with rules attached in some states, so check before you buy.
  • Wool socks. Two pair. Cotton socks in a wet boot in February is self-harm.
  • A hat with a brim. Keeps rain off your glasses so you can see the water.
  • Polarised glasses. The cheapest thing on this list that makes the biggest difference to reading water, and they stop a hook from taking an eye.

Then the rod and reel

One decent all-round setup beats three specialised ones you do not know how to use. For this river, a medium or medium-heavy spinning rod somewhere in the eight and a half to nine and a half foot range covers most of what a person does — cutthroat with lighter line, steelhead and salmon with heavier. Length helps you control line at distance, which matters more on a river than casting distance does.

The reel needs a smooth drag and to not be full of grit. Rinse it in fresh water after tidewater or the bay, every time, and it will last years. Do not rinse it and it will last one season.

The small stuff people forget

Pliers or forceps, a knife, nail clippers for line, a headlamp because you will be walking out in the dark, a tape measure if regulations mention length, and a bag for your rubbish. A net with a rubber bag is kinder to fish and does not tangle hooks. A wading staff if you go in the water at all in winter, which is genuinely a safety item and not an accessory.

And a cooler with ice in it in the truck, not a plan to buy ice later. Cold fish is covered properly in the handling piece.

What we would not bother with early

Six rods. Three tackle boxes organised by month. A fish finder for a river. Scent products, until you have caught enough fish to know whether they change anything. Anything sold as a system.

The thing that puts fish in the net is time on the water and knowing where to stand, and no purchase substitutes for either.

What we keep

We are not a tackle shop and we are not pretending to be. What we carry is the run-out-of list: line, hooks, weights, a basic river tackle assortment, bait, rubber boots, wool socks, a cooler and a folding chair. The things you discover you are missing at ten to six in the morning when Newport is twenty-seven minutes away and closed.

It is all on the outdoors shelf inside the door, and the coffee is on by six for the same reason.
