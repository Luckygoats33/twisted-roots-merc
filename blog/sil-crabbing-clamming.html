Crabbing and Clamming, and Checking the Closure First

The one thing you must do before you go, and then the practical business of pots, rings, shovels and tides.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

This is the one activity on this coast where the safety check is not optional and not a formality. Shellfish can be closed for biotoxins, and a closure is not about conservation, it is about people getting badly ill. So the order of operations is: check, then plan, then go.

Check the closure. Every single time.

Marine biotoxins — the things behind paralytic and amnesic shellfish poisoning — accumulate in filter feeders, and levels change through the year with no visual warning. The water looks fine. The clams look fine. Testing is the only way anybody knows.

Oregon Department of Agriculture runs the shellfish biotoxin closure information, and ODFW carries it too. Closures can apply to razor clams, bay clams, mussels, and sometimes to crab viscera specifically while the meat stays fine. Sections of coast close and reopen independently.

Do not rely on a friend, a forum, a sign that may be old, or the fact that it was open the last time you went. Check the current status on the day, before you drive.

Crabbing, the practical part

Dungeness in the bays is the main event. You can crab from a dock, from a boat, or off a pier, and each is a different amount of effort.

  • Rings are cheap, fold flat and need working — you drop them, wait, and pull them fast and steady so the crab do not swim off the flat ring.
  • Pots hold crab in, so you can leave them longer and check less often. Heavier, more expensive, harder to haul by hand.
  • Bait is fish carcasses, chicken, or whatever is oily and strong. In a bait container or wired in so it does not just feed the crab and leave.
  • A gauge to measure, and the knowledge of how to tell males from females, are both required equipment rather than nice to have.

Tide matters enormously. The general working rule is that slack water around high tide is easiest, because you are not fighting current and crab move on the tide. Fishing a big outgoing from a dock mostly teaches you about drag.

Clamming

Bay clams — gapers, cockles, butter clams — come out of the mud flats on a low tide, with a shovel or a clam gun and a lot of bending over. Razor clams are a surf beach thing, on a minus tide, and they are fast and will beat you to the hole if you are slow.

You want the biggest low tide you can get, and you want to be there before it, not at it. The window is short. There is a whole piece on reading a tide table because clamming without one is just walking on mud.

Wear something you do not care about. Rubber boots that come off in mud are worse than useless, so laced or strapped is better. A bucket, gloves and a hose at the end of it.

Do not dig past your limit and do not leave holes

Limits, sizes, whether you keep every clam you dig regardless of size, and licence requirements are Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife business and they differ between species. Read the current rules. The keep-what-you-dig rule for some clams surprises people and is worth knowing before you start.

Fill your holes on a flat. Leaving a field of craters is both bad manners and hard on everything living in the mud.

Getting them home edible

Keep crab cold and damp, not submerged in fresh water and not sitting in melted ice. Cook them the same day. Purge clams in salt water so they spit out sand, or you will be eating grit and blaming the recipe.

More on the general principle in keeping the catch cold. Coolers, gloves, buckets and rubber boots are on the outdoors shelf, and a few things worth doing with the result are in the recipes.
