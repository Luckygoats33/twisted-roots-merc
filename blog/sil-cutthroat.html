Around Siletz

Sea-Run Cutthroat, the Friendliest Fish in the River

Late summer, light gear and a fish that will actually chase things — the easiest way into fishing the Siletz.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

If somebody asks how to start fishing this river without spending a winter being cold and unsuccessful, the answer is sea-run cutthroat. Light rod, small gear, decent weather, and a fish that is genuinely willing to come and look at what you are doing.

What they are

Coastal cutthroat that go to sea and come back into the rivers. They run smaller than salmon and steelhead by a long way — think of a good trout rather than a freight train — and they are bright and hard-fighting for their size. They come back into the lower river and tidewater in the back half of summer and into fall, generally ahead of the fall salmon push, and they move up as the water allows.

There are resident cutthroat in the smaller water year round too, up the tributaries and the little creeks, which is a different and quieter kind of fishing.

Where to look

Tidewater and the lower river, around structure. Cutthroat like an edge. Undercut banks, overhanging alder, log jams, the deep side of a bend, the shade line where a tree hangs over the water. If it looks like a place a fish could ambush something, fish it.

They move with the tide down low, and they will follow bait fish. Early and late in the day is better, as it is with almost everything, and an overcast day beats bright sun.

Gear you probably already own

  • A light spinning rod and a small reel. Nothing heroic.
  • Light line. Six to eight pound is plenty and lighter fishes better in clear water.
  • Small spinners and spoons. The single most reliable way to catch them.
  • Worms or a small bait under a float where bait is allowed.
  • A fly rod if that is your religion. They are one of the best fly targets on the coast.

Small hooks, pinched barbs, and something to get them off with. A cheap spinning combo and a box of spinners will genuinely do the whole job.

How to fish it

Cast past the structure and bring the lure back along it rather than at it. Vary the retrieve until something works, then keep doing that. Cover water. Cutthroat are not a fish you sit and wait for; you walk and cast and walk again, and you find them in pockets rather than spread evenly.

From a boat in tidewater, work the banks. From the bank, work whatever bit of structure you can reach and then move on. Twenty casts in one spot is usually nineteen too many.

Handle them like you want them back

Cutthroat are not an inexhaustible resource and a lot of this fishing is catch and release by choice as much as by rule. If you are letting one go, keep it in the water, wet your hand before you touch it, back the hook out, and hold it facing into current until it kicks off on its own. A fish held up in the air for a photograph while somebody finds their phone is a fish with a much worse chance.

What you may keep, where, and with what gear is Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife business, it differs between the mainstem, tidewater and the tributaries, and it changes. Look at the current regulations before you go rather than after.

Why it is a good place to start

Because you will get bites. Learning to fish while never touching anything is a terrible way to learn, and cutthroat in late summer give you enough feedback to figure out what casting well, reading a bank and setting a hook actually feel like. Everything you learn there transfers straight to the bigger fish in the harder months.

Take a kid. Take the light rod. Go in the evening when the wind drops and the river goes flat, and do not bother driving far — the water inside a few minutes of town is as good as anything.

Spinners, line and hooks are on the outdoors shelf, and there are sandwiches in the case by seven if you want to take one with you.
