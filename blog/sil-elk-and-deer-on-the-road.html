Elk and Deer on the Road

Two different animals, two different sets of consequences, and a handful of habits that keep you from meeting either at speed.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Everybody out here has a story. What is worth understanding is that deer and elk present completely different problems, and the advice that works for one is wrong for the other.

Deer

Black-tailed deer are everywhere around here, in the woods, in the fields, in gardens and on the verges. They are small enough that a collision at moderate speed is usually a damaged vehicle rather than a catastrophe, and they are unpredictable in a specific way: they bolt, they stop dead in the road, and they change direction for no visible reason.

The rule that matters: if you see one deer, there are more. They move in loose groups and the second one is always still to cross. Braking for the deer you can see and then accelerating is how people hit the one they could not.

Elk

Roosevelt elk are an entirely different proposition. A cow elk is several hundred pounds and a bull is a great deal more, and they are tall — the mass sits high, so in a collision the body comes over the hood and through the windscreen rather than under the bumper. This is why elk collisions injure and kill people while deer collisions mostly injure vehicles.

They move in herds. A herd crossing a road at night is a wall of dark bodies with no reflective surface at car-headlight height, and drivers report seeing legs before they see anything else. They are also unhurried. An elk that has decided to cross will cross, and it does not respond to a horn the way you want it to.

Where they are: river bottoms, meadows, the edges of clearcuts and reprod, and pasture at the forest edge. Which describes most of the ground the roads here run through.

When

Dawn and dusk, heavily. Those are the movement hours and they coincide with the two worst light conditions for spotting anything. Autumn is worse than average because the animals are moving more, and it stays busy through the winter as they come down out of higher ground.

Fog and rain make all of it worse in the obvious way — see the fog piece.

Driving habits that actually help

  • Scan the verges, not the centreline. Sweep your eyes along the edge of the light. That is where the warning is.
  • High beams whenever you can. Eyeshine is visible much further out with them.
  • Slow down at dawn and dusk on the river road. Not a token amount. Enough that you could stop.
  • Take the wildlife signs seriously. They are not decorative; they are placed where there is a history.
  • Cover the brake through open meadow edges and along field lines.

If one is in front of you

Brake hard and straight. Do not swerve. This is the single most important piece of advice on this page, and it is counterintuitive.

Swerving on a narrow road with no shoulder puts you into oncoming traffic, into a ditch, into a tree, or over an edge. The overwhelming majority of serious injuries in animal collisions come from what the driver hit after the animal, or from a rollover. Hitting a deer squarely at a reduced speed with the vehicle under control is a survivable, insurable event. Leaving the road is often not.

Keep the wheel straight, brake as hard as the surface allows, and stay in your lane.

Afterwards

Get off the road with hazards on if the vehicle moves. Check for people first, then the vehicle. If an injured animal is on the road it is a hazard to the next driver, and that is a call to make to the state police non-emergency line or the county — they will handle it. Do not approach an injured elk. An animal that size in pain is dangerous at close range and there is nothing useful you can do.

Salvage rules for road-killed animals exist in Oregon and have specific requirements including a permit. That is Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife territory, so check their current rules rather than assuming, and do not just load one in a truck.

Report the collision to your insurer. Animal strikes are usually handled differently to at-fault collisions, and people fail to claim because they do not know that.

Good lights, a headlamp, a first aid kit and a blanket in the vehicle cover the rest. Most of that is on the storm shelf, and there is more in the truck kit.
