Around Siletz

What Actually Burns Well Around Here

You will mostly be offered alder, fir and maple — how they compare, what to pay attention to, and what not to put in a stove.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Firewood arguments around here are settled by what is available, and what is available is what grows: alder, Douglas fir, maple, and whatever came down in the last storm. There is no oak-and-hickory conversation to be had on this coast.

The thing that actually matters

Dryness. It is not close. Well-seasoned alder beats badly seasoned maple every single time, and a stove full of wet fir is worse than a stove with less wood in it.

Wet wood spends its energy boiling water out of itself instead of heating your house. You get less heat, a smoky fire that will not stay lit, and creosote building in the flue, which is the actual cause of chimney fires. So species is a preference and moisture is a requirement. The whole business of getting there is in splitting and seasoning.

Buying it

A cord is a stacked pile four feet by four feet by eight feet — a hundred and twenty-eight cubic feet. That is the legal unit and it is worth knowing, because terms like truckload, rick and face cord mean nothing consistent and are how people get short-changed.

  • Ask what species, and ask when it was cut and split. Cut is not the same as split, and split is when the drying really starts.
  • Look at the ends. Seasoned wood has cracks radiating from the centre, is grey or dull rather than fresh-coloured, and two pieces knocked together ring rather than thud.
  • Heft it. Dry wood is startlingly light compared to green.
  • See it stacked before it is tipped, if you can, or stack it yourself and measure. A tipped pile is not a measurable pile.
  • Buy in the spring for next winter. Buying in October means burning wet wood, and everybody knows it, which is why the price is higher then.

What not to burn

  1. Driftwood. Tempting on this coast and a genuinely bad idea. Salt-laden wood releases corrosive compounds that eat stoves and flues.
  2. Treated, painted or stained wood. Toxic in the smoke and in the ash.
  3. Plywood, particle board, pallets of unknown origin. Glues and whatever they carried.
  4. Anything you would not want to breathe. Which includes most rubbish people put in a stove because it is there.
  5. Green wood of any species. See above.

Ash from clean wood is fine on a garden bed in moderation. Ash from anything on the list above is not, and should go in the bin.

Getting your own

Cutting firewood on public forest land generally requires a permit, and on timber company ground it depends entirely on the owner and the season. Ask first. Fire restrictions in the dry months can shut chainsaw use down completely at certain hours of the day, and those restrictions are real and enforced.

If you are heading up a haul road for wood, read the log truck piece and take the truck kit.

The practical answer for most people

Alder, split, stacked under cover, a full year ahead. It is what most people burn here, it dries faster than anything else available, and if you have a year of it under a roof you will be warm and your flue will stay clean.

Bundles are out front for a campfire or a first night in a cold house, and the stove kit — gloves, matches, fire starter, a moisture meter — is inside. The storm shelf covers the rest of the cold-weather list.
