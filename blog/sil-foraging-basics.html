Around Siletz

Foraging Basics, With One Hard Rule

There is a great deal growing out here that is worth eating, and a small amount that will put you in a hospital — the difference is certainty.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Start with the rule, because everything else on this page depends on it.

If you are not completely certain what something is, you do not eat it. Not a taste. Not a small piece. Not because it looks like the picture. Certainty or nothing.

That is not caution for the sake of it. Oregon has mushrooms that will destroy a liver, and the symptoms of the worst of them do not appear until well after the damage is done, by which point feeling fine is not evidence of anything. There is no folk test — not smell, not peeling, not silver spoons, not whether animals eat it. Those are all myths and some of them have killed people.

How to actually get to certainty

  1. Learn from a person, not a picture. A mycological society, a class, or somebody experienced walking with you. Photographs flatten the exact details that matter.
  2. Learn one species at a time, thoroughly, including the things it can be confused with. Knowing the lookalikes is the actual skill.
  3. Use a proper regional guide, not a general one and not an app. Species vary by region, and an identification app is a suggestion engine, not an authority.
  4. Check every diagnostic feature, every time, on every specimen. Not most of them. Not the easy ones.
  5. When you are new, get it confirmed by somebody who knows before you cook it.

The things that are genuinely low risk

Berries are where most people start, sensibly, because the common edible ones here are distinctive and the dangerous ones are not easily confused with them once you know both.

  • Blackberries — abundant to the point of nuisance. Their own subject entirely.
  • Salmonberry — early, orange to red, on arching canes in wet ground. Variable in flavour.
  • Thimbleberry — soft red berry, large maple-shaped leaves with no thorns. Delicate and does not travel.
  • Huckleberry — the prize, and covered separately.
  • Salal — dark purple, slightly mealy, good cooked down.

Learn the things you should not eat as deliberately as the things you should. Around here that includes the white-berried plants, the red baneberry, and anything in the ditch that resembles a wild carrot — the water hemlock family is present in the Northwest and it is one of the most toxic plants on the continent. If a plant with lacy leaves and umbrella-shaped white flower clusters is growing in wet ground, leave it entirely alone. That whole family is not a beginner's category and arguably not anybody's.

Mushrooms, said carefully

The Oregon coast is famous for its mushrooms and people here take them seriously. Chanterelles in particular are a genuine autumn tradition, and there are others.

What we are not going to do is print an identification guide, because a store website is the wrong medium for something where being ninety percent right is not good enough. Go with somebody. Take a class. Join a group. That is how everybody who does this well learned it, and it is not a step that can be replaced with reading.

The rules that are not about safety

  • Know whose land you are on. Private, timber company, public and tribal land all have different rules and some require permits. Find out first — the same discipline as river access.
  • Permits exist for harvesting on public forest land, including for personal use in some cases and certainly for commercial quantities. Ask the managing agency.
  • Take a portion, leave the rest. Cut mushrooms rather than tearing up the ground, leave the small ones and the old ones, and do not strip a patch.
  • Do not pick out of a roadside ditch. Runoff, spray and traffic all end up in what is growing there.

Going out safely

Off-trail here means salal, downed timber, steep ground and no visibility. It is very easy to get turned around in a place that looks the same in every direction. Take a compass, tell somebody your route, wear something visible, and expect your phone not to work — dead zones. During any hunting season, wear blaze orange, which is on the hunt wall and costs almost nothing.

Watch for ticks, which are a real presence in the brush here — the tick piece covers checking and removal.

Baskets, gloves, boots and a compass are on the outdoors shelf. The identification is on you, and it should be.
