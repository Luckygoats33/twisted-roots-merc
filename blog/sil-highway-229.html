Around Siletz

Highway 229 and the River Road

A beautiful piece of road, a narrow one, and one that behaves completely differently in the wet half of the year.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Highway 229 follows the Siletz River, and following a river means doing what the river does. It bends where the water bends, it runs tight against rock in places and against a drop in others, and it was laid out to connect places rather than to move traffic quickly.

People who drive it every day like it. People meeting it for the first time in the rain at dusk tend to have opinions.

What the road is like

Narrow lanes, no shoulder worth the name in a lot of it, and blind corners where the bank comes right down to the pavement. Trees close on both sides in stretches, which means it is darker than the sky suggests and it stays wet long after the rain stops. Guardrail where there is a drop, and not everywhere there is a drop.

There are pullouts, and there are places that look like pullouts and are somebody's field entrance. There is a difference and the difference matters if a log truck comes around behind you.

The wet-season behaviour

  • Standing water on corners. Ditches fill and drains block, so water sheets across the road in specific repeatable spots. Learn where yours are.
  • Gravel and debris. Rain washes material off the cut banks onto the pavement, especially at the outside of bends. Hitting a fan of gravel mid-corner is the classic local single-vehicle accident.
  • Branches and whole trees. After any wind. Assume at least one on a long drive during a storm.
  • Slides and slumps. The bank comes down, or the road edge goes. See landslides.
  • Moss and leaf mat in the shaded stretches, which is slick in a way that is not obvious until you brake.

None of that is dramatic. It just means the road you drove at a comfortable speed in August is not the same road in January, and the people who come off it are almost always driving it at the August speed.

How to drive it

Stay right, because somebody will be over the centre line on a blind bend and it will be a wide vehicle. Stay off the very edge, because the edge crumbles and there is often gravel or a soft shoulder there. The middle of your own lane, consistently, is the whole technique.

Slow into corners and accelerate out, rather than braking mid-corner on a wet surface. Assume something is stopped, standing or lying in the road just past every blind bend, because eventually it is.

Lights on in daylight. It is a dark corridor and being visible earlier is free.

Sharing it

Log trucks use this road and they are the priority vehicle whether or not anybody has said so. Loaded, they cannot stop quickly and they cannot swerve. Give them the room and let them past when you can — the whole subject is here.

The same applies to farm equipment, propane trucks, the school bus and anybody towing a boat to the ramp early. If you have three cars behind you, use a pullout. It costs you fifteen seconds and it stops somebody making a bad decision on a blind bend.

Wildlife

Elk and deer use the river bottom, and this road runs through the river bottom. Dawn and dusk are the worst, and a herd of elk on the road is a genuinely different problem to a single deer — covered separately.

Signal

Do not count on it. The canyon and the timber take coverage away for meaningful stretches, so if something goes wrong you may be walking or waiting rather than calling. Which is the reason for the truck kit and for telling somebody your route before a winter drive.

What it is good for

It is one of the better drives in the county on a clear evening, it goes past a lot of water worth looking at, and it is the reason a lot of people found this town in the first place. Take it slowly and enjoy it, which happens to be the same advice as taking it safely.

If you break down on it, we are on it. Come inside and ask for Eric — there are jumper cables behind the counter and that is not a marketing line, it is just what happens. Hours are here.
