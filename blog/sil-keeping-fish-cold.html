Keeping Fish Cold, Which Is Most of Whether It Tastes Good

Almost everything people dislike about the fish they catch happens in the first hour, in the back of a warm truck.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A salmon that has been handled properly and a salmon that rode home warm in a bucket are not the same food. They are barely the same species. And the difference is decided in the first hour, before anybody gets near a kitchen.

Bleed it immediately

If you are keeping a fish, dispatch it and bleed it right away. Cut the gill arches or the artery under them and put the fish back in the water for a minute or two, or into a bucket of river water, and let it empty out. Blood left in the flesh is what makes fish taste strong and metallic, and it is what makes it go off faster.

This is not a refinement. It is the single biggest thing you can do to the quality of the meat, and it costs nothing but doing it in the first minute instead of the last.

Then get it cold, and keep it cold

Cold is the whole game after that. A fish sitting on grass in the sun for an hour has already lost a lot of what you were going to eat.

  • Ice, in a cooler, in the truck, before you leave the house. Buying ice on the way home is buying it too late.
  • Layer it. Ice underneath, fish, ice on top. Contact is what does the work; a bag of ice sitting beside a fish is decoration.
  • Slush beats cubes. Ice plus a little water makes a slurry that touches every part of the fish and pulls heat out far faster than dry ice cubes with air gaps.
  • Drain it. Meltwater sitting around a fish for a day is not doing it favours. Keep the plug open or tip it out.
  • Gut it sooner rather than later if you can do it cleanly. The guts are the fastest-spoiling part and they are right against the meat.

The cooler is a real piece of equipment

A twenty-five quart cooler handles a normal day. A big fall chinook does not fit in one, and folding a fish to make it fit is a good way to bruise the meat. If you are targeting big fish, size the cooler for the fish, not for the sandwiches.

Pre-chill the cooler the night before with a sacrificial bag of ice or a few jugs of frozen water. A warm cooler spends its first hour melting your ice instead of cooling your fish. Frozen water jugs also last much longer than loose ice and you get drinking water at the end of it.

Getting it home

The back of a canopy on a sunny day is an oven. Coolers go in the shade, lid closed, opened as little as possible. If the drive is long, throw a wet towel over the cooler — evaporation does real work.

Then deal with the fish that evening rather than tomorrow. Filleting a properly chilled fish is easier anyway because firm flesh cuts clean.

Freezing without ruining it

Freezer burn is dehydration. Air is the enemy.

  1. Portion into meal-sized pieces before freezing. Nobody wants to thaw a whole side to feed two people.
  2. Vacuum seal if you have it. If not, wrap tight in plastic with the air pressed out, then foil, then a bag.
  3. Label with what it is and which fish it came off. You will not remember.
  4. Freeze flat so it thaws evenly and stacks.
  5. Eat the oldest first. A rotation is the whole trick.

Thaw in the fridge overnight, not on the counter and not in warm water. Slow and cold keeps the texture.

Crab and clams are different

Shellfish generally want cold and damp but not drowned in fresh water or sitting in melted ice. Keep crab in a cooler with a wet towel and some ice separated from them rather than submerged. Cook them the day you get them if you possibly can. And check closures before you go out at all — see the shellfish piece.

Coolers, ice and the rest of it are on the outdoors shelf. If you end up with more than you can eat, there are a couple of ways to use it up in the recipes.
