Sharing a Road With a Logging Truck

They are working, they are heavy, and they cannot do the things you are assuming they can — how to drive around them without incident.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

This is timber country and the roads carry timber. A loaded log truck is one of the heaviest things on any road around here and it is being driven by somebody doing a job with a schedule, on roads that were not built for it, often on a gravel spur before it reaches the pavement.

Almost every problem between cars and log trucks comes from a driver assuming a truck can do something it cannot.

What they cannot do

  • Stop quickly. A loaded truck needs a great deal more distance than you do, and more again on a wet road or a grade. Pulling out in front of one is not the same as pulling out in front of a pickup.
  • Swerve. The load is high, long and heavy. A sudden avoidance manoeuvre is how a truck ends up on its side, and no driver is going to choose that.
  • Stay entirely in one lane on a tight bend. The trailer tracks inside the tractor. On a narrow river road corner, part of that rig is over the line, and it has to be.
  • See you where you think they can. The blind areas alongside and directly behind are very large. If you cannot see the mirrors, the driver cannot see you.

How to be behind one

Sit well back. Further than feels necessary. Two reasons: you can see more of the road ahead, and you are outside the zone where a shifted log or a thrown rock reaches you. Gravel comes off those tyres with real energy.

Do not tailgate to pressure a truck into pulling over. Most drivers around here will use a pullout when there is a safe one, and crowding them just makes it harder to judge.

Passing

Only where you can see the whole manoeuvre through to the end. That means a genuine straight, not the middle of a bend, and it means accounting for the length of the rig — passing a log truck takes considerably longer than passing a car and you need to be back in your lane with margin.

Do not pass on a bend, do not pass on a grade where you will lose speed, and do not pass on a wet gravel surface. When you come back in, leave real distance before you do — cutting back in front of a vehicle that cannot brake is exactly the wrong move.

Meeting one on a narrow road

Move right, slow down, and hold your line. Do not drift toward the centre to look past. If the road is genuinely too narrow, the smaller vehicle backs up or uses a pullout, because it is the one that can — a loaded truck reversing on a mountain road is a serious operation.

Expect the trailer to be inside the line on a corner and give it the room. Expect wind buffeting as it passes. Hold the wheel.

Gravel and spur roads

On active forest roads the rules change again. Many mainlines have haul traffic that assumes it owns the road, and some are radio-controlled with drivers calling mile markers on a designated channel. If you are on those roads recreationally you need to know that this is happening and to drive expecting a truck around every blind corner.

Drive slowly, stay right, use your lights, and pull well off if you stop. Never park in a turnout that a truck needs, and never block a gate. If a road is signed closed for active operations, it is closed — those closures exist because there is a machine around the corner that cannot see you.

Etiquette that costs nothing

Use pullouts. If you have traffic behind you on the river road, let it past. A wave when a driver pulls over for you costs nothing and goes a long way in a small place where everybody knows the trucks and often the drivers.

And keep in mind that this is work, not an inconvenience. A lot of families around here are attached to those loads, and the same is true of the fishing boats and the mill traffic. It is one of the things that separates a working town from a scenic one.

If you are heading up a forest road for firewood, wood cutting permits, gate closures and where cutting is allowed are managed by whoever owns the ground — check first. There is more on what actually burns well around here in firewood species, and the things worth carrying are in the truck kit. Chain, straps, gloves and fuel cans are on the outdoors shelf.
