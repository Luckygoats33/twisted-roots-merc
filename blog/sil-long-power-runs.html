Why the Power Goes Out Here and Stays Out Longer

The wire that reaches a rural house is long, single, and runs through timber — which explains almost everything about outages out this way.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

People move out here and cannot understand why town has power back in two hours and they are on hour thirty. It is not neglect and it is usually not a company being difficult. It is geometry.

The shape of a rural line

In a town, distribution is a network. Wire runs down streets in loops with switches, and if a section fails, crews can often reroute around it and restore most customers quickly while they fix the break.

Out here it is a tree, not a net. A single line leaves the substation, runs for miles along a road or through timber, and branches off into smaller and smaller taps until one wire arrives at your house. There is nowhere to reroute from. Every customer past the fault stays dark until that specific fault is fixed.

And that line runs through exactly the environment most likely to damage it: two hundred foot conifers on saturated ground in a gale. That is the whole story of a coastal outage.

Why it takes so long

  1. Finding it. A fault can be anywhere along miles of line, much of it not visible from a road. Crews patrol it, sometimes on foot.
  2. Getting to it. The break is frequently in a place you reach by driving a track that now has trees across it.
  3. Clearing the tree first. The line cannot be repaired until the timber on it is removed, and that is its own job with its own hazards.
  4. Multiple faults. In a real storm the same line has several breaks and each has to be found and fixed.
  5. Triage. Crews restore the biggest blocks of customers first, which is the right call and which means a line serving eleven houses is not first.
  6. Safety. Nobody goes up a pole in a gale. Repairs start when the wind drops, not when the outage starts.

None of that is unreasonable, and knowing it changes how you plan. You do not plan for a two-hour outage out here. You plan for a couple of days and are pleased when it is less.

What that means practically

  • Water. A well pump is electric, so no power means no water at all — not low pressure, none, past whatever is in the pressure tank. Fill containers and the bath before a forecast storm.
  • Heat. A gas furnace still needs house current for the blower and the controls. A woodstove does not, which is why so many houses here have one — a woodstove year.
  • Fridge and freezer. Leave them shut. A full freezer holds a long time; a half-empty one does not. Fill space with jugs of water and they hold better.
  • Septic pumps, if you have one, stop. Know whether yours needs power before you find out.
  • Phones and internet. The tower has hours of battery and your home equipment has less. Text rather than call as it degrades — dead zones.
  • Fuel and cash. Pumps and card readers both need power, in every town between here and the coast.

Generators, said carefully

A generator is the standard answer out here and it needs to be done right.

Run it outside, well away from doors, windows and vents, and never in a garage, a shed or under a porch — carbon monoxide from a generator kills people every winter and it does it fast and without warning. Have a working carbon monoxide alarm regardless.

Never plug a generator into a wall socket to feed the house. Back-feeding energises the line outside and can kill the crew working to restore you. If you want a generator to run circuits, get a transfer switch or an interlock installed by an electrician. That is not bureaucracy, it is the difference between a legal installation and a lethal one.

Run it a few times a year, keep fuel fresh and stabilised, and store fuel properly and outside. Stale fuel is the reason most generators do not start on the night they are needed — see the first blow of the season.

Downed lines

Assume every downed wire is live. Stay well back, keep others back, and report it. A line lying in water or across a wire fence energises a long way from where it fell. Do not attempt to move a branch off a line, ever, and do not drive over a wire in the road.

Report it, even if you are sure they know

Outage systems work from customer reports to locate faults. On a rural tap serving a handful of houses, your call may genuinely be the only signal that the branch is out. Have the number saved in your phone and written on the fridge, because you will be looking for it in the dark.

Lamps, batteries, jugs, fuel cans, cord and everything else is stocked on the storm shelf and the counts there come off the register. We stay open as long as we have power, and Carrie keeps a propane burner behind the counter so there is coffee either way.
