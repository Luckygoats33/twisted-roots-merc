Securing a Bin Against Raccoons

They have hands, they are patient, and they are smarter than the latch you bought — what actually holds and what only looks like it does.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The raccoon is the animal that will actually cost you money out here. Not the bear, not the cougar. A family of raccoons that has worked out your bin will visit every week for years, and they will teach their young to do it.

Understand what you are up against

Raccoons have genuinely dexterous front paws and they are problem solvers. They will lift, slide, unhook, pull and tip. They will work at a latch for a long time because there is nothing else they need to be doing. And once a bin has yielded food once, it is a permanent fixture on the route.

They are also strong enough in a group to tip a full bin, and clever enough to tip it against something so the lid opens on the way down.

What does not work

  • A brick on the lid. Charming. They push it off, or tip the bin and let gravity do it.
  • A bungee cord across the top. They unhook it. This is the most commonly recommended and most commonly defeated solution.
  • Simple flip latches. Anything a human can operate with one hand without thinking is within reach.
  • Ammonia, pepper, mothballs, radios, plastic owls. They work for about a week, if that.
  • Putting the bin out the night before. This is the single most common mistake. That is the entire window they need.

What actually works

  1. Keep the bin somewhere enclosed. Garage, shed, or a built enclosure with a proper door. This is the real answer and everything else is a compromise.
  2. Put it out in the morning, not the night before. Nothing else on this list matters as much.
  3. A carabiner or a locking latch that needs two distinct actions to open. Two-step is the threshold — one-step gets defeated.
  4. Strap the bin to a post or a rail so it cannot be tipped. A tipped bin defeats every lid mechanism.
  5. A ratchet strap around the whole bin, lid included, if you have no better option. Awkward for you, effective against them.
  6. Build a bin box. A simple frame with a hinged lid and a latch, or a three-sided enclosure with a gate. Lumber, hinges and a hasp, one afternoon.

Remove the reason

Securing the container is half of it. The other half is making the container less interesting.

  • Bag food waste separately and tie it.
  • Rinse the containers that held anything greasy or sweet.
  • Freeze the worst of it — bones, fish scraps, meat trimmings — and add it to the bin the morning it goes out.
  • Wash the bin out occasionally. The smell alone advertises across a considerable distance.
  • Keep pet food inside. Feed pets inside. An outdoor cat bowl is a raccoon feeding station.
  • Pick up fallen fruit, clean the barbecue, and take bird feeders down if you are getting visits.

The things they get into besides the bin

Chicken coops — they are one of the main reasons a coop needs proper latches and hardware cloth rather than chicken wire, which is covered in keeping chickens out here. Crawlspaces and attics, through a vent screen or a gap under a deck. Pet doors, which they will absolutely come through.

If something is living under your house, do not seal the entrance until you are certain nothing is inside, because a shut-in animal does a great deal more damage than one coming and going. Deal with it in the right season and get advice if there may be young.

The wider point

Everything on this page is the same principle that keeps bears out of a yard: no available food, no easy access, nothing to investigate. A property that is boring gets walked past. See bears, cougars and coyotes as neighbours for the rest of that argument.

And secure the bin before a storm for a completely different reason, which is that a loose wheelie bin in a gale is a projectile — the storm list.

Latches, hasps, carabiners, ratchet straps, hardware cloth and the lumber for an enclosure are all here, and the yard is around back if the enclosure is getting ambitious.
