How to Read a Tide Table Without Guessing

Four numbers a day, one negative sign that matters, and why the tide at the beach is not the tide up the river.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

If you moved here from somewhere without salt water, a tide table looks like a receipt. It is actually one of the more useful documents on this coast, and it takes about five minutes to understand properly.

What the numbers mean

A tide table gives you, for each day, roughly four events: two high tides and two low tides, each with a time and a height in feet. The height is measured against a reference level, which on this coast is about the average of the lower low tides. That is why some numbers are negative.

A negative low tide means the water is going out further than the usual low. Those are the tides that expose clam beds and tide pools and rocky reef that is normally underwater. A minus tide is the one you plan a clamming trip around, and the more negative it is, the better.

The two highs in a day are usually not the same height and neither are the two lows. That is normal.

The timing shifts

Tides run on the moon, not the clock, so everything moves later by roughly fifty minutes a day. A tide that was convenient this week is inconvenient the next. Do not remember a time; look it up each trip.

Twice a month, around the new and full moon, the sun and moon line up and you get spring tides — bigger highs and lower lows. Around the quarter moons you get neap tides, which are smaller and duller in both directions. Clammers and tide poolers want spring tides. People who want a calm afternoon on a dock do not care.

The bit that catches everyone: the river runs late

The time in the table is for a reference station, usually a point on the open coast or at a bay entrance. The tide does not arrive everywhere at once. It takes time to push up into a bay and further time to work up a tidal river, so the further upstream you are, the later high tide happens and the smaller the swing.

Up in the tidewater stretch of a coastal river, high tide can be well over an hour behind the table time. If you plan a tidewater fishing trip using the entrance time, you will show up at the wrong stage of water and wonder why nothing is happening. Most tide apps and tables publish corrections for secondary stations — use the one closest to where you will actually be.

What to plan around

If you are doing thisAim for
Digging clamsThe lowest minus tide you can get, arriving well before it
Tide poolsSame. Low and negative
Crabbing from a dockAround slack water at high, when current is least
Fishing tidewaterMoving water; many people favour the last of the incoming and the first of the outgoing
Walking a beach for agatesAn outgoing tide toward low, on freshly worked gravel
Anywhere below a bluff or headlandGoing out on a falling tide, back before it turns

The safety use, which is the real one

People get cut off. You walk around a point at low water, spend an hour on the other side, and come back to find the route underwater and the cliff behind you. It happens on this coast every year and it is entirely preventable with two minutes and a tide table.

Before you walk out anywhere below a headland, know what the tide is doing and when it turns. Give yourself a wide margin, because a rising tide plus swell is a lot faster than the number in the table suggests. And remember the table describes water level, not waves — see sneaker waves and logs for the part that kills people.

Wind and pressure push it around

The predicted heights assume ordinary weather. A strong onshore wind and a deep low pressure system stack water up against the coast and the real tide comes in higher than predicted, sometimes by a noticeable amount. That is how a big predicted high plus a storm becomes a problem — the subject of king tides.

Get a printed tide table and keep it in the truck. It works with no signal, which describes most of the places you will want it. Buckets, gloves, boots and a cooler are on the outdoors shelf for whatever you are going out to do with it.
