Reading Water: Where Fish Actually Are

Most water in a river holds nothing. Learning to spot the small percentage that does is the skill that changes everything.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Standing in the wrong place with perfect gear catches nothing. Standing in the right place with a rod off the rack catches fish. This is the least glamorous skill in fishing and the only one that really separates people.

What a fish wants

Three things, all the time: somewhere it does not have to work hard, something to eat coming past, and somewhere to hide from things that eat it. Any piece of water that gives all three holds fish. Water that gives one or none is scenery.

Current costs a fish energy. So it sits where the current is slow but food is being delivered by current that is fast. That is why nearly everything in reading water comes down to finding edges.

The edges worth knowing

  • Seams. Where fast water runs alongside slow water there is a visible line on the surface. Fish sit on the slow side of it and eat what comes down the fast side. This is the single most productive feature in any river.
  • Behind boulders. A rock breaks the current and leaves a pocket of soft water behind it, and often a cushion in front of it too. Small pockets hold fish out of all proportion to their size.
  • The tail of a riffle. Where broken water flattens out and drops into a pool. Oxygenated, full of dislodged food, and shallow enough that people walk straight past it.
  • The head of a pool. Where the riffle pours in. Deep, aerated, and the first place fish moving upstream stop.
  • The inside of a bend. The current cuts the outside deep and leaves the inside soft. In high water, fish move to the inside.
  • Undercut banks and log jams. Cover, shade and ambush. Awkward to fish, which is exactly why they still hold fish in August.

Colour and depth tell you the rest

You cannot see the bottom of a coastal river most of the year, so you read depth by colour and surface texture. Darker green or grey means deeper. A smooth, oily-looking slick over otherwise broken water usually means something deep underneath. Choppy, uniform, light-coloured water is shallow.

Learn one stretch at low summer water when you can actually see the bottom, and remember it. Every rock, ledge and drop-off you memorise in August is worth money in February when the same run is opaque and four feet higher.

How water level moves fish

The same run does not fish the same at different heights. As a river comes up, fish slide toward the banks, the inside of bends and the softer edges, because the middle becomes unlivable. As it drops, they slide back into the deeper, faster, more oxygenated slots and become harder to approach.

So a spot is not good or bad. It is good at a certain height. Keeping a mental note of what level each of your places fishes best at is what turns a person from someone who fishes into someone who catches.

A practical routine

  1. Stop at the top of the run and look at it before you put a line in. Two minutes of looking beats twenty of casting.
  2. Pick the three most likely features you can reach.
  3. Fish the closest one first, so you do not walk through it to reach the far one.
  4. Work through from the top down, or from the bottom up, but be systematic.
  5. If a feature has not produced in a reasonable number of casts, move. Confidence in a spot is not evidence.

The quiet part

Fish know you are there long before you think they do. Heavy footsteps on a gravel bar carry through water, and a shadow across a shallow tailout empties it. Approach from downstream where you can, stay low, and stop wading in further than you need to. Half the time the fish were where you are now standing.

None of this needs new gear. It needs walking, looking, and remembering. Rods, line and tackle are on the outdoors shelf if you are short of something, but the useful part is free.
