Rip Currents: How They Work and What to Do in One

The instinct that gets people killed in a rip is the obvious one, and knowing better takes about two minutes to learn.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

You do not have to be a swimmer for this to be worth knowing. Most people who end up in trouble in a rip on this coast were wading, or went in after a dog or a child. The knowledge is cheap and the alternative is not.

What a rip is

Waves push water up onto the beach. That water has to get back out, and it does not go back evenly. It finds the path of least resistance — a gap in a sandbar, the side of a jetty, a channel scoured in the sand — and returns seaward in a concentrated, fast-moving lane.

That lane is a rip current. It is not an undertow and it does not pull you under. It pulls you out, away from the beach, faster than you can swim against.

Spotting one

From the water they are almost invisible. From above — a bluff, a dune, a headland — they are often obvious. Look for:

  • A gap in the line of breaking waves, where the surf looks flatter or calmer than either side. Calm-looking water between breakers is usually the dangerous part, not the safe part.
  • A channel of choppier, darker water running seaward.
  • A plume of sand, foam or debris moving steadily out past the breakers.
  • Anywhere alongside a jetty, groin or rock outcrop. Structures make permanent rips.

Take thirty seconds from higher ground before you go down to the sand. It is the single most useful thing you can do.

What to do if you are in one

  1. Do not swim straight back at the beach. That is the instinct and it is what exhausts people. You are swimming against a current that is faster than you.
  2. Float. Get your breathing under control. Cold water makes people gasp and panic in the first minute. That minute is the dangerous one. Lie back, get air in, stop fighting.
  3. Swim parallel to the beach. Rips are narrow. Moving sideways gets you out of the lane, often in a short distance.
  4. Then come in at an angle, on the waves, once you are out of the moving water.
  5. If you cannot swim out of it, keep floating and signal. Wave an arm, shout. Rips weaken past the breakers and many will circulate you back toward the surf zone if you let them.

If somebody else is in one

Do not go in. This is the hardest instruction on this page and the most important one. The people who drown on this coast are very often the rescuers, not the person who was originally in trouble.

Instead: keep your eyes on them and do not look away, because a head in surf disappears in seconds and is very hard to reacquire. Call for help. Throw or push out anything that floats — a cooler, a boogie board, a jug. Shout the instruction: float, then swim sideways. Get somebody else to go for help so you can keep watching.

The cold water part

The ocean here does not warm up in the summer in any meaningful way. Water in the fifties takes the breath out of you on entry and takes coordination and grip strength away within minutes. Somebody who is a strong swimmer in a pool is a weak swimmer here.

That is why the advice is float first. Cold water shock passes in a minute or so if you let it, and people who fight through that minute are the ones who get in trouble.

The general rule for this coast

Treat the ocean here as something to look at rather than something to swim in, unless you have a wetsuit, company and a reason. There is nothing precious about that view; it is how most people who live here behave.

Sneaker waves and logs are the other half of the same conversation, over in beach safety. And if you are going down to watch weather rather than swim, read where not to stand first, and take a proper coat — they are on the outdoors shelf.
