Around Siletz

What Runs in the Siletz, Roughly by Season

A plain calendar of what moves through the river through the year, and why nobody should treat it as a schedule.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

People ask this at the counter more than almost anything else, usually while paying for coffee and looking at the weather out the window. What is in the river right now. The honest answer is that a river is not a train timetable, and anybody who gives you exact weeks is selling confidence they do not have.

What is true is that the year has a shape. The Siletz runs a long way from the Coast Range down to the bay at Taft, it has a gorge stretch and a slow tidewater stretch, and different fish use different parts of it at different times. Learn the shape and you will be right more often than not.

The shape of the year

Spring chinook come in first, and they are the most talked-about fish on this river. Summer brings a steelhead run the Siletz is genuinely known for, which is unusual on the Oregon coast and the reason people drive here from over the hill. Late summer into fall, the sea-run cutthroat move up out of the salt. Fall chinook follow the first real rain that raises the river. Coho are in there too. Then winter steelhead, which is cold, wet, patient work and the season most locals actually plan their year around.

Rain is the actual calendar

Nothing above happens on a date. It happens on water. Fall fish stack up in the bay and the lower river and wait, sometimes for weeks, until enough rain comes down to lift the river and give them a road. Then everything moves at once and the boat ramp looks like a parking lot. A dry autumn pushes the whole thing later. A wet one pulls it forward.

Same in reverse in winter. A river blown out brown and full of alder branches is not fishable no matter what the calendar says, and a river that has dropped and cleared two days after a storm is about as good as it gets. Locals do not watch the calendar. They watch the gauge and the sky, and they take the day off when the river says so rather than when the weekend says so.

Rules are not ours to state

Which of these you can keep, how many, where, with what gear, and whether the fish has to be a hatchery fish with a clipped fin — all of that is set by Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife, it varies by stretch of river, and it changes. You will notice this website does not print a single date or limit. That is deliberate. A stale regulation on a page nobody updates is worse than no page at all.

Check ODFW before you go. Check it again if it has been a while. And if you are fishing near the reservation or in tidewater, know that there are separate frameworks at work that are not ours to explain, so read rather than assume.

What to do with all that

If you are new here, pick one season and learn it properly instead of chasing all six badly. Winter steelhead is the traditional apprenticeship because there is a lot of it and the water teaches you. Sea-run cutthroat in late summer is the friendliest, because the fish are willing and the weather is not trying to kill you.

Either way, ask around. Eric will talk considerably longer than you intended about what is running, and he is usually within a week of right. Bait, line, hooks and the small stuff you forgot are on the outdoors shelf, and there is coffee going by six.
