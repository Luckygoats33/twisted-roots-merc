Around Siletz

Fishing the Siletz for Salmon Without Wasting a Season

How chinook and coho actually use this river, and the handful of things that separate a good day from a long wet one.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Salmon fishing on a coastal river is mostly a game of being in the right piece of water at the right stage of the tide with the right amount of rain behind you. The gear matters less than people think. The timing matters more than they want to hear.

Two different fisheries wearing the same name

Spring chinook and fall chinook behave differently and get fished differently, and lumping them together is why people come home empty.

Spring fish come in bright and early, before the river has any real push to it, and they hold in deep, cool water. That means tidewater and the slow deep holes. It is slow, methodical fishing. Anchored, or trolling a bait very slowly, and a lot of waiting. People who are good at it are good at sitting still.

Fall chinook are a rain fishery. They gather in the bay and the lower river and they wait for the first storm that lifts the water enough to move. Before that rain, you fish the bay and tidewater on the tide. After it, the whole run shifts upriver over a few days and you follow it. Coho come through the same door, generally a bit behind, and they tend to be more willing to chase something moving.

Tide is half of it in the lower river

Down in tidewater the river is not really a river. It is a long tidal slough that happens to have current, and the fish move with the water. The general rule people fish by is that the incoming brings fresh fish up and the top of the tide and the first of the outgoing is when they are most likely to bite. That is a rule of thumb, not a law, and every regular has a story about the exact opposite working.

What is not debatable is that fishing tidewater without knowing the tide is throwing away half your day. There is a longer piece on reading a tide table if you have never had to.

What actually gets fished here

  • Bait under a bobber. Cured eggs, sometimes with a sand shrimp, hung at the depth the fish are holding. Simple, deadly, and the way most fish on this river get caught.
  • Back-bouncing or drifting bait through a deep hole from a boat, letting it walk downstream along the bottom.
  • Trolling a spinner or a plug in tidewater and the bay, slowly, with the tide.
  • Spinners from the bank where there is a seam a fish has to swim past. Less common, but it works and it costs almost nothing to try.

Heavier line than you would use for trout, a rod with some backbone, and a net that is actually big enough. People lose more good fish to an undersized net than to bad knots.

The unglamorous part

A chinook is a large animal and it goes bad fast in a warm truck. Bleed it, get it on ice, and do it right away rather than at the end of the day. There is a whole piece on handling and keeping fish cold because it is the difference between good fish and freezer-burned regret.

Barbless hooks, gear restrictions, wild versus hatchery, how many and from which stretch of river: Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife sets all of it and it moves. Check the current regulations before every trip that matters. We are not going to print numbers here that might be wrong by the time you read them.

Being decent about it

Do not crowd somebody who got to a hole before you. Do not anchor in the middle of a drift other boats are running. If you are releasing a fish, keep it in the water, wet your hands, and let it go without a photo session. And be aware that this river matters to a lot of people for reasons that have nothing to do with your weekend, so behave accordingly.

Bait, line, tackle and a cooler are on the outdoors shelf, and ice is in the chest by the door.
