Slugs: The Actual Limiting Factor

In this climate slugs are not a pest to be managed occasionally — they are the primary reason seedlings fail, and they work year round.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Somewhere in the first season everybody here plants out a tray of seedlings, comes back the next morning, and finds bare stems. That is the introduction. It happens to everyone and it is not bad luck, it is the default outcome of putting tender green plants into a temperate rainforest without a plan.

Why it is so bad here

Slugs need moisture and mild temperatures and they get both, more or less permanently. There is no hard freeze to knock the population back and no dry spell long enough to matter in the growing months. They breed through much of the year, they lay eggs in the soil, and there are always more.

The big yellow-and-brown ones people notice are not the main problem. The main problem is the small grey and brown ones, in numbers, at night, plus their relatives underground going after roots and potatoes. And the banana slug, the big native one, is largely a decomposer rather than a garden villain — leave it alone.

The strategy that works

Nothing on its own is enough. What works is layering several things and being relentless about the vulnerable window, which is from germination to the point a plant has real leaves and some size to it.

Change the habitat

  • Clear the dense damp cover next to beds — long grass, boards on soil, piles of pots, thick mulch right up against stems.
  • Water in the morning rather than the evening, so the surface is drier at night when they feed.
  • Keep a clean edge around beds. A strip of bare, dry ground is a real barrier.
  • Do not mulch heavily around seedlings. Mulch later once plants can take a hit.

Change the plants

  • Plant out bigger. A seedling raised to a decent size in a pot survives what a direct-sown one does not. This is the single most effective change most people make.
  • Sow more than you need and accept losses.
  • Grow the things they mostly leave alone as well — alliums, tough herbs, fennel, and most aromatic foliage.
  • Protect individual plants with collars cut from bottles or cans until they are established.

Kill them

  • Iron phosphate bait. The pellets that are considered safe around pets and wildlife, unlike the older metaldehyde products, which are genuinely dangerous to dogs. Read the label, use it thinly, and reapply after heavy rain — which here means often.
  • Go out at night with a torch. Unglamorous, extremely effective, and the only method that deals with the ones that are actually eating your plants at that moment. Half an hour a few nights running around planting out makes a visible difference.
  • Traps. A board or a slate laid on the soil gives them somewhere to hide by day; turn it over in the morning and deal with what is under it. Beer traps work but they are fiddly and they need emptying.
  • Copper tape around pots and raised beds helps when the barrier is complete and clean. It is worth doing on a small scale and pointless if there is a leaf bridging it.
  • Ducks, if you are that far in. They are extremely good at this and they will also walk on everything.

What does not work

Coffee grounds, eggshells and most of the folk barriers do very little, and salt damages soil. Diatomaceous earth is useless the moment it gets wet, which in this climate is immediately. Do not spend a season on them.

Timing is everything

The critical windows are early spring, when everything is small and the ground is wet, and after any autumn rain returns. Bait and patrol hardest in those periods rather than spreading effort evenly.

And deal with them before you plant, not after. A few nights of hunting and a bait application in the week before planting out is worth far more than the same effort afterwards, because by then the damage has already happened.

The realistic goal

Not a slug-free garden. That is not available in a place like this. What is available is getting plants through the first few weeks with enough size to shrug off a bit of grazing, and that is genuinely the whole job.

Bait, copper tape, row cover, pots and seed trays are on the shelf — we open at six — and there is more about what to plant and when in the coastal garden and the growing season.
