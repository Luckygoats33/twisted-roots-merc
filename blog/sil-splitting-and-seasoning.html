Splitting and Seasoning Firewood in a Wet Climate

Drying wood in a place that rains for eight months is a matter of cover and airflow, and the year you start is the year that matters.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

In a dry climate you can leave firewood in a heap and it becomes firewood. Here it becomes a mushroom farm. Seasoning wood on this coast is entirely about controlling where water can and cannot reach it, and about starting a year earlier than feels necessary.

The one-year rule

Split and stacked under cover with air moving through it, alder will generally be ready inside a year and often sooner. Fir and maple want a full year. Anything that was standing dead and soaked, or anything left in rounds, takes longer.

The practical version of that: the wood you burn next winter should be split and stacked by the spring before. People who buy in October burn wet wood, complain about their stove, and then do it again. Get a year ahead once and you never go back.

Split it, do not leave it in rounds

Bark is a waterproof jacket. A round with bark all the way around it barely dries at all, and out here it will grow moss on the outside while staying wet in the middle. Splitting exposes end grain and face grain and that is where the water leaves from.

Split it smaller than you think. Smaller pieces dry faster and burn better, and a mix of sizes gives you something to build a fire with and something to hold it overnight.

Splitting without hurting yourself

  • A splitting maul or a splitting axe, not a felling axe. The wedge shape is what does the work.
  • A big low block to split on. Splitting on the ground is slow and wrecks the edge; a block that is too tall puts the round at a bad height.
  • Aim for the edge of the round, not the middle. Working in from the outside is far easier on big or knotty pieces.
  • Let the tool do it. Drop the head with a straight back, do not swing from the shoulders all day, and stop before you are tired. Almost every firewood injury happens when somebody is tired.
  • An old tyre or a ring on the block holds the round together so you are not bending down after every stroke. This is the single best trick in the business.
  • Eye protection. Chips come off fast and there is a lot of grit in bark.
  • A hydraulic splitter earns its money if you are doing several cords or dealing with knotty maple.

Stacking so it dries

  1. Off the ground. Pallets, rails, gravel — anything to break contact with wet soil. Wood on dirt wicks moisture straight back up.
  2. Cover the top only. A roof, a sheet of metal, a tarp weighted over the top few inches. Do not wrap the sides. A fully tarped pile is a sealed damp box and it will not dry.
  3. Single rows are better than a big block. Air has to get through, and a wide deep stack only dries at its edges.
  4. Leave gaps. Stack loose rather than tight, and leave space between rows.
  5. Somewhere with wind and what sun there is. A shaded spot against a north wall under trees is the worst place available and it is where most people put it.
  6. Bark side up on the top course, so it sheds.
  7. Ends stacked crosswise or against something, or the row falls over in the first storm.

Knowing when it is ready

Cracks radiating from the centre of the end grain. Grey, dull colour. Bark loose or falling off. Noticeably light in the hand. Two pieces knocked together give a sharp ring rather than a dull thud.

Or use a moisture meter on a freshly split face — the outside of a piece tells you nothing. Under twenty percent is the target; a good deal of what gets sold as seasoned around here is well above that.

Bringing it in

Keep a few days' worth inside near the stove so it comes up to temperature and sheds the last of the surface damp. Do not bring in a month's worth — you will bring in the beetles and the spiders with it, and stacked wood indoors in a warm room is where they wake up.

A rack, a canvas carrier and a doormat save a great deal of arguing about bark on the floor.

The wood shed argument

Everybody eventually builds one, and everybody wishes they had built it bigger. Three sides at most, open to the prevailing wind, roof with an overhang, floor off the ground. Two years of wood if you can manage it, so you always have a fully seasoned year to burn while this year's dries.
