Storm Watching, and Where Not to Stand

Watching a winter storm hit this coast is one of the better things about living here, and there is a right and a wrong place to do it from.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

People come to this coast in the winter specifically to watch the weather try to take it apart, which is either strange or entirely reasonable depending on how long you have lived here. It is worth doing. It is also the activity with the highest ratio of enjoyment to risk of anything on the coast, provided you get the geometry right.

What makes a good one

Three things, and they do not always arrive together.

  • Wind. A deep low offshore, the pressure dropping fast, and a sustained onshore blow. This is what makes the noise and moves the trees.
  • Swell. Big long-period swell, often generated by a storm that is still hundreds of miles away. Swell arrives before the wind does, which is why the sea can be enormous on a calm morning.
  • Tide. Big surf on a high tide runs much further up. Combine it with one of the winter's highest tides and you get the spectacular version. See king tides.

The best sessions are usually the front edge of a system, when the swell is already up and the wind is arriving, rather than the middle of it when you cannot see anything through the rain.

Where to watch from

The principle is simple: high ground, set back, with the weather in front of you and something solid behind you.

Headlands and viewpoints with real elevation are the classic option. A room with a window facing the water is the other, and it is genuinely the better one — warm, dry, and you can watch for hours rather than fifteen minutes. Half the coast economy in winter is built on this.

From a vehicle in a viewpoint parking area is fine as long as you are well back from the edge and not under trees. Trees are the thing people forget.

Where absolutely not to stand

  1. Jetties. Wet rock, no handholds, water coming over the top, and no way for anyone to reach you. The single worst place on this coast in a storm.
  2. Beaches, at the water line. A big set in a storm runs an extraordinary distance up. Sneaker waves are worse in these conditions, not better.
  3. On or beside drift logs. Storm water lifts them and turns them. This kills people. See beach safety.
  4. The edge of a bluff. The ground here is soft and undercut, and it goes without warning, particularly when it is saturated.
  5. Under big trees or power lines, in a car or out of it. Branches come down in gusts and so do lines.
  6. Low roads near tidewater, where a storm surge plus a high tide puts water and logs across the road.

The inland version

Up here away from the beach the show is different but it is still a show. What you get is the sound — a Coast Range hillside in a real gale is genuinely loud — and the sight of hundred-foot conifers moving in a way that they should not be able to.

The risk profile inland is trees and lines, and it is not small. Do not go for a walk in the woods in a windstorm. Do not park under alder. Widowmakers are branches that have already broken and are hung up waiting, and a gust brings them down long after you thought it was over. Give it a day.

Practical kit for going out

Waterproof everything, a hat that stays on, gloves, boots, and a headlamp because the power goes out and the viewpoint is dark. Keep the fuel tank above half from the first blow of the season onward. Cash, because card readers go down along with everything else.

And tell somebody where you went. Coverage on the coast highway is patchy and in the canyons it is worse — the dead zone problem.

Afterwards is the good part

The day after a blow is when the beaches are worth walking. Fresh gravel, glass floats occasionally, and the best agate hunting of the year — see looking for agates. The wind has usually dropped, the light comes back, and the whole coast looks scrubbed.
