Around Siletz

Learning the Woods: Fir, Spruce, Alder, Salal and Salmonberry

Five plants that make up most of what you are looking at out here, and what each one tells you about the ground it is standing on.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A coastal forest looks like an undifferentiated green wall until you can name about five things in it. Once you can, it stops being scenery and starts being information — about soil, about water, about what happened on that ground in the last century.

The conifers

Douglas fir

The one everything is built out of. Tall, straight, thick furrowed bark on an old one, and needles arranged all around the twig, soft to the hand. The cones are the giveaway: small, with a three-pointed bract sticking out between each scale that everybody is taught looks like a mouse's back legs and tail.

It wants reasonably drained ground and it dominates most of the managed forest around here. It is also the wood you will meet as lumber, as firewood and as the thing that fell across the road.

Sitka spruce

The coastal specialist. It thrives in the salt air and fog belt near the ocean, less so as you go inland. The way to know it without looking at anything else is to grab a branch: spruce needles are stiff and genuinely sharp, and they come off a little peg on the twig. Fir needles are soft. Grab once and you will never confuse them again.

Bark is scaly and greyish. On the headlands they grow into wind-shaped, contorted things, which is more or less where this store got its name.

Western red cedar and hemlock, briefly

Cedar has flat, scale-like sprays of foliage, stringy bark, and a smell you know immediately. It likes wet ground and it rots slowly, which is why it ends up as fence posts, shakes and decking.

Western hemlock has short flat needles of uneven lengths and a drooping top — the leader nods over, and from a distance a stand of hemlock has a soft, bowing skyline where fir stands stiff. It comes up thickly in shade, including on rotting logs.

The alder and the understory

Red alder

The deciduous one, with smooth pale grey bark that is usually mottled with lichen, and leaves with a toothed, slightly rolled-under edge. It is the tree that colonises anything disturbed — road cuts, slides, old skid roads, stream banks, clearcuts.

Alder fixes nitrogen, so it improves the ground it grows on and gets replaced by conifers over time. It is short-lived, it drops limbs readily in wind, and it is the best firewood most people here will get their hands on. Where you see a solid stand of alder, something happened to that ground within living memory, and there is probably water in it.

Salal

The waist-high evergreen shrub with thick, leathery, oval leaves that is everywhere under the trees and along every road cut. Small urn-shaped pink-white flowers, then dark purple berries that are edible and taste better than they look.

It is tough as anything, it is the reason walking off-trail here is hard work, and it is cut commercially for the floral trade. If you want something that survives a coastal yard with no attention, it is already growing next to you.

Salmonberry

Tall, arching canes in the wet spots — ditches, creek banks, the shady edges. Bright magenta-pink flowers early in the season, and then soft berries somewhere between orange and deep red, ripening well before blackberries.

They vary enormously in flavour from bush to bush, from watery to genuinely good. Where salmonberry is thick, the ground is wet, and it is a reliable indicator when you are working out where to put a fence, a shed or a driveway.

Reading the whole picture

What you seeWhat it usually means
Even-aged fir, all one sizePlanted after a harvest
Solid alderDisturbed ground, often wet, within a few decades
Cedar and skunk cabbage togetherWet ground most of the year
Spruce dominatingClose to the ocean, in the fog belt
Big old stumps with springboard notchesCut a very long time ago, by hand
Salmonberry thicketsSeep, ditch or stream nearby

Learn those and a drive up the river stops being green and starts being readable. If you are heading out to pick anything you find out there, read the foraging piece first, because the caution in it is not decorative. And what all this timber means once it is cut and stacked is in firewood species.
