Ticks: Checking, Removing and Not Being Dramatic About It

They are here, mostly in brush and grass, and the whole management strategy is a habit that takes two minutes.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Ticks in western Oregon are not the plague they are in some parts of the country, and they are also not nothing. The sensible position is somewhere between panic and ignoring them, and it costs about two minutes a day in the seasons when you are out in brush.

Where you pick them up

Not from trees. Ticks do not drop out of anything. They sit on the tips of grass and low brush with their front legs out and wait for something to walk past — the technical term is questing — and they grab on as you brush by.

So the risk is: tall grass, brushy edges, trail margins, salal and fern along a path, and old fields. Walking down the middle of a wide trail is low risk. Pushing through brush after a mushroom or a berry patch is not.

They are most active in the cooler damp parts of the year here rather than the height of summer, which surprises people who expect an August problem. Spring is a busy period. That said, on this coast conditions are mild enough that they are around in some form across a lot of the year, so the habit should not be seasonal.

Keeping them off

  • Long trousers tucked into socks. Looks ridiculous, works better than anything else on this list.
  • Light-coloured clothing, so you can see them on you before they find skin.
  • Repellent with DEET on skin, or a permethrin treatment on clothing and boots, which stays effective through washes and is the more thorough option for anybody working in brush regularly.
  • Stay on the trail where the option exists.
  • Check dogs too, and talk to a vet about preventatives. Dogs carry them into the house, which is how people end up with one in a bed.

The check

Do it the same day, every time you have been in brush. Ticks wander for a while before attaching, so an immediate check catches many of them loose.

Look in the places they favour: behind and around the ears, along the hairline, back of the neck, armpits, waistband, groin, behind the knees. Anywhere clothing is tight or skin is soft. Get somebody else to check your back and scalp. A shower helps, both because it washes off unattached ticks and because you are already looking.

Clothes into a hot dryer for a good stretch kills any that came home on them. Washing alone does not reliably do it; heat does.

Removing one properly

  1. Fine-tipped tweezers. Not fingers, not a hot match, not petroleum jelly, not nail polish. Those methods are folklore and several of them make the tick worse to deal with.
  2. Grip as close to the skin as you can get, on the tick's head, not the body.
  3. Pull straight out, steadily. Do not twist and do not jerk. Do not squeeze the body.
  4. If a bit of mouthpart stays behind, leave it or remove it as you would a splinter. It is not carrying anything.
  5. Clean the bite and your hands.
  6. Note the date somewhere and keep an eye on it. If you want to be thorough, keep the tick in a sealed bag.

When to call a doctor

If you develop an expanding rash around the bite, particularly a ring-shaped one, or a fever, aches, headache or unusual fatigue in the weeks after a bite, go and be seen and mention the tick. Tick-borne illness in this region is uncommon but it exists, and the treatable version of it is treatable early.

Do not spend an evening reading the worst material on the internet. Do tell a doctor there was a tick, because it changes what they are looking for.

The proportionate view

Most people out here spend their lives in this brush and deal with the occasional tick as a minor irritation. The habit — tuck, check, remove properly — is what keeps it minor. Skipping the habit for a season is how something small turns into a story.

Tweezers, repellent, a first aid kit and long socks are all on the outdoors shelf, and there is more about going into the brush safely in the foraging piece.
