Around Siletz

What to Keep in the Truck Out Here

A short list that covers most of the bad afternoons: no signal, no shoulder, no traffic and a long way from anywhere.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The reason to carry things in a vehicle here is not disaster preparedness. It is that the ordinary failures — a flat, a dead battery, a tree across the road, a wrong turn onto a spur road — happen in places with no signal, no traffic and no shoulder, and they happen in the rain.

This list is not long. Most of it fits in one tote behind the seat.

The things you will actually use

  • Jumper cables or a jump pack. A lithium jump pack is better because it does not need a second vehicle, which is the entire problem on a quiet road.
  • A working spare, a jack and a lug wrench — and check the spare has air in it now rather than finding out later. This is the most commonly neglected item on any vehicle.
  • Tow strap. Rated, with no hooks flying about. Getting somebody out of a ditch is a routine favour around here.
  • A folding shovel. Mud, gravel, snow, and clearing a drain that is putting water across a driveway.
  • Headlamp plus a spare set of batteries. Hands free, because everything you will be doing needs two.
  • Rain gear and rubber boots. You will be outside in it, and doing a wheel change soaked is how a small problem becomes a miserable one.
  • Gloves. Work gloves, and a warm pair.
  • A blanket. Wool or a decent synthetic, kept dry in a bag.
  • Water and something to eat that survives a hot summer and a cold winter in a vehicle.
  • First aid kit, with more gauze than the cheap kits include.
  • A knife or multi-tool.
  • Duct tape and zip ties. Between them they will get almost anything home.
  • Paper map of the county. Not a joke. Navigation apps fail in the canyons and forest roads are not reliably mapped.

The ones specific to here

A small saw or a folding bow saw, because a branch across a one-lane road is a five-minute job with a saw and a two-hour wait without one. A chainsaw if you own one and know how to use it around a loaded tree, which is a real skill and not one to learn on the day.

Hi-vis of some kind. Standing on the edge of a dark narrow road in a grey coat is a genuinely dangerous thing to do.

Cash. Card readers go down with the power, and they go down often enough that the twenty in the glovebox earns its keep.

Fuel discipline

From the first blow of the season to spring, treat half a tank as empty. Pumps need electricity, a closed road can double a route with no warning, and there is nothing between here and the far side of the range. This one habit prevents more trouble than everything else on this page put together.

Seasonal additions

The habit that matters most

Tell somebody where you are going and roughly when you will be back, particularly for anything on forest roads or over the hill at night. Coverage is patchy to non-existent in a lot of the places people drive here, and a person who knows your route is worth more than any piece of equipment — see dead zones.

Check the tote twice a year. Batteries die, water goes stale, the tape turns to glue and the spare loses air quietly. Do it when the clocks change and you will never think about it again.

Most of this list is on the shelf here — cord, tape, straps, lights, batteries, gloves, boots and fuel cans. The winter version is arranged on the storm page, and the field gear is on the hunt wall.
