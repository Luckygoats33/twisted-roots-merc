Around Siletz

Why It Rains the Way It Does Here

Not more storms than everywhere else — just a mountain range in the way of an ocean, doing the same thing over and over all winter.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The rain here has a character to it that surprises people. It is not violent. There is very little thunder, almost no hail worth mentioning, and days of the sort of downpour that makes national news are rare. What there is instead is persistence — a grey ceiling and steady water for days at a time, and then more.

The machinery

Two things combine.

First, the Pacific. In the cool half of the year the jet stream drops south and starts steering low pressure systems straight into this coast, one after another, sometimes lined up for a week. Each one drags a long tongue of moist air ahead of it. When those tongues get organised and narrow they are what forecasters call atmospheric rivers, and that is where the very wet stretches come from.

Second, the Coast Range. Air arriving off the water has to climb it. Rising air cools, cool air cannot hold as much water vapour, and the water comes out. That is orographic lift, and it is why the west-facing slopes of these hills are among the wetter places in the country while the valley on the other side is comparatively dry.

So the storms are not unusual. The terrain wrings them out here.

Why it is so steady

A thunderstorm is a small violent thing that dumps a lot of water in fifteen minutes and moves on. What we get is a broad frontal system, hundreds of miles across, moving through slowly. The rainfall rate is low. The duration is enormous. Total up a week of that and it is a startling amount of water delivered so gently you can walk around in it in a decent jacket.

This is also why umbrellas are not really a coastal item. It is not falling straight down often enough for one to help, and the wind takes them. A hood is the local answer.

The seasonal split is extreme

What surprises people more than the total is how uneven it is. The cool months carry the overwhelming majority of the year's rain. Late summer here can be genuinely dry for weeks — dry enough that gardens need watering, wells get low, creeks drop and the fire danger is real.

So people arrive in August, decide the reputation is overblown, and then meet October. And people arrive in February, decide it is unlivable, and then meet a clear cool summer with no humidity and no mosquitoes worth mentioning.

What that pattern does to everything else

  • Ground stays saturated through the wet months, which is why slides happen and why you cannot drive on a wet field. See landslides and slumps.
  • Rivers respond fast. Steep drainages plus saturated soil means the river comes up hard after a storm and drops quickly after. Which is the entire basis of winter fishing.
  • Everything grows on everything. Moss on the roof, algae on the deck, mildew in a closet. Not a maintenance failure, a climate. See mud, moss and mildew.
  • Nothing dries outdoors for months. Firewood has to be under cover and started a year ahead — splitting and seasoning.

The upside nobody mentions

It is why the trees are the size they are. A temperate rainforest is a rare thing globally and this is one, and a Douglas fir two hundred feet tall exists because of exactly the weather people complain about. The moss on the alder, the ferns growing out of stumps, the fact that a cut bank is green again in one season — all the same account.

It is also why it is green in February when most of the country is brown, and why the water in the taps and the river is generally not something anybody has to worry about.

Living with it

The people who do well here stop treating rain as an event to be waited out. You get a proper coat and proper boots, you accept that the firewood goes under cover, you run a dehumidifier or crack a window, and you go outside anyway. The alternative is staying indoors from October to May, and people who choose that generally do not stay long.
