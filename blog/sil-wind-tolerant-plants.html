Planting Something That Will Stand Up to the Wind

Salt, gales and saturated ground rule out a great deal — what survives on an exposed coastal property and how to give it a chance.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Wind is the reason nothing out here grows straight, and it is the reason half of what people plant in a coastal yard dies in its second winter. It is not usually the cold that kills. It is constant mechanical stress, salt in the air, and a root ball rocking in wet soil until the roots break.

What wind actually does to a plant

Three separate injuries. It dries foliage out faster than roots can replace the water, which is why leaves brown on the windward side. It carries salt inland and burns anything sensitive. And it rocks the stem, which opens a socket around the root ball that fills with water and rots.

Inland here, up the valley, salt is much less of a factor than it is on the headlands, but wind and saturated ground still are. Everything below applies in some measure, and closer to the ocean it applies harder.

Plants that hold up

  • Shore pine. The classic coastal survivor. Sculpted by wind and content to be.
  • Sitka spruce. Native to exactly these conditions and thoroughly at home in salt air.
  • Western red cedar in ground that holds water.
  • Salal. Already growing on your road cut. Tough, evergreen, shade tolerant, indifferent to weather.
  • Evergreen huckleberry. Handsome, well behaved, and it fruits — the berry piece.
  • Wax myrtle. A genuine coastal workhorse for a screen or a hedge.
  • Escallonia. Widely used along this coast as a hedge for good reason.
  • Hebe, olearia and other tough evergreens from similarly windy maritime places.
  • Rugosa rose. Salt tolerant, thorny, and it will hold a bank.
  • Ornamental grasses. They bend and they are meant to.
  • Hydrangea and fuchsia, which do very well in coastal gardens with a bit of shelter.

Things that will not enjoy it

Anything with large soft leaves in an exposed position. Most Mediterranean plants, which want sharp drainage and dry air and get neither. Tall thin conifers in a windy spot with wet soil, which is a blowdown waiting to happen. Espaliered anything on an exposed wall. Big-leaved tropical-looking plants that look wonderful in a sheltered courtyard and shred in a week out in the open.

Filter the wind, do not block it

This is the single most useful principle and it is counterintuitive. A solid fence or wall does not calm the wind behind it — it makes turbulence, and the eddies on the lee side can be worse than the open exposure.

What works is a permeable barrier that slows the air through it: a mixed hedge, a shelterbelt of tough shrubs and small trees, or a slatted fence with gaps. A well-designed windbreak gives useful shelter for a distance of several times its height downwind, which is a large area for a modest planting.

Plant the windbreak in layers — tough stuff on the outside taking the beating, softer things behind it. And plant it before you plant the things it is meant to protect, because it needs a head start.

Getting new plants established

  1. Plant small. A small plant establishes faster and roots into the site rather than sitting in a pot-shaped hole rocking. Large specimens in windy sites fail far more often than small ones.
  2. Plant in autumn so roots have the whole wet season to establish before the dry weeks.
  3. Stake low and loose, if at all. A stake at the bottom third lets the stem flex and strengthen. A rigid tall stake produces a weak plant that falls over the day you remove it.
  4. Firm it in properly and check it after every gale in the first year. Re-firm anything that has rocked open.
  5. Mulch, but not against the stem.
  6. Temporary shelter helps. Windbreak netting on a few posts around a new hedge for the first couple of seasons is not cheating, it is how coastal hedges get established.

Drainage again

Half of what people call wind damage out here is really root death from waterlogged soil plus a bit of wind finishing the job. Plant on a mound or a raised area if your ground holds water in winter, and choose things that tolerate wet feet if you cannot fix the drainage. There is more of this in the coastal garden.

The long view

A shelterbelt is the highest-value planting anybody makes on an exposed property here. It takes years and then it quietly makes everything inside it possible — a garden, a sitting spot, a place the washing survives. Everybody who has one planted it a long time ago and everybody who does not wishes they had started.
