Around Siletz

Wind Is the Weather That Actually Causes Trouble Here

Rain is a nuisance and wind is the thing that takes the power out, blocks the road and puts a tree through a roof.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

People move here braced for the rain and get surprised by the wind. Rain is constant and mostly harmless. Wind is occasional and is responsible for essentially every bad day this coast has in the winter.

Where it comes from

Winter wind here is the circulation around low pressure systems moving in off the Pacific. The deeper the low and the faster the pressure falls, the harder it blows. The strongest gusts usually come with or just behind the front, and they come from the south or southwest first, then swing around to the west or northwest as the system passes.

That direction change matters more than people realise. A tree that has stood in the prevailing southwest wind its whole life has grown roots braced for it. When the wind comes around from a new quarter, or when it comes from the usual quarter but through a gap that was recently logged, trees fall that had never moved before.

Why it does so much damage in a forest

Because we live under two hundred foot trees in ground that is saturated. Soil that has taken weeks of rain has no grip left in it. Add a big lever arm and a gale and root plates lift out whole.

So a windstorm here means:

  • Trees across roads, sometimes several on one stretch, sometimes for hours.
  • Trees across power lines, which is why the power goes out and why it stays out — the crews have to clear the tree before they can even look at the line.
  • Branches down everywhere, including hung-up ones that come down later.
  • Anything not fastened down leaving: bins, tarps, panels, trampolines, patio furniture, unsecured metal roofing.

Before it arrives

  1. Put away or tie down everything loose in the yard, including the bin. A wheelie bin at speed does real damage — and see the bin piece for the other reason to secure it.
  2. Fuel the truck and the generator can. Pumps do not work without power.
  3. Charge everything, and have a light per person. Headlamps beat flashlights because you need your hands.
  4. Fill water containers if you are on a well, because a well pump is electric and there is no reserve beyond the pressure tank.
  5. Get cash. Card readers go down along with everything else.
  6. Park away from trees and lines if you can. This costs nothing and saves vehicles.

The full version of that list lives on the storm page, and the shelf inside the door is stocked to match.

During

Stay out of the woods. Do not go for a walk to look at it. Do not park under alder, which is the tree most likely to shed limbs. Keep away from windows on the exposed side. If a line is down, assume it is live and stay well back, and that includes lines lying in water or across a fence, because a fence will carry it a long way.

If you are driving and a tree is across the road, do not try to get past it on the shoulder. The shoulder is where the next one lands.

After

The day after is when people get hurt. Widowmakers — broken branches hung up in the canopy — come down with no wind at all, often while somebody is standing underneath cleaning up. Look up before you start working. If you are cutting a fallen tree, understand which way it is loaded before you put a saw in it; a trunk under tension will move fast and it does not care what is in the way.

And be patient about power. A rural line that runs miles through timber to serve a handful of houses is a long walk for a crew and it is not going to be first on the list. That is the trade for living out here — see long single-line power runs.

The first one gets everybody

Every year the first real blow catches people who did all of this last winter and did not get around to it this time. There is a piece specifically on that — the first big blow of the season — because the pattern is remarkably consistent.
