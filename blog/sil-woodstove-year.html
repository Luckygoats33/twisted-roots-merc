Around Siletz

A Woodstove Year

Running a stove properly is a twelve-month job with about four months of actual fire in it, and the other eight are what make it work.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A woodstove out here is not a romantic accessory. It is the thing that keeps a house warm when a tree takes the power out for two days, and in a lot of places along this valley it is the primary heat rather than the backup. Run properly it is cheap, dependable and pleasant. Run badly it is smoky, expensive and a genuine fire risk.

The year, in order

Spring

Buy next winter's wood now. This is the whole secret and it is the step people skip. Wood bought in the spring, split and stacked under cover, is ready by autumn. Wood bought in October is wet and will be wet all winter — see splitting and seasoning.

Let the stove go cold, clean it out properly, and shovel the ash. Leave a small amount in the firebox if your manual suggests it, and take the rest to the garden if it was clean wood, or the bin if it was not.

Summer

The stove is cold, which is when everything gets inspected.

  • Get the chimney swept and inspected. Annually, by somebody who does it properly.
  • Check the door gasket — close the door on a piece of paper and try to pull it out. If it slides freely, the gasket is done and it is a cheap fix.
  • Look at the firebricks and the baffle. Both are consumables and both are much easier to replace in July.
  • Check the glass gasket and the latch.
  • Look at the chimney cap and the spark arrestor for blockage or corrosion, and check the flashing on the roof while you are up there.
  • Check the hearth pad and clearances, especially if furniture has migrated over the years.

Also: split and stack. Wood dries in the dry months, and this is the only stretch of the year the weather is on your side.

Autumn

Bring the first month's wood under cover close to the house. Test the smoke alarms and the carbon monoxide alarm, and put fresh batteries in both — carbon monoxide is not optional in a house with combustion appliances. Break in a new stove or new firebricks with a few small fires rather than a roaring one. Get kindling and fire starter in before you need them at nine at night in the rain.

Winter

Burn it properly. Which is the next section.

Burning it properly

  1. Dry wood only. Under twenty percent moisture on a freshly split face. Everything else follows from this.
  2. Build fires top-down. Big pieces at the bottom, smaller above, kindling and starter on top. It lights cleanly, produces far less smoke, and does not need poking.
  3. Get it hot fast, then settle it. A hot, bright fire with the air open at the start establishes draught and warms the flue. Damping a cold fire down is what produces creosote.
  4. Do not smoulder it overnight. Choking a stove down to make wood last is the main cause of chimney deposits and it gives you less heat, not more.
  5. Watch the chimney from outside. Once it is going, you should see almost nothing. Visible smoke means you are burning wet wood or starving it of air.
  6. A stove thermometer is worth ten dollars of anybody's money. It tells you when you are too cool, which is dirty, and too hot, which damages the stove.
  7. Ash bed. An inch or so of ash insulates the base and helps the fire. More than that chokes the air inlet.

Safety, plainly

Ash goes into a metal container with a lid, on a non-combustible surface, outside, and stays there for days. Ash holds live coals far longer than anybody believes, and ash in a cardboard box or a plastic bin on a wooden deck is one of the most common causes of house fires in country like this.

Keep a working carbon monoxide alarm. Keep the area around the stove clear — no drying laundry on it, no kindling stacked against it. Have an extinguisher in the kitchen and know where the second exit is.

If you have a chimney fire: get everybody out, call it in, and do not try to be clever. They are far more common with wet wood and unswept flues, which is why the summer list above exists.

The outage case

The stove is why a power cut here is an inconvenience rather than an emergency. Keep a way to boil water on it if it takes a kettle, a supply of dry wood inside, and matches somewhere that is not damp. A house with a stove and a week of dry wood is a house that can wait out anything the weather here does.
