Storm Ready

Battery Types, Shelf Life, and Why the Ones in the Drawer Are Dead

Alkaline, lithium and rechargeable each fail differently, and one of them fails inside your flashlight and ruins it.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The most common outage problem we hear about at the counter is not that somebody had no flashlight. It is that they had a flashlight and the batteries in it were dead or, worse, had leaked and welded themselves into the tube.

The three kinds and where each belongs

Alkaline. The default AA and AAA. Cheap, available everywhere, roughly a ten-year shelf life sitting unused in the package. Their failure mode is the problem: as they discharge and age they can vent potassium hydroxide, the white crusty stuff, which destroys the contacts in whatever device they are in. Never leave alkalines in a device that sits unused.

Lithium primary. The non-rechargeable lithium AA and AAA. Two to three times the price, and worth it in two specific places. They hold charge for fifteen to twenty years, they do not leak, and they keep working in the cold when alkalines go weak. This is what belongs in the emergency light that lives in the kit, the one in the truck, and anything you will not touch for a year.

Rechargeable. Low self-discharge nickel-metal hydride cells hold most of their charge for a year on the shelf, which the older rechargeables did not. Excellent for things you use constantly. Not a good bet as your only emergency supply, because they need a charger and the charger needs power.

Standardize the house

The mistake is owning a AA lantern, a AAA headlamp, a D-cell flashlight, a CR2032 thermometer and a 9-volt smoke alarm, and then trying to keep spares of all five. Pick one size for as much as you can — AA is the sensible choice, it is in everything and it is the one we stack deepest when the wall goes to storm levels — and let the exceptions be exceptions.

Then buy spares by the box, not the blister pack, and store them together in one place.

Storing them so they last

  1. Keep them in the original packaging until use. Loose cells rolling in a drawer can short against each other and against keys, coins and screws.
  2. Room temperature. Cold is fine and the fridge myth is harmless but pointless. Heat is what kills them, so not the attic and not on top of the water heater.
  3. Do not mix. Not old with new, not brands, not chemistries. Mixed cells discharge unevenly and the weak one gets reverse-charged by the strong ones, which is exactly what causes leaks.
  4. Pull the batteries out of anything seasonal before you put it away. The camp lantern, the trail camera, the kids' toys.
  5. Write the season on the package with a marker when you buy it, so rotation is obvious without a spreadsheet.

If one has already leaked

Wear gloves and eye protection. The residue is caustic. Scrub the contacts with a cotton swab dipped in white vinegar or lemon juice, which neutralizes it, then dry thoroughly and burnish the contacts with a pencil eraser or fine sandpaper until they are shiny. If a spring has corroded through, the device is done.

The twice-a-year job

When you check the smoke alarms, check the battery stash. Pull the alkalines out of anything that has been sitting, put lithium primaries in the emergency lights, top off the rechargeables and the power banks, and note what is thin.

AA, AAA and D by the pack and by the case are at the front counter, and they get restocked hardest when the forecast turns — see what is on the wall. The lights they go in are covered in flashlights, headlamps and lanterns.
