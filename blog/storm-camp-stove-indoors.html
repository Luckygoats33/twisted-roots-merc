When a Camp Stove Can Come Inside, and When It Absolutely Cannot

There is a real line between a butane burner and a two-burner propane camp stove, and it is not about being careful.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Every outage, somebody sets a two-burner camp stove on the kitchen counter and cooks dinner on it, and nothing happens, and they tell everybody it is fine. Usually it is. The problem is that the failure mode is not a small one, and the difference between the appliance that is safe indoors and the one that is not comes down to how it is designed, not how careful the cook is.

The actual distinction

All combustion produces carbon monoxide. A well-designed, well-adjusted burner at proper pressure with enough air produces very little. A burner running rich, or starved of oxygen, or with a soot-blocked port, produces a lot.

Appliances certified for indoor use are built with that in mind: regulated pressure, burner geometry designed for complete combustion, and often an oxygen depletion sensor that shuts the gas off if the room's oxygen drops. Outdoor appliances are built assuming there is an infinite supply of air and nothing above them, because there is a sky.

The other half is heat. A camp stove is designed to sit on gravel, not four inches under a range hood, a cabinet, or a curtain.

If you are using the indoor-rated one

  1. Crack a window in the room. Not a token inch on the far side of the house — the room you are cooking in.
  2. Have a battery CO alarm in that room and know it is working.
  3. Hard, level, non-combustible surface. Not a plastic table, not a countertop overhang the stove can tip off.
  4. Nothing above it. Not a cabinet, not a range hood, not a shelf. Heat rises and cabinets scorch long before they burn.
  5. Use it for cooking only, for as long as cooking takes. Never as a heater and never overnight.
  6. Never leave it lit and walk away.
  7. Watch the flame. It should be blue and steady. Yellow, lazy, sooty flames mean incomplete combustion, which means CO — shut it off and take it outside.

The outdoor-under-cover compromise

The practical answer for most families is cooking outside, under cover, on a covered porch or in a carport with genuinely open sides, and carrying the pot in. It is annoying for four minutes and then it is fine.

What makes an outdoor space acceptable is open sides that let wind through, not a roof. A carport with three walls is a garage. A porch with a roof and one wall is outside. If you would not feel a breeze standing there, do not cook there.

Fuel storage, which people get wrong

Never store propane cylinders indoors, including the 1 lb green ones and including in the garage or a crawlspace. Propane is heavier than air, so a slow leak pools at floor level and finds a pilot light. Store them outside, upright, in the shade, off the ground.

Disconnect the cylinder from the stove when you are done. The valve on a camp stove is not a long-term seal, and a stove left connected in a shed for six months is often an empty stove come winter.

Butane canisters are less of a hazard and can live in a shed. They also do not work well below about forty degrees, which on this coast in January is a real consideration.
