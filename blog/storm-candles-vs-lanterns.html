Candles Versus Battery Lanterns, and When a Candle Is Actually Fine

House fires spike during outages and candles are the reason, but there is a sensible way to use them and it is not on a bookshelf.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Candles are romantic for about forty minutes and then they are a chore and a hazard. Fire departments see a jump in house fires during long outages, and candles are consistently at the top of the list. Almost always the same story: a candle on a soft or cluttered surface, left burning, in a house where everybody has gone to bed exhausted.

Why battery light wins on the merits

It is not just safety. A candle puts out roughly the light of a ten-watt bulb, and it puts it out at eye level with a flicker that makes reading tiring. A modest LED lantern will light a whole room for a hundred hours on one set of cells. The lantern is brighter, safer, cheaper per hour, and it does not need watching.

Battery light also does not consume oxygen or produce combustion products, which in a shut-up house over several days is not nothing.

Where a candle still earns its place

Two situations, honestly.

Backup to the backup. When the batteries genuinely run out on day six, a box of candles in the cupboard is a real resource. A good beeswax pillar burns for many hours and does not depend on any supply chain.

The morale one. A single candle on the kitchen table while you eat, watched, with everyone in the room, is a fine thing on the third dark evening. That is a different use than lighting a hallway all night.

If you are going to burn one, do it properly

  1. Hard, non-combustible surface. A plate, a saucer, a jar lid. Not a bookshelf, not a windowsill with a curtain, not the arm of a chair.
  2. Inside something. A glass hurricane, a canning jar, an empty coffee can. Containment turns a knocked-over candle into a mess instead of a fire.
  3. Three feet of clear space in every direction, including above. Heat rises and shelves char.
  4. Never in a room with nobody in it. Not for a minute, not while you go check the generator.
  5. Out before bed. Every one, every night, no exceptions, and check twice because in a dark house it is easy to miss one.
  6. Nowhere a pet or a child can reach. Cats and candles is a bad combination and it is always the tail.
  7. Snuff, do not blow. Blowing scatters hot wax and can throw an ember.

Never around fuel

If there is any chance of a gas leak — you smell gas, the storm knocked something around, a line got damaged, a propane tank got hit by a limb — no open flame of any kind. No candle, no lighter, no match, and do not flip a light switch either. Get out and call it in from down the road.

Same goes for anywhere you are storing or pouring gasoline for a generator. Vapors travel along the ground further than people expect.

The heat myth

You will read that a few tea lights under a clay pot will heat a room. They will not. The arithmetic does not work — a candle produces about eighty watts of heat, which is less than a person sitting still, and the pot just redistributes it. It will, however, produce carbon monoxide and it will burn somebody. Heating a house without power is a real problem with real solutions, covered in heating a house safely when the power is out.

Lanterns and batteries are on the storm wall. The beeswax candles are on the local shelf, poured up the valley, and they are genuinely good ones — better as a gift than as a lighting plan.
