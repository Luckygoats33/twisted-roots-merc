Storm Ready

What Should Actually Live in Your Truck All Winter

Not a survival fantasy — a blanket, a light, some water and a way to be seen, plus two things nobody carries.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The realistic bad scenario out here is not being lost in the wilderness. It is sitting on Highway 229 or the Siletz Highway for four hours behind a tree, in the dark, in the rain, with no cell signal and a quarter tank. Everything in a sensible car kit is aimed at that.

The core kit

ItemWhy this one
Wool blanketWorks wet. A fleece throw does not
Flashlight or headlamp, lithium batteriesLithium cells survive a hot summer glovebox and a cold winter
Jumper cables, or a lithium jump packThe pack means you do not need a second vehicle. Charge it twice a year
Water, a few bottlesRotate. Do not let it sit a whole year
Food that survives heatBars, jerky, nuts. Not chocolate, not anything that melts and leaks
First aid kitA real one, and know what is in it
Phone charger and a small power bankThe 12-volt socket works only while the engine does
Reflective triangles or flaresBeing seen on a dark curve is the actual life-safety item
Tire inflator and sealantFixes the common failure without changing a wheel in the mud
Work glovesYou will be moving something wet and splintery
Rain jacket and rubber bootsBeing wet is what turns an inconvenience into hypothermia
Tow strap and a folding saw or hatchetFor the limb that is not big enough to wait for a crew
Paper map of the countyYour phone's map is only as good as the signal

The two nobody carries

Cash, in small bills, in the glovebox. Fifty or a hundred in fives and tens. When the power is out from here to Newport, the card readers are down and the pumps do not run, and the one station with a generator is taking cash only and cannot break a hundred.

A written list of phone numbers. Not in the phone. On paper, in the glovebox, laminated or in a sandwich bag. Nobody knows anybody's number anymore, and a dead phone means you cannot call your own house from a borrowed one. There is a whole short piece on this: the paper phone list.

The rule about fuel

Half a tank is empty in winter. Fill at half, every time, from the first big blow to spring.

The reasoning is not range, it is idling. Sitting in a stopped line of traffic running the engine for heat burns a surprising amount of fuel over four hours, and when the power goes out the pumps stop working, which means the fuel you have is the fuel you get for a day or two. A full tank is also less prone to condensation.

If you are stuck in the vehicle

  1. Stay with it unless you can see somewhere safe to walk to. A vehicle is shelter, it is visible, and searchers look for vehicles.
  2. Get flashers on and triangles out behind you, well back, especially on a curve.
  3. Run the engine about ten minutes an hour for heat, not continuously. It saves fuel and it limits exhaust risk.
  4. Check the exhaust pipe is clear before you run it — of mud, water, debris or a snowbank. A blocked pipe puts carbon monoxide into the cabin, which is the way people die in stopped cars.
  5. Crack a downwind window slightly while the engine runs.
  6. Layer up and use the blanket rather than relying on the heater.
  7. Text rather than call, and put the phone in low power mode immediately.

Keeping it honest

Check the kit twice a year — the same weekend you check the smoke alarms works fine. Rotate the water and the food, charge the jump pack and the power bank, test the light, and look at the spare tire pressure, which is always low and is the one thing in the vehicle nobody ever checks.

Blankets, lights, jump packs, cables, triangles, inflators, gloves, boots and tow straps are all here — see the storm wall and the Fix It side. Driving conditions on these roads are their own subject: driving 229 and the coast range in bad weather.
