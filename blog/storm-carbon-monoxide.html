Carbon Monoxide: The One That Does Not Announce Itself

It has no smell, it makes you too confused to help yourself, and a twenty dollar alarm catches it every time.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Carbon monoxide poisoning during power outages is the most preventable serious injury in this entire subject. It is also the one people are least frightened of, because the risk is invisible and the sources feel harmless.

What it is and why it fools people

CO is a product of incomplete combustion. Anything that burns a fuel — gasoline, propane, natural gas, charcoal, kerosene, wood — makes some. It is colorless, odorless, tasteless and slightly lighter than air, so it does not settle in the basement and it does not warn you.

It binds to hemoglobin hundreds of times more readily than oxygen does, so your blood fills up with something that cannot carry oxygen while you feel, at first, like you are coming down with something. That is the trap. The early symptoms are indistinguishable from flu, and the middle symptoms include confusion and poor judgment, which means the person being poisoned is the least able to figure out what is wrong.

Symptoms, in order

LevelWhat it feels likeWhat to do
EarlyHeadache, mild nausea, tiredness, feeling offGet outside. Do not wait to be sure
ModerateWorse headache, dizziness, vomiting, confusion, blurred visionEveryone outside, call for help
SevereLoss of coordination, collapse, unconsciousnessDo not enter to rescue without air. Call and wait

The tell that separates it from flu: everybody in the house feels bad at once, and it gets better when you go outside. The pets go down first because they are smaller. If the dog is acting strange and two people have a headache, that is your answer.

The alarm

Battery-powered, not plug-in — a plug-in alarm during an outage is decoration. One on every level of the house and one outside each sleeping area. Test them at the start of storm season along with the smoke alarms.

They expire. The sensor in a CO alarm degrades and the whole unit has a service life of five to seven years, printed on the back. An alarm past its date can read normal in a room full of gas. Check the date on yours and if you cannot find one, replace it.

If it goes off, the response is not to investigate. It is: everybody outside, count heads, then call from outside. Do not open windows first and do not wander around trying to find the source, because ventilating the house before responders arrive destroys the evidence of where it came from and does not make you safe any faster than walking out the door does.

The outage sources, ranked

  1. A generator too close to the house. Twenty feet minimum, exhaust pointed away, never in a garage or under a deck. This is the number one cause.
  2. Charcoal indoors. A grill or a hibachi in a garage or a fireplace. Consistently fatal.
  3. A gas range or oven used for heat. Common, feels harmless, is not.
  4. A blocked flue. A storm can put a limb, a nest or a chunk of debris into a chimney or a furnace vent. Then the appliance works and the exhaust comes back inside.
  5. A vehicle idling in an attached garage. Including running the truck to charge phones. Back it out first.
  6. Unvented propane or kerosene heaters without an oxygen sensor, in a room sealed up against the cold.

The thing to check after the wind

Once the storm passes, before you fire up the furnace or the woodstove, walk outside and look at the terminations — the furnace vent, the water heater vent, the dryer vent, the chimney cap. Debris, a bird's nest knocked loose, a limb resting against a flue, or snow and leaves piled against a low sidewall vent. It takes five minutes and it is the check nobody does.

CO alarms sit with the smoke alarms on the Fix It wall and get pulled to the front when the storm wall goes up. If you own a generator and not a CO alarm, you have bought the dangerous half of the pair.
