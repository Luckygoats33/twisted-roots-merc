Chainsaw Readiness: The Saw Is Not the Problem

Bar oil, fresh mix, a sharp chain and chaps, all bought in the calm, because none of it is available on the morning of.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The morning after a big blow, three things happen at once out here: everybody needs a saw, everybody discovers their saw will not start, and the shelf of bar oil in every store between here and Newport is empty by ten. The saw itself is almost never the issue.

Why the mix goes off

Two-stroke mix is worse than straight gas. The ethanol pulls water in, the mix separates, and the light fractions that make it start evaporate. Mix that is two months old is marginal. Mix from last winter is why you are pulling the cord forty times.

Mix small batches — a gallon at a time, not five — use fresh gas, add stabilizer, and dump what is left at the end of the season into something with a bigger engine. Never guess the ratio. Single-dose bottles sized to a gallon exist precisely so nobody has to do arithmetic in a shed.

Sharpening, which changes everything

A sharp chain pulls itself into the wood and throws chips. A dull chain has to be pushed, throws sawdust, smokes, and makes the whole job take three times as long, which is when people get hurt.

The tells: fine powder instead of chips, having to lean on the saw, the cut wandering off square, smoke from the bar.

  1. Chain brake on, saw braced, bar in a vise or wedged solid.
  2. Use the correct round file diameter for your chain. It is stamped on the chain or in the manual, and using the wrong one changes the cutting geometry.
  3. File every cutter from the inside out, same number of strokes, same angle. Match the witness marks stamped on the cutter.
  4. Do all the cutters facing one way, then turn the saw around and do the others.
  5. Check the depth gauges — the little raker in front of each cutter — with a gauge tool every few sharpenings. Too high and the cutter cannot bite; too low and the saw grabs.
  6. Keep the chain the same length on both sides or the saw will cut in a curve.

If a chain has hit dirt, a rock or a fence staple, it is done for the day. Bring the spare.

Chaps, seriously

Chainsaw chaps are not padding. They are layers of loose ballistic fibre that get pulled out by the chain, wrap around the sprocket, and jam the saw within a fraction of a second. They work, they are cheap next to an ambulance ride from Siletz, and the injury they prevent is a femoral one.

The classic storm-cleanup injury is not a fall or a dropped tree. It is a tired person, on day two, cutting at knee height with a dull chain, getting a kickback into their own leg. Wear the chaps for the little cut too.

Do this at the start of the season

Pull the saw out on a dry day. Fresh mix, fresh bar oil, new plug if it is due, clean the air filter, clean the bar groove and the oiler port with a scrap of wire, flip the bar to even the wear, sharpen or fit a new chain, check the chain brake actually stops the chain, and run it through a round of firewood for five minutes.

Then you know. That is the whole point — knowing in October rather than finding out in a dark driveway.

Bar oil, mix, files, gas cans, gloves and glasses are on the Fix It wall and the storm wall. Once it is running, clearing a tree off the drive is the part where the thinking happens.
