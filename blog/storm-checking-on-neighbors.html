Storm Ready

Checking On the Neighbors Without Making It a Thing

The people who get in trouble in an outage are almost never the ones who call for help, which is why somebody has to knock.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

In a rural outage the emergency system is genuinely stretched. The fire district is running calls, the utility is chasing lines, and the sheriff is dealing with roads. What fills the gap is people on the same road knowing who is out there.

Who to think about

Not everybody needs checking on. The list is short and you probably already know it:

  • Anyone living alone, particularly older folks
  • Anyone on oxygen, a CPAP, a powered chair or any device that plugs in
  • Anyone recently out of hospital or on a treatment schedule
  • Households with a new baby, or with small children and one adult
  • Anyone without a vehicle, or without one that would start
  • Anyone who does not speak much English, or who is new to the area and does not know how bad it can get
  • The one house on your road you never see anybody come out of

Do the work before the storm

The hard part is not the knocking, it is knowing who is where. Work that out on a dry afternoon.

  1. Walk the road and get names and phone numbers. Say plainly why: the power goes out here, and it would be good to be able to check on each other.
  2. Write them on paper. A list of five houses, names, numbers, who lives there, anything relevant — a device, a dog that bites, a gate code, where they keep a spare key if they offer one.
  3. Agree a signal. A porch light in a window when it comes back, a flag on the mailbox, a text when you are all right. Something so nobody has to guess.
  4. Sort out who has what. Somebody has a generator, somebody has a chainsaw, somebody has a truck that will pull, somebody has a woodstove and can host. Nobody needs all of it if everybody knows who has which.
  5. Pair people up so each person on the list has one specific person who will come, rather than an assumption that somebody will.

How to knock without it being awkward

Do not lead with concern. Lead with information, and give them something.

Power looks like it will be out through tomorrow, thought you would want to know. We are running the generator this afternoon if you want to charge a phone. Heading to the store, need anything. We have soup on, come eat.

That gives a proud person a way to accept help without ever having to ask for it, which for a lot of folks out here is the entire obstacle. Bringing something — a thermos of coffee, a hot meal, a spare lantern — is better than asking whether they need anything, because the answer to that question is always no.

What to actually look at while you are there

  • Is it warm enough? A house at fifty degrees is fine for most people and genuinely risky for someone frail. Older people lose heat faster and often do not feel cold coming on.
  • Do they have water and something to eat that needs no cooking?
  • Any medication running low, and any device that needs power? See the medical devices piece.
  • Light. If somebody is using candles all night, bring them a lantern. That is a fire waiting to happen.
  • Heat sources. If there is a grill, a camp stove or a generator anywhere near an enclosure, that is the conversation to have, kindly and immediately. Carbon monoxide kills more people in outages than anything else and it is the one you can prevent from a doorstep.
  • Are they warm, dry, fed and not alone. That is the whole assessment.

Call it in when it is beyond you

You are not a paramedic. If somebody is confused, unusually drowsy, cold to the touch, short of breath, or has stopped making sense, that is a call for help, not a problem to solve with a blanket. The non-emergency line for a welfare check, or the emergency number if it is worse.

Then keep going back. Day one is easy. It is day four, when everybody is tired and nobody is dropping by anymore, that the checking actually matters.

The store's part in it

When the power is out we run on generator and cash, and the counter turns into a message board — who is out, which road is open, who needs a hand. Leave a note, take a note, and tell us if there is somebody up your road we should be asking after. That is not a service we advertise. It is just what a store in a small town is for.
