Storm Ready

The Walk-Around After the Wind Drops

An hour with a notebook and a phone camera, in daylight, in a specific order, before you touch anything.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The urge after a storm is to start dragging limbs. Resist it for an hour. Walk the whole place first, in daylight, with a notebook and a camera, because there are things that are dangerous and things that are expensive and you want to find both before you start moving debris around.

The safety pass, first

Before anything else, look for the four things that can hurt you:

  1. Wires. Any wire down, sagging, or with a tree on it. Assume it is live, stay the length of the tree away, keep children and animals in, and call it in. A wire in wet grass energizes the ground around it.
  2. Loaded trees and limbs. Anything hung up in a canopy, bent under tension, or leaning on something. Do not walk under it and do not cut it — see clearing a tree.
  3. Gas and propane. Smell around the tank and the regulator. If you smell gas, no flame, no switches, no phone use near it. Get away and call from a distance.
  4. Structure. Anything leaning, sagging, or with a new crack. If a roof has something heavy on it, do not go inside underneath it.

Then the outside, in order

Start at the road and work in, so you do not miss the things at the edge of the property.

  • Driveway and culvert. Washouts, a plugged culvert, ruts, a new gully. The culvert is the one that turns into an expensive problem quietly.
  • Trees. Down, leaning, split, hung up. New lean, fresh soil cracks at the base, and anything now pointed at the house.
  • Fences and gates. Down wire, broken posts, gates off their hinges. Do this before you turn stock out.
  • Roof, from the ground with binoculars. Missing or lifted shingles, exposed underlayment, a bent or missing ridge cap, damaged flashing, a dented or displaced vent. Do not get on a wet mossy roof to check — glass from the ground is safer and tells you almost as much.
  • Gutters and downspouts. Full, pulled loose, disconnected at a joint.
  • Siding, windows and screens. Impact marks, cracked panes, anything driven into a wall.
  • Outbuildings. Shed doors, roof panels, anything that has moved on its skids.
  • Vents and flues. The furnace vent, dryer vent, water heater vent and chimney cap. A blocked flue is a carbon monoxide problem the moment you fire anything up — see the CO piece.
  • The well head and the septic area. Cap intact, ground not ponding, no surfacing over the drainfield.
  • Debris that is not yours. Roofing, insulation, metal, plastic. Photograph where it came from if you can tell.

Then the inside

  • Ceilings and the tops of walls, for stains and bulges. A stain that was not there yesterday is an active leak, even if nothing is dripping.
  • The attic, with a light, looking for daylight and for wet insulation.
  • Around windows and doors, along the floor.
  • The crawlspace: standing water, torn vapor barrier, insulation fallen down.
  • Every drain and toilet, for slow running or gurgling, which points at a saturated septic system.
  • Turn appliances back on one at a time and listen, rather than everything at once.

Write it down as you go

ColumnWhat goes in it
What and whereSpecific. North gutter, over the kitchen window
Photo takenWide shot and close-up, both
UrgencyDangerous now / stops further damage / can wait for spring
Who fixes itMe / somebody with a bucket truck / insurance

Sort by the urgency column and work the top of it. The middle group — the things that stop further damage — is the one that matters financially, because a tarp today is a roof repair and a tarp next week is a ceiling.

Before you clean up

Photograph everything before you move it. Insurers want to see the damage as it happened, and a pile of neatly stacked limbs proves nothing. There is a whole short piece on getting the photos right: insurance photos. Keep receipts for anything you buy to mitigate damage, because that is usually reimbursable.

Then start work at the top of your list, not at whatever is most annoying to look at. Tarps, sealant, gloves, straps and contractor bags are on the storm wall and the Fix It side, and the lumber for a real repair is out in the yard.
