Storm Ready

Driving 229 and the Coast Range When the Weather Turns

The hazard on these roads is rarely ice; it is standing water, a fresh slide, and a limb around a blind corner.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The road between here and anywhere is a two-lane with a river on one side and a cut bank on the other, and it does specific things in bad weather. None of them are surprising once you have seen them, and all of them are worse at night.

What these roads actually do

  • Standing water on the outside of curves. The road is crowned and the runoff has nowhere to go on a saturated shoulder. Hitting a sheet of it at speed on one side of the vehicle pulls the steering hard toward it.
  • Debris around blind corners. Limbs, rock, mud off the cut bank, and sometimes a whole tree. On a road with no sight line, the only defence is speed.
  • Slides. Small ones are common on the steep cuts after weeks of rain. A pile of mud and rock in the lane at night with no warning is the classic coast range incident.
  • Water over the road in the low spots where the river or a creek comes up. It is worse than it looks, always.
  • Fallen line. A wire across the road, sometimes without an obvious pole down.
  • Log trucks. They still run in the rain, they cannot stop the way you can, and they need the whole road on the tight corners.

How to drive it

  1. Slow down more than feels necessary. Every hazard on this road is a sight-distance problem, and speed is the only variable you control.
  2. Stay off the painted lines and out of the standing water on the outside of curves. Take the crown where you can do it safely.
  3. If you hit deep water and the wheel goes light, do not brake and do not jerk it. Ease off the throttle, hold the wheel straight, let it slow itself.
  4. Low beams in fog and heavy rain. High beams reflect back off water in the air and you see less, not more.
  5. Leave far more following distance than you would in town. On wet chip seal with fir needles on it, braking distance goes up a lot.
  6. Assume there is something in your lane around every blind corner, because eventually there is.
  7. Pull over to let a log truck have the corner. They will thank you and you will be glad.

Never drive into water

This one is absolute. Turn around, do not drown is a slogan because it keeps having to be said.

Six inches of moving water will take your feet out from under you. A foot will float most cars. Two feet will move a pickup, and moving water pushes sideways with far more force than people imagine. Worse, on a road that has been under water you cannot see whether the surface is still there — culverts wash out from underneath and the road looks intact right up until it is not.

The same goes for driving over a downed line. Do not, ever, even if it looks dead and even if the traffic behind you is impatient. Back up and go another way, and call it in.

Before you leave

Check road conditions before you commit to a drive in a storm. The state's traveler information line and website carry closures and incidents, and the county puts out road closures for the local routes. Ask at the counter, too — somebody has usually just come from the direction you are going, and that is better information than any website.

Tell somebody your route and when you expect to be back. On a road where cell service comes and goes, that is your entire safety net.

Know your alternates, and know when there are none

Siletz has a small number of ways out, and in a bad enough storm some of them close. Work out your alternate routes in daylight, on a paper map, before you need them — a phone map will happily route you onto a logging road that is a creek in December.

Sometimes the honest answer is that you do not go. Waiting six hours in a warm house beats sitting on a closed highway, and there is no errand in Newport worth the coast range at night in a windstorm.

Wipers, ice scrapers, triangles, tow straps and lights are on the Fix It wall, and what should live in the vehicle all winter is in the car kit piece. If it goes wrong anyway, getting stuck is the next one.
