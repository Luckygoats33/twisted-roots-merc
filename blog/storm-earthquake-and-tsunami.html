Storm Ready

Earthquake and Tsunami Basics for This Stretch of Coast

For a local quake there is no warning system; the shaking is the warning and the whole plan is to walk uphill.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

This is the one nobody wants to write about and everybody who lives on this coast should have thought about once, properly, so that they never have to think about it again — the thinking is the whole preparation.

Two completely different tsunamis

Distant. A quake somewhere far away — Alaska, Japan, Chile. The wave takes hours to get here, the warning system works, officials will tell you, and there is time to move calmly. This is the version the sirens and the alerts are designed for.

Local. The Cascadia Subduction Zone, offshore, right here. The wave arrives in something like fifteen to twenty minutes. There is no official warning, because there is no time for one. The shaking is the warning. If you are on the coast and the ground shakes hard for a long time, that is the entire alert you get.

Everything else about tsunami preparedness follows from that one distinction.

What to do when it shakes

  1. Drop, cover, hold on while it is shaking. Under a sturdy table, hands over your neck. Do not run outside during the shaking — falling debris outside a building is worse than being inside one.
  2. When it stops, if you are in a tsunami zone, go inland or uphill immediately. Do not wait for an official word. Do not go home for anything. Do not check the ocean.
  3. Go on foot. Roads will be cracked, bridges may be down, and everybody else will be driving. Walking is faster and it does not get stuck in a jam.
  4. Get to high ground and stay there. Later waves are often bigger than the first, and it can go on for hours.
  5. Expect strong aftershocks. If you feel another long one, do not go back down.

Siletz sits inland up the river, which changes the picture considerably compared to being on the beach — but river valleys carry surge, and anyone who works, shops or has kids at school nearer the water should know the zones and the routes for those places. Look at the evacuation maps once, walk the route once, and then it lives in your legs rather than in your memory.

The parts people underestimate

The shaking in a Cascadia event is expected to last several minutes, not seconds. Minutes is an extraordinarily long time to be in an earthquake and it is what makes this different from anything most people have felt.

Afterward, the coast should expect to be genuinely on its own for a while. Bridges, roads and the coast range highways are the weak points, and help coming from the valley has to get over them. This is exactly why the state's advice out here is two weeks ready and not three days. Everything in the storm section of this site — water, light, heat, food, sanitation, a radio — is the same kit, and it is the kit that matters most in this scenario.

The family plan

Agree the meeting places out loud, once: one near home, one on high ground, and one out of the area. Agree that nobody goes back for anybody — everybody goes up and you find each other later at the meeting point. That sounds cold and it is the thing that saves people.

Everybody knows the out-of-area contact's number, on paper, per the phone list. Long distance and text get through when local does not.

And then stop thinking about it

Do the thinking once. Walk the route, strap the water heater, put shoes under the bed, build the two-week kit, and then live your life. The point of preparation is that it lets you stop worrying, not that it gives you something new to worry about.

The kit is the same kit either way — see the storm wall and going-bag versus stay-home kit. The evacuation maps for this county are posted at the counter, and Eric has walked most of the routes.
