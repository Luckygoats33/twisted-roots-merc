Storm Ready

Extension Cord Gauge, Length, and Why the Cheap One Gets Warm

A cord that is too thin or too long steals voltage, cooks the appliance and heats the cord, and the fix is one number on the jacket.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

An extension cord is a resistor you plug things into. The longer and thinner it is, the more of your voltage it eats on the way to the far end, and that lost voltage comes out as heat somewhere in the middle of the cord.

This is not academic. Undervoltage is what burns out fridge compressors and pump motors — the motor tries to draw more current to make up the difference, gets hotter, and eventually cooks a winding. Half the appliance failures blamed on generators are actually cord failures.

Reading the jacket

Printed on every cord is something like 12/3 SJTW. The first number is the wire gauge, and smaller is fatter — 12 gauge is thicker copper than 14, which is thicker than 16. The 3 means three conductors, which means it has a ground. The letters are the jacket rating; the W is the one that matters, and it means outdoor rated.

Indoor cords in the wet are a real hazard, not a technicality. Everything outside during a storm here gets soaked and stays soaked for a week.

When in doubt, buy 12 gauge. It costs more, it is heavier and stiffer in the cold, and it will run anything you plug into it at any length you own. There is no downside except your wrists.

The rules that come up during an outage

  • Do not daisy-chain cords. Two fifty-footers joined is not the same as one hundred-footer — you have added two connections that can loosen, arc, and let water in. And the effective gauge is still the thinner of the two.
  • Uncoil it completely. A cord left coiled on the reel while carrying real current is an inductor and a heat trap. This is the single most common way people melt a cord jacket.
  • No cords under rugs or through doorways that close on them. The damage happens where you cannot see it.
  • Feel the cord. Warm is a warning. Hot means stop now and go up a gauge.
  • Keep the connection out of the puddle. Hang the joint off a nail or set it on a block. Or buy a cord long enough that the joint lands somewhere dry.
  • A surge strip is not a generator accessory. Cheap portable generators put out messy power and the strip's protection components can wear out fast on it. Plug sensitive electronics into an inverter generator or through a UPS, or accept the risk.

How many cords you actually need

Two 50-foot 12-gauge outdoor cords covers almost every house. One goes to the fridge, one goes to the freezer or wherever the chest freezer lives, and if you need a third for lights, run a light-duty cord off a strip at the near end rather than a fourth long haul from the generator.

Label them. A strip of tape on each end saying FRIDGE and FREEZER sounds fussy until the fourth night, when somebody unplugs the wrong thing to charge a phone and nobody notices until morning.

What to do in the calm

Pull your cords out before storm season and look at them in daylight. Cracked jacket, a bent or missing ground pin, a plug that feels loose in an outlet, any scorching or greenish corrosion on the blades — that cord is done. Cut the ends off so nobody rescues it out of the trash.

Outdoor cords in 25 and 50 foot, surge strips, and the electrical odds and ends are on the Fix It wall, and they get restocked deeper when the storm wall goes to storm levels.
