Flashlights, Headlamps and Lanterns: Which One for Which Job

Most households own four flashlights and no headlamps, which is exactly backwards for an outage.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Every house has a flashlight. Almost none have enough light, because a flashlight is the least useful of the three types for what you actually do in an outage, which is work on things with two hands in a dark room.

The three tools, and what each is for

Headlamp. The one to buy first, and the one people resist because they feel silly wearing it. It puts light exactly where you are looking and leaves both hands free. Getting a generator started, feeding animals, changing a fuse, cooking, carrying a child down a dark hall — all two-hand jobs. A headlamp per person is the single biggest upgrade to a household's outage capability.

Lantern. The one to buy second. A flashlight lights a spot; a lantern lights a room. It is what makes an evening in a dark house feel like an evening rather than a crisis. Put one on the kitchen table, one in the bathroom, and the household stops walking into things.

Flashlight. Genuinely best at distance and at throwing a tight beam a long way — checking the roofline, walking the property, seeing what fell across the drive. Keep one good bright one. You do not need four.

Brightness is the spec people shop on and it matters least. A thousand-lumen headlamp on high runs for forty minutes and blinds everyone you talk to. You will use the low setting almost always, so buy on runtime at low and on how comfortable it is to wear.

Rechargeable or replaceable

This is the real decision, and the answer is both, on purpose.

Rechargeable is better for the thing you use daily — brighter for the money, no battery cost, and you top it off from a power bank. Replaceable-battery lights are better for the thing that sits in a drawer for a year, because a AA in a drawer still works and a built-in lithium cell in a drawer might not.

The sensible household split is rechargeable headlamps that live on a hook and get charged, plus battery lanterns and one battery flashlight in the kit that never gets touched except to swap cells at the start of the season. Whatever you choose, standardize the battery size across the house so one kind of spare covers everything. There is more on that in the battery piece.

How many, and where

  1. One headlamp per person, on a hook by the door or in a bedside drawer. Not in a tote in the garage.
  2. One lantern per occupied room, minimum kitchen and bathroom.
  3. One good flashlight by the back door for going outside.
  4. One light in each vehicle, checked when you check the tires.
  5. Spare batteries stored with the lights, in a bag, not loose in a drawer.

The trick with the lantern

A lantern on a table lights the table and shadows everything else. Set it high — on top of the fridge, on a shelf, hung from a cabinet handle — and it lights the whole room off the ceiling. Or stand a headlamp on end with the strap around a gallon jug of water and you get a diffuse room light that is genuinely pleasant. It is the oldest camp trick there is and it works.

Headlamps, lanterns, flashlights and the batteries that feed them are all on the storm wall, front left as you come in. If you own one flashlight and nothing else, buy a headlamp next.
