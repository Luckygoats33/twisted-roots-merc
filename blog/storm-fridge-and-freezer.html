Keeping a Fridge and Freezer Cold With No Power

Four hours and forty-eight hours are the two numbers that matter, and both of them assume you keep the door shut.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

An unopened refrigerator holds safe temperature for about four hours. A full freezer holds for about forty-eight, and a half-full one for about twenty-four. Every one of those numbers assumes the door stays closed, and every one of them collapses if the household treats the fridge like it still works.

Before the wind, if you get warning

  1. Turn both appliances to their coldest setting the day before. You are banking cold in the food itself, which is where nearly all the thermal mass is.
  2. Fill empty freezer space with jugs of water. A full freezer holds cold twice as long as an empty one, and you end up with drinking water as they thaw.
  3. Freeze a few gallon jugs to move to the fridge later.
  4. Put a thermometer in each. This is the whole game and it costs a few dollars — more on that below.
  5. Buy a bag or two of ice and know where the cooler is.

The frozen-cup trick

Do this now, before anything happens. Freeze a small cup of water solid, then set a coin on top of the ice and put the cup back in the freezer.

When you come home after an outage you did not witness, look at the cup. Coin still on top: it never thawed, food is fine. Coin partway down: it thawed some and refroze, use judgment. Coin on the bottom: everything in there got warm and refroze, and it looks perfectly frozen but it is not safe.

That cup answers a question you otherwise cannot answer, which is what happened while you were gone.

The rules during the outage

  • One trip. Decide what you want, open, take it all, close. Standing with the door open deciding costs you an hour of holdover every time.
  • Eat down the fridge first. Fridge food goes bad first, so it should be the first three meals. The freezer is a bank, not a pantry.
  • Do not move freezer food to the fridge to thaw. Move fridge food to a cooler instead, and keep the freezer sealed as a single cold block.
  • Move milk, eggs and leftovers to a cooler with ice on day one. Now you can open the cooler all you like and the fridge stays shut.
  • Blankets on the freezer. A sleeping bag or a couple of blankets over a chest freezer adds real hours. Not over the vents on a fridge, and not on anything with a running compressor.
  • A chest freezer beats an upright by a wide margin, because cold air falls and stays in a chest when you open the lid.

Dry ice, if you can get it

Fifty pounds of dry ice will hold a full freezer for two days or so. It goes on top of the food, not underneath, because the cold falls. Handle it with gloves, never seal it in an airtight container, and give the room ventilation because it sublimates into carbon dioxide.

Realistically, dry ice on this coast in a widespread outage is gone within hours. It is a plan for a single-house outage, not a regional one.

The thermometers

Buy two cheap appliance thermometers and leave one in each. This is the difference between guessing and knowing, and it is a couple of dollars each.

Outside is not a refrigerator

It is tempting, and on this coast it is wrong. Winter here sits in the forties and fifties, which is refrigerator-warm, not refrigerator-cold, and it swings. The porch also has raccoons, and we have all seen what a raccoon does to a cooler. If you use outside at all, use it as a cold room for sealed things and keep a thermometer out there too.

What to keep and what to throw when it is over is a separate and more detailed question — see food safety after the power comes back. Coolers, ice, thermometers and the rest are on the storm wall and in the coolers aisle.
