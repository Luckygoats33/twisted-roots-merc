Generator Fuel: Storing It, Stabilizing It, Not Running Out of It

The generator in your shed will probably not start, and the reason is almost always the gas that has been sitting in it.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Every year somebody rolls a generator out on the first big blow, pulls the cord forty times, swears at it, and drives to town. The engine is usually fine. The fuel is not.

Why gas goes bad, and how fast

Pump gasoline starts degrading in about thirty days. The light fractions that make it easy to start evaporate first, so what is left is harder to ignite. Then oxidation produces gums and varnish that glue up the tiny passages in a carburetor — the main jet on a small engine is a hole about the width of a pin, and it does not take much to block it.

Ethanol makes it worse in two ways. It absorbs water out of the air, and when it has absorbed enough it separates out into a layer of water-and-alcohol in the bottom of the tank, which is exactly where the fuel pickup sits. Ethanol-free gas, where you can get it, stores far better and is worth the extra money for anything that sits.

Pump gas with ethanol, untreated, vented canAbout 1 month
Pump gas with ethanol, untreated, sealed canAbout 3 months
Pump gas with ethanol, stabilizedAbout 12 months
Ethanol-free gas, stabilized, sealed, cool2 years, sometimes more
Diesel, treated with biocideAround a year, longer if kept dry
PropaneIndefinite. This is propane's whole argument

Stabilizer, used properly

Stabilizer only works on fuel that is still good. It is a preservative, not a cure — pouring it into a can of two-year-old varnish accomplishes nothing.

  1. Add the stabilizer to the empty can first, then fill with fresh gas at the pump. The splash of filling mixes it far better than pouring it on top and shaking.
  2. Dose per the label. More is not better and can affect how the engine runs.
  3. After you fill the generator's own tank, run the engine ten minutes so stabilized fuel actually reaches the carburetor. Stabilizer in the tank does nothing for the fuel bowl that is already sitting there.

How much to keep

A typical portable generator running a fridge, a freezer, some lights and a laptop burns somewhere around half a gallon an hour at partial load. Run it eight hours a day and that is four gallons a day.

Outage lengthRunning 8 hrs/dayRunning in 2-on/4-off shifts
1 day4 gallons4 gallons
3 days12 gallons6 gallons
1 week28 gallons14 gallons

Two or three five-gallon cans is a realistic household number. More than that stored at home starts being its own hazard. Store cans in a detached shed, not the garage, not near a water heater pilot, up off the floor and in the shade.

The rotation that actually happens

The theory is you rotate the cans through your truck every few months. In practice people forget. The version that works: pick a habit you already have — mowing season starting, hunting season starting, the first week the heat comes on — and tie fuel rotation to it. Pour the old cans into the truck, refill at the pump with stabilizer already in them, done.

Off-season shutdown

When storm season ends, do one of two things and not neither. Either fill the tank completely with stabilized fuel so there is no air space for condensation, or run the carburetor dry by shutting the fuel valve and letting the engine quit on its own. Half a tank of untreated gas sitting all summer is how you get here again next winter.

Stabilizer, five-gallon cans, funnels and two-cycle oil are all on the storm shelf. So is everything about running the thing without poisoning the house, which matters more than the fuel does.
