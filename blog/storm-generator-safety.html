Running a Generator Without Killing Anybody

Carbon monoxide and backfeed are the two things that turn a generator from a convenience into a coroner's report.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A generator is the single most useful and single most dangerous thing you can add to an outage. Both of the ways it kills people are entirely preventable and both of them come from doing something that feels reasonable at the time.

Carbon monoxide

A portable generator puts out more carbon monoxide in an hour than a hundred idling cars. That is not a figure of speech, it is roughly the ratio. CO is colorless, odorless, and it makes you confused before it makes you unconscious, which is why people who are being poisoned do not save themselves.

The rules are simple and there is no version of them that bends:

  • Outside only. Never in a garage, never in a shed, never in a carport, never in a crawlspace, never on a covered porch, never just inside the open barn door.
  • Twenty feet from the house minimum, further if you can, with the exhaust pointed away from any door, window or vent.
  • Open garage door is not ventilation. People die this way every single winter. An open door does not move air; wind moves air, and the wind is usually pushing toward the house.
  • Battery CO alarms inside, on every level, and test them before storm season. A plug-in alarm is useless in an outage.

If anyone in the house gets a headache, feels sick, or goes strangely dull and irritable while the generator is running, get everybody outside first and ask questions from the driveway.

Backfeed

The other one. Somebody makes a cord with a male plug on both ends, plugs the generator into a dryer outlet, and now the house is energized from the panel. It works. It also pushes power backward through your service drop into the utility transformer, which steps it back up to several thousand volts on the line outside — the same line a lineman is about to touch believing it is dead.

It is also a fine way to burn your own house down, because nothing in that path is protected the way it is supposed to be.

The only correct answers are a transfer switch or an interlock kit installed at the panel by somebody licensed, which physically makes it impossible for the main breaker and the generator breaker to be on at the same time. An interlock is the cheap version and it is not expensive. Until you have one, run cords.

Running cords instead

Cords are fine. Cords are how most people get through an outage, and there is no shame in it.

  1. Start the generator outside, at distance, before you plug anything into it.
  2. Run outdoor-rated extension cords in through a window or a door with a towel around them, not through a hole you drilled in a panic.
  3. Match the cord gauge to the load and the length — a long thin cord starves the appliance and heats up. There is a table in the piece on cord gauge.
  4. Prioritize: fridge, freezer, one lamp, phone charging. Not everything at once.
  5. Run the fridge and freezer in shifts. Two hours on, a few hours off, is plenty to hold temperature and it stretches your fuel enormously.

The other three that get people

Wet generators. Never operate one in the rain uncovered. A generator tent or an open-sided canopy with plenty of clearance is the answer, not a tarp draped over it — that traps heat and exhaust.

Hot refueling. Shut it down and let it cool before you pour gas. Spilled fuel on a hot muffler goes up instantly.

Ungrounded and unsecured. Read the plate on yours for grounding. And chain it to something, because a generator sitting in a driveway during an outage is the most stealable object in the county.

Cords, gas cans, fuel stabilizer and CO alarms are all on the storm wall, and the electrical bits live on the Fix It side. We do not sell generators. We do sell everything that keeps one from being a problem.
