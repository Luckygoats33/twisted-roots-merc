Storm Ready

Getting Unstuck on a Wet Coastal Road

Traction, not power, and the moment you start digging holes with the tires you have made it a recovery instead of a nudge.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Around here you get stuck in mud, in a soft shoulder, or in a rut on a gravel road that has gone to soup after three weeks of rain. Snow is a novelty. The principles are the same and the first one is the one everybody violates: stop spinning.

The first thirty seconds decide everything

A spinning tire polishes the surface under it, melts a bit, digs a hole, and drops the vehicle onto its frame. Five seconds of wheelspin turns a thirty-second push into an hour with a shovel. The instant you feel the tires break loose and go nowhere, come off the throttle.

Then get out and look. Every recovery starts by looking at where the tires actually are, which axle is driving, and what is under the vehicle. Two minutes of looking beats twenty of guessing.

The order to try things

  1. Clear the tires. Dig the mud away from in front of and behind each driving wheel, and clear anything packed into the tread. If the frame or the diff is resting on ground, dig that out too — you cannot drive a vehicle that is sitting on its belly, no matter how much traction you find.
  2. Straighten the wheels. Turned front wheels are a plough.
  3. Rock it. Gently between drive and reverse, in a rhythm, using the vehicle's own momentum. Small throttle. If it moves an inch each way, keep going and the arc grows. If it does not move at all after a few tries, stop before you dig in.
  4. Add traction under the driving wheels. Gravel, sand, floor mats, cut fir boughs, a scrap of plywood, cardboard, kitty litter. Wedge it hard against the tire, not loose in front of it. Stand out of the line of fire, because whatever you put under there can come out fast.
  5. Let some air out if you are in soft ground with no other option. Down to fifteen or eighteen psi widens the footprint dramatically. You must reinflate before driving any distance, which is why a tire inflator is in the car kit.
  6. Try reverse first when in doubt. The ground behind you is ground you already drove over and know is solid.

Getting pulled out

This is where people get hurt, and it is worth knowing two things.

Use a proper recovery point. A frame-mounted tow hook or a receiver with a rated shackle. Never a ball hitch — a ball can shear off and it becomes a cannonball. Never a bumper, never an axle, never a suspension component, never a tie-down loop meant for shipping the vehicle.

Nobody stands in line with the strap. If a strap or a shackle lets go it travels at lethal speed along the line of pull. Everyone stands well off to the side. Lay a coat or a floor mat over the middle of the strap so that if it breaks it drops rather than flies.

Use a proper recovery strap, take up the slack slowly, and communicate — a hand signal both drivers agreed on before anybody got in a vehicle. Do not use a chain, and do not use a ratchet strap; neither is designed for shock loading and both fail badly.

When to leave it

If the vehicle is on a soft shoulder over a drop, in moving water, or leaning in a way that could roll, stop and call somebody. The same goes if it is dark, raining, and you are on a blind corner — the recovery is now more dangerous than the situation, because the actual risk is another vehicle coming around that corner.

Get flashers on and triangles well back up the road first, before you do anything else. That is not optional on a road like 229.

Bag of sand or gravelDoubles as bed weight, which prevents half of these

Reflective trianglesDo these first, always

Not getting stuck

Weight over the drive wheels — a couple of bags of sand or gravel in the bed over the axle transforms an empty pickup in the wet. Stay off soft shoulders in the rain, especially where the shoulder has been chewed by log trucks. And on a gravel road that has gone to ruts, drive the ridge between the ruts or the untouched edge, not the polished bottom of the rut.

Shovels, straps, gravel and sand are out in the yard, and the recovery gear and inflators are on the Fix It wall. Road conditions themselves are covered in driving the coast range in bad weather.
