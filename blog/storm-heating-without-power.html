Storm Ready

Heating a House Safely When the Power Is Out

Coastal winter rarely gets dangerously cold, which is exactly why people take risks with heat that they would never take in Bend.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A house in Siletz in a winter outage is not going to freeze your pipes most of the time. It is going to get to the low fifties inside and stay there, damp, which is miserable but not dangerous for a healthy adult. Understanding that changes the calculation, because the risky heating methods people reach for are riskier than the cold they are avoiding.

What still works when the power goes

  • Woodstove. The only genuinely complete answer. Needs no power, heats, cooks, dries clothes. If you have one, the whole question is whether you have dry wood, which means wood you split and stacked under cover a season ago, not wood you buy wet on day two.
  • Gas fireplace with a standing pilot. The old millivolt kind generates its own tiny current from the pilot flame and runs with the power out. If yours has an electronic ignition and a blower, it does not.
  • Propane heater rated for indoor use. The small radiant heaters with a low-oxygen shutoff sensor and a tip-over switch. These are designed to be run inside with ventilation, and they are the one portable combustion heater that belongs in a house.
  • Bodies, blankets and one small room. Boring, free, and the thing that actually gets most people through.

What absolutely does not

These are the ones that kill people in outages, and every one of them feels reasonable to somebody at two in the morning:

  • A gas range or oven for heat. An oven is not designed to run open-door as a heater. It produces carbon monoxide and it is the single most common source of outage CO poisoning.
  • A charcoal or gas grill indoors or in the garage. Charcoal is the worst offender there is. Even in a garage with the door up.
  • A generator anywhere enclosed. Covered in the generator piece, and it bears repeating in every piece.
  • An unvented kerosene heater in a sealed room, or any combustion heater without an oxygen depletion sensor.
  • A camp stove as a heater. A camp stove is for cooking, briefly, with ventilation. There is a whole separate discussion in when a camp stove can come inside.

Every one of those needs a working carbon monoxide alarm to catch it, on batteries, tested. That is the cheapest insurance in this entire subject and it is covered in the CO piece.

Heat one room, not a house

This is the technique that makes everything else easier. Pick the smallest room with the fewest exterior walls and windows — often a bedroom or an interior hall — and make that the warm room.

  1. Close doors to every room you are not using, and close the vents in them.
  2. Hang a blanket over doorways that have no door, especially at the top and bottom of stairs. Heat leaves upward.
  3. Cover windows at night with blankets or the window film off the Fix It wall. Glass is where most of the heat goes.
  4. Put a rolled towel or a door sweep along the bottom of exterior doors.
  5. Everybody sleeps in the warm room. Including the dog, who is a genuine heat source.

Dress for it indoors

Layers, and wool over cotton — cotton holds moisture against you and in a damp house everything is slightly damp. A hat indoors makes a startling difference. Wool socks, and change them if your feet get damp at all. Keep moving a little; a person sitting still gets cold in a way a person doing chores does not.

Eat and drink something warm on a schedule. Digestion generates heat and dehydration makes people feel colder, and nobody drinks enough water in an outage because the tap situation is annoying.

The pipe question

Freezing pipes are a real but uncommon problem here. If it is genuinely going below freezing and the house has no heat, open the cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls, let a faucet drip, and know where your main shutoff is. If you leave the house for days in a hard freeze with no heat, shut the water off at the main and drain the system.

Wool blankets, wool socks, hand warmers and the window film are all stocked — see the storm wall, and there is a separate piece on staying warm overnight, which is where most of the discomfort actually lives.
