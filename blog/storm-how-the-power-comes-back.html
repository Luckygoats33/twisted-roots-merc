Storm Ready

Why Your Line Is Last: How Power Gets Restored Out Here

Crews do not fix outages in the order they were reported, and understanding the actual order will save you a lot of phone calls.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Somebody in Newport got their lights back four hours ago and you are still sitting in the dark. That is not favoritism and it is not because you failed to call it in enough times. It is arithmetic.

The order crews actually work in

Restoration goes largest-first, on purpose. The sequence is roughly:

  1. Transmission lines. The big steel-and-wood runs feeding whole regions. Nothing downstream matters until these are up.
  2. Substations. One substation can carry thousands of meters.
  3. Main distribution feeders. The three-wire runs along the highway. One repair here can restore a whole town.
  4. Critical facilities. Hospitals, water treatment, emergency services, sometimes a shelter.
  5. Tap lines and laterals. The single line that leaves the highway and goes up your road.
  6. Individual services. The one wire from the pole to your house.

If you live at the end of a road, your outage is at the bottom of that list almost by definition. The crew is not ignoring you. They are getting eight hundred people back on before they get you and your three neighbors back on, which is the correct call and would be the correct call if it were their house too.

Why rural coastal lines break more

Three things stack up here. The runs are long — miles of line serving very few meters, which means a lot of exposure per customer. The right-of-way is narrow and the trees on either side are Douglas fir and Sitka spruce that go a hundred and fifty feet up. And the ground is saturated from weeks of rain before the wind even arrives, so trees do not snap, they tip, roots and all, and take the line with them along with a hundred feet of neighbors.

That last part is why the wind speed number in the forecast matters less than what came before it. A fifty mile an hour gust after a dry stretch takes down branches. The same gust after three weeks of rain takes down whole trees.

Report it once, properly

Call or text the outage in one time, from the account phone number if you can, and then stop. Utilities use the pattern of reports to map the fault — if ten houses on one lateral report and nobody past the crossroad does, that tells them where the break is. Your fifth call does not add data. It occupies a phone line somebody with a downed wire in their yard is trying to reach.

Do report separately, and immediately, if you have a wire on the ground, a pole broken, or a tree burning. That is a different call and it jumps the queue.

Reading the estimate

The first restoration estimate you get is usually a placeholder — a default number the system puts out before anyone has laid eyes on the damage. It will often get worse before it gets better, because the real assessment comes after a crew drives the line. When the estimate suddenly jumps from four hours to three days, that is not the utility giving up. That is the first honest number.

Plan for the estimate you get, then quietly plan for twice it. Nobody has ever regretted being ready for an extra day.

Assume that if the wind is still blowing, nothing is getting fixed. Crews will not put a man in a bucket in a sustained blow, and they should not. Repairs start when the wind lays down, which on this coast is often the middle of the night after the front passes.

Meanwhile, what you can control is what is in the house. That is the whole point of the storm wall, and of building a kit before you need it.
