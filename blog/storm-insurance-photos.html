Storm Ready

Taking Storm Photos Your Insurer Will Actually Accept

Wide, medium, close, with something for scale, taken before you move anything, plus the ones you should have taken last summer.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Claims get reduced or denied over documentation far more often than over coverage. Not because anybody is being difficult, but because an adjuster arriving three weeks later is looking at a tidy yard and a tarped roof and has to take your word for what was underneath.

The pass you should have made last summer

The single most useful photo set is the one taken before anything happened. It establishes what condition things were in, which is the argument adjusters actually have with people — whether that roof was already worn.

On a dry day, walk the property and photograph: each side of the house, the roof from each corner, every room, inside every closet with the doors open, the contents of the garage and the shed, the outbuildings, the fences, and any tree that matters. Open the drawers and cupboards and photograph what is in them. Get the serial numbers and model plates on the big appliances, the furnace, the water heater, the generator and the tools.

Then store it somewhere that is not in the house. Cloud storage, or a drive at a relative's. A photo record that burned with the house is not a record.

After the storm, before you touch anything

  1. Take the safety pass first. No photograph is worth standing under a hung-up limb or near a downed wire.
  2. Shoot before you move a single thing. This is the rule that matters most. The debris pile in the yard is evidence until you have photographed it.
  3. Three distances for every item of damage. A wide shot that shows where on the property it is, a medium shot that shows the whole damaged element, and a close-up that shows the detail.
  4. Something for scale in the close-up — a tape measure is best, a hand or a shoe will do.
  5. Include a landmark in the wide shot so it is obvious which building and which side.
  6. Video too. Walk the whole property with the camera rolling, narrating what you are looking at. It takes five minutes and it captures things you would not have thought to photograph.
  7. Photograph the cause as well as the effect — the tree, the limb, the failed gutter — because the cause is often what determines coverage.

Keep everything else too

  • Receipts for anything you buy to prevent further damage — tarps, plastic, fasteners, a pump rental. This is usually reimbursable and it is usually forgotten.
  • Receipts for a motel and meals if the house was uninhabitable. Many policies cover additional living expenses.
  • A written log with dates for who you called, when, and what they said. Note the claim number and the adjuster's name the moment you have them.
  • The damaged material itself, if you can keep a piece of it safely. A section of shingle tells a story a photo cannot.

Do the temporary repair anyway

Policies require you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage. Waiting for an adjuster before you tarp a hole is not reasonable, and the additional water damage from waiting may not be covered.

So photograph it, then tarp it, then photograph the tarp. See tarping a roof leak. Do not throw out the damaged material until the claim is settled, and do not sign anything with a contractor who turned up in your driveway uninvited the morning after a storm.

File it the same day

Call it in as soon as you have the photos, even if you do not yet know the full extent. Claim volume after a coastal windstorm is enormous and adjusters get scheduled in the order calls came in. A call on the morning after and a call four days later are weeks apart in practice.

Have the policy number handy, which is why it is on the paper phone list. The full walk-around procedure is in the post-storm walk-around, and the materials for the temporary repair are on the storm wall.
