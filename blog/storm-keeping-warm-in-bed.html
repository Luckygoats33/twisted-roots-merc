Storm Ready

Getting a Warm Night's Sleep in a Cold House

Sleeping badly for four nights is what makes people careless, and fixing it costs less than any heater does.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Nobody plans for this part. The list has water and batteries and a radio on it, and then night three arrives and everybody is lying awake at fifty-one degrees being quietly miserable. Sleep deprivation is what turns a manageable outage into a bad decision with a chainsaw on day four.

The physics, which is mostly about the floor

You lose heat four ways: conduction into whatever you are touching, convection into moving air, radiation to cold surfaces, and evaporation off damp skin. In a bed, conduction downward is the biggest one by a distance and it is the one everybody ignores. A mattress compressed under your body is not insulation, and the air under a bed is the coldest air in the room.

So the first move is not another blanket on top. It is a blanket under you. A wool blanket or a closed-cell foam pad between you and the sheet is worth more than two on top.

The order to build a warm bed

  1. Insulation underneath. A wool blanket, a foam pad, or a folded comforter beneath the sheet.
  2. Wool next to skin. Long underwear, dry wool socks, a hat. Most heat loss people notice is from the head and feet.
  3. Layers on top, loose. Several thin layers trap more air than one thick one. Do not tuck them so tight you compress them; loft is what insulates.
  4. Shrink the space. Sleeping bag inside the bed, or a smaller bed, or everybody in one bed. Less volume to heat with your own body.
  5. A mylar emergency blanket as the outer layer. It reflects radiated heat back and it costs a dollar. It also crinkles and sweats, so never put it next to skin — outermost only.

The hot water bottle

The single best thing on this list and almost nobody does it anymore. Heat water on the woodstove or a camp stove, fill a rubber bottle or a well-sealed screw-top jug, wrap it in a towel so it does not burn you, and put it at your feet twenty minutes before you get in.

A quart of hot water gives off heat for hours and warms the bed rather than warming you directly, which is the trick — get into a warm bed instead of trying to warm a cold one with your body. Do the same with a rice sock heated on the stove if you would rather.

Go to bed warm, not cold

You cannot warm a bed from a standing start if you are already chilled. Ten minutes of moving before bed — carrying wood, doing dishes with heated water, anything — and something hot to eat or drink gets your core up. Then get in immediately.

The related mistake is going to bed hungry. Your body burns fuel overnight to hold temperature, and it needs something in it to burn. A snack with some fat in it before bed is a legitimate warmth strategy, not an excuse.

Stay dry, which is the coastal problem

Cold here is not the difficulty. Damp is. A house without heat for a week gets clammy, and damp bedding conducts heat away from you several times faster than dry bedding.

  • Do not sleep in the clothes you wore all day, especially not socks. They are damper than you think.
  • Air the bedding during the day if you get a dry hour.
  • Crack a window slightly. It sounds wrong, but four people breathing in a sealed room all night puts a remarkable amount of moisture into the bedding, and a little airflow is worth the heat it costs.
  • Wool over cotton for anything against skin. Wool insulates when damp; cotton does the opposite.

Who to watch

Infants, older folks, and anyone with a circulatory condition lose heat faster and notice it later. An older neighbor living alone in an unheated house for four days is a genuine risk and it is worth a knock on the door — see checking on the neighbors. Babies should not have loose blankets, so dress them in an extra layer and a sleep sack rather than piling covers on.

Wool blankets, wool socks, hand warmers and mylar sheets are on the storm wall. A wool blanket is the one item on this list people buy once and then thank themselves for every winter after — see also heating a house safely without power.
