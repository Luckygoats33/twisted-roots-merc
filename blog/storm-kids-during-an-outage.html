Storm Ready

Kids in a Four-Day Outage

Children take their reading of how bad this is entirely from the adults, and after the first hour the problem is boredom.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The first hour of an outage is an adventure for a child. The second day is not, and by the fourth everybody in the house is short-tempered and tired. Two things are actually going on: children read your face to decide how frightened to be, and children with nothing to do become a problem you then have to manage on top of everything else.

The tone thing

Kids are not listening to what you say about the storm, they are watching how you say it. Calm, matter-of-fact and slightly bored is the register to aim for. Not falsely cheerful — children see straight through that and it makes them more anxious, not less, because now the adult is lying.

Tell them what is happening in plain terms and what the plan is. The wind knocked a tree onto a wire. The power company will fix it. It might be a couple of days. We have water and food and lights and we are fine. Concrete facts and a plan is what settles a child; vagueness is what worries them.

Give them a job. Fetching batteries, holding the light, checking the thermometer in the fridge, being in charge of the lantern, counting the water jugs. A child with a role is not a child sitting in the dark worrying, and small children take a job like that extremely seriously.

Answering the hard questions

Answer honestly, at their level, without volunteering more than they asked. If you do not know how long it will be, say that, and then say what you do know. Do not promise it will be back by morning, because if it is not, they will not believe the next thing you tell them either.

Watch for the quiet ones. A child who goes silent, clingy, or regresses a bit — bedwetting, wanting to sleep in your bed, not wanting to be alone in a room — is telling you they are scared, and the answer is proximity and routine rather than a conversation about it.

The boredom, which is the real problem

All the screens are dead and the ones that work are rationed for the flashlight and the phone. Have something ready that does not need power. Keep it in the kit so it is a novelty rather than the same thing they ignored last week.

AgesWhat works
ToddlerA new small toy kept in the kit, board books, cardboard boxes, a torch to play with
Early schoolCard games, dominoes, colouring, sticker books, building blocks, shadow puppets
Older kidsBoard games, cards, a proper book, a deck of cards and someone to teach them cribbage
TeenagersA charged device with downloaded things on it, honestly, plus a real job to do outside
EverybodyCooking on the woodstove, reading aloud, telling stories, going outside when it is safe

The one that works better than any of them: reading aloud by lantern. It fills time, it is calming, and children remember it for years afterward as the good part of the storm.

Keep the routine

Meals at the usual times, bed at the usual time, the same bedtime story. Routine is the single strongest signal to a child that things are under control, and it costs nothing. Bedtime in a dark house is harder, so lean into it — a lantern on low, everybody in one room, and let them sleep together if they want to.

They will be colder than you are. Children have more surface area for their size and lose heat faster, and they will not necessarily say so. Extra layer, hat, wool socks, and for a baby an extra layer rather than loose blankets. There is more in keeping warm overnight.

Safety, with kids in the house

  1. No candles anywhere a child can reach, and no candles at all if there is a toddler. Battery lanterns only — see candles versus lanterns.
  2. Everybody gets their own light. A child with their own headlamp is a child who does not panic in a dark hallway.
  3. Talk about the generator and the woodstove specifically: hot, do not touch, do not go near.
  4. Keep them away from the downed limb and the flooded ditch. Fast water and downed wires are the two that take children.
  5. Make sure they know their address and a phone number by heart, or have it written in a pocket.

School

School will be closed and the announcement will come on the radio, which is another argument for having one — see which stations matter here. Do not try to run school at home during an outage. Everybody is tired and it is not worth the fight.

Lanterns, headlamps, batteries, cards and the everyday shelf are all here — see the storm wall. If it goes a few days, come get a cinnamon roll and let somebody else be in a warm room for an hour. Come see us.
