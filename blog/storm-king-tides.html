King Tides and Coastal Flooding, and Why the Combination Matters

A king tide on its own is a spectacle; a king tide with a storm surge and a river in flood is the day things get wet.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

King tide is not a technical term, it is a nickname for the highest predicted tides of the year, which happen when the sun, moon and earth line up and the moon is near its closest approach. They are entirely predictable — printed in a tide table years in advance — which makes them the one flooding hazard you can put on a calendar.

Why an extra foot matters

On its own, a king tide is a good day to take a photograph. The problem is that it stacks with three other things, and each of them adds height on top of the predicted number:

  • Storm surge. Low atmospheric pressure literally lets the ocean rise, roughly a foot for every inch of mercury the pressure drops. A deep low off the coast adds a foot or more before the wind does anything.
  • Wind setup and wave run-up. A sustained southwesterly piles water against the shore and pushes it up the bay and into the river mouth.
  • River discharge. After weeks of rain the Siletz is already running high and full. A high tide backs it up, the water has nowhere to go, and levels upstream rise even where the ocean never reaches.

Any one of those is manageable. All four on the same afternoon is when low-lying roads go under, boat ramps disappear, and water gets into places it has not been in years.

The practical version

  1. Know the times. The tide table tells you exactly when the peak is, and the couple of hours either side is the window.
  2. Move vehicles, equipment and anything on a low pad up the hill the evening before, not the morning of.
  3. If your driveway or road floods on a big tide, plan around it. Come home early, or do not go.
  4. Check culverts and ditches before, because a plugged culvert on a rising tide is how a driveway washes out.
  5. Never drive through water over a road, on any tide. The road under it may not still be there.

Watching them safely

King tides bring people to the beach and the jetties, and this is where the injuries happen. Sneaker waves on this coast are a genuine, regular killer — a set that runs far higher than everything before it, with no warning, on a beach that has looked fine for twenty minutes.

  • Never turn your back on the ocean, and never stand on a jetty, a rock, or a low headland during a high surf event.
  • Never let children or dogs near the water line on a big tide. A dog goes in and then a person goes in after the dog, and that is the sequence that repeats every winter.
  • Watch from high ground and well back. The photograph is the same from up there.
  • Logs on the beach are the other one. A drift log that has been sitting for months will float and roll in six inches of run-up, and it weighs a tonne. Never stand on or behind one.

The long view

King tides are useful for something beyond the day itself. What floods on a king tide today is a decent preview of what an ordinary high tide will do as sea level rises. If your access road, your well head or your outbuilding gets wet on the biggest tide of the year, that is worth knowing before you make decisions about either.

Take a photo from the same spot every year. It is the cheapest coastal monitoring programme there is and it will tell you more about your own property than any map.
