Storm Ready

Medications and Powered Medical Devices in an Outage

This is the one part of the list where running out is not an inconvenience — it is the emergency itself.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Every other item on a storm list has a workaround. You can eat cold food, read by a headlamp, and be cold for a week. There is no workaround for insulin, oxygen, or a home dialysis machine, and these are the things people plan for last.

The two-week supply, and how to get it

Aim for a two-week supply of every prescription in the house. Getting there is a conversation, not a purchase:

  1. Ask your prescriber for an emergency supply or a ninety-day fill. Many will do it if you explain the geography, and some insurers have a specific provision for exactly this.
  2. Refill at the two-thirds mark rather than when the bottle is empty. Doing that consistently builds a buffer without anyone having to authorize anything.
  3. Keep a current written list: drug name, dose, frequency, prescriber, pharmacy, and the reason you take it. On paper, in the kit and in the car, plus a photo on your phone.
  4. Photograph every label. It is the fastest way to get an emergency refill somewhere unfamiliar.
  5. Include the over-the-counter things you actually rely on: pain relief, antihistamines, a rescue inhaler, glucose tablets, epinephrine, blood thinner, anything for a chronic condition.

The ones that need to stay cold

Insulin and some other injectables need refrigeration. The good news is that most insulin is more forgiving than people assume — unopened vials and pens generally tolerate room temperature for a limited period, and an in-use pen is often kept at room temperature by design. The exact tolerance depends on the product, so read the package insert now, before you need to know, and ask your pharmacist.

Practically: a small cooler with ice packs, refreshed with ice, keeps insulin in range for days. Do not let it freeze — freezing destroys it, and a vial sitting against an ice block in a cooler can freeze. Put a towel between them and a thermometer in the cooler.

Powered devices

DeviceBackup plan
Oxygen concentratorCylinders as backup, and a conversation with the supplier before storm season
CPAP or BiPAPBattery pack rated for it. Many run from a deep-cycle battery with an inverter
NebulizerBattery model, or a spacer with a metered inhaler as the fallback
Powered wheelchair or scooterCharge fully at the watch, not the warning. Know the manual release
Home dialysisTalk to the clinic now about their outage protocol and where to go
Feeding pumpBattery, plus know the gravity or syringe method
Electric hospital bed or liftKnow the manual override and have somebody who can help

Two things worth doing regardless of the device. First, register with your utility as a medical-needs customer. It does not get your power back sooner in a big regional outage — nothing does — but it gets you on a list for notification and sometimes for a welfare check, and it costs one phone call.

Second, know what your generator or battery can actually run, and test it. A concentrator drawing several hundred watts continuously is a real load, and finding out on the night is the wrong time.

The written summary

Keep a one-page sheet for each person with a condition: diagnoses, medications and doses, allergies, devices, prescriber and pharmacy numbers, insurance details, and an emergency contact. In the kit, in the car, and with somebody who is not in your house.

If you or somebody you care for needs any of this, an outage is a good reason to leave rather than to tough it out. Have somewhere in mind — family inland, a friend with a generator, a warming centre — and go early rather than late, while the roads are open and you are not making the decision by headlamp.

The neighbor version of this

Most people who get into trouble in an outage are not the ones reading this. They are older, living alone, on a device or a medication, and they do not call anybody. If you know somebody like that on your road, knock. That is the whole system out here and it works — see checking on the neighbors.

First aid kits, over-the-counter basics, coolers, ice and power banks are all on the shelf here — see the storm wall. What we cannot do is fill a prescription, so build that buffer in the calm.
