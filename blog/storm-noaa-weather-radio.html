What a NOAA Weather Radio Does That Your Phone Cannot

It is a one-way broadcast from a transmitter that does not care whether the cell tower is up or whether you have a signal at all.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

People push back on weather radios because their phone already sends weather alerts. Fair enough, right up until the phone does not. Here is the actual difference, which is about the direction the information travels.

One-way beats two-way

Your phone alert is a two-way system. It needs a tower with power and backhaul, it needs your phone to have battery and signal, and it needs the carrier's network in between to be working. Take any one of those away and you get nothing.

A NOAA weather radio is a receiver listening to a transmitter on a mountaintop that runs on its own generator and is legally required to have backup power. Nothing has to know you exist. You are eavesdropping on a broadcast that is going out whether anyone is listening or not.

Out here, where cell coverage is thin in the valleys and the towers start running down their batteries a few hours into an outage, that difference is the whole point.

What it actually carries

  • Continuous forecasts for the coastal zone and the coastal waters, updated around the clock. Not a national summary — the actual marine and coastal forecast for this stretch.
  • Watches and warnings the second they are issued, including high wind, coastal flood, flash flood, and the ones that are not weather at all.
  • Tsunami warnings. This is the one that justifies the whole purchase on this coast, and it is why the transmitters exist.
  • Civil emergency messages — evacuations, boil water notices, road closures, AMBER alerts. Local emergency management can push into the same system.

Getting one that works here

Two features matter and the rest is noise.

Alert tone with SAME. Specific Area Message Encoding lets you program in your county code so the radio stays silent until something is issued for Lincoln County, then it wakes up and speaks. Without SAME you either listen to a drone of forecasts all day or you leave it off, which means it is off when it matters. The county code is a six-digit number you enter once. It is printed in the manual and posted at the store.

Hand crank plus battery plus USB. The crank is not a gimmick — it is what makes the radio a device that is always ready rather than a device that needs fresh batteries you did not buy. A minute of cranking gets you several minutes of listening, which is enough for a forecast cycle. Most of ours also charge a phone off the crank, slowly, and have a light built in.

Set it up before you need it

  1. Put batteries in and confirm they are fresh. Do this at the start of storm season.
  2. Find the strongest transmitter frequency at your house. Reception varies a lot in the valleys around here; try each channel and pick the clearest one, not the first one that talks.
  3. Program the county code so it only alerts on things that concern you.
  4. Set it to alert mode and put it somewhere you will hear it at three in the morning. A hallway, not a basement.
  5. Test it with the weekly test broadcast once, so you know what it sounds like and it does not scare the dog into next week.

The rest of your radio plan

A weather radio tells you what the weather is doing. It does not tell you which road is closed or when the store opens. For that you want an ordinary AM/FM radio and the local stations, which is covered separately in which radio stations matter out here. Most hand-crank units do both, which is why they are the one radio worth owning.

They live on the storm wall next to the flashlights, and Eric will program the county code into it at the counter before you leave if you ask. It takes about ninety seconds and it is the step most people skip.
