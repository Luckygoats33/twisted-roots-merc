Storm Ready

Pets in an Outage and an Evacuation

The plan that fails is the one where you have three minutes, a scared cat, and no carrier you can find.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Two things go wrong with pets in an emergency. In an outage, they get forgotten in the logistics — nobody thinks about the fact that the dog food is nearly out and the store is closed. In an evacuation, the animal panics and hides, and you lose the twenty minutes you did not have.

The pet kit

Keep it with the household kit, not in a separate place you have to remember.

The carrier problem

The most common reason people cannot evacuate with a cat is that the carrier lives in the attic and the cat associates it exclusively with the vet. The fix takes a month and costs nothing: leave the carrier out, permanently, in a room the cat uses, with a blanket in it, and feed treats in there. A carrier that is furniture is a carrier you can use in a hurry.

Same with dogs and crates. A crate-trained dog in a strange, loud, crowded place is calm. One that has never been in a crate is not.

In an outage

  • They get stressed by the dark and quiet too. No hum, no lights, a generator running outside, people moving around with headlamps. Expect odd behavior and be patient with it.
  • Keep them inside during and after a windstorm. Downed wires, broken glass, nails in debris, and unfamiliar smells that make dogs wander.
  • Leash for every outing for a few days afterward, even for a dog that never needs one. The landmarks and the scent map they navigate by have been rearranged.
  • Do not let them drink standing water after a flood. It has been through everything on the ground uphill.
  • Watch the woodstove and the candles. A cat and a candle is a house fire, and a dog's tail clears a coffee table.
  • Warmth. Short-coated dogs, old animals and anything small get cold in an unheated house. A blanket in a small space, and let them sleep with you.

Evacuating

  1. Bring them in as soon as you hear a watch, not when you decide to leave. Cats sense stress and disappear, and you cannot find a cat that does not want to be found.
  2. Crate or leash before you open any exterior door.
  3. Take the kit. It lives by the door for exactly this.
  4. Never leave an animal tied outside or shut in a crate in a place that may flood.
  5. If you truly cannot take them, leave them loose inside on an upper floor with food and water out and a note on the door saying what is in the house and how many.

Know your options before you need them. Not every shelter takes animals, though the rules have improved a lot, and hotels inland vary. A short list of pet-friendly places and the number of a boarding kennel outside the area, on paper, is a twenty-minute job that pays for itself once.

Identification

A microchip with current contact information registered is the single best thing you can do. Chips are useless if the registry still has a phone number from two houses ago. Check yours. Add a physical tag as well, because a tag works for anyone who finds the animal and a chip needs a scanner.

Pet emergency kits, food, bowls and leashes are stocked on the storm wall and in the everyday aisle. The going-bag piece covers the human half of the same decision.
