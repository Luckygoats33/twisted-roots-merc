Storm Ready

Which Radio Stations Actually Matter Out Here

When the internet is out and the phone is rationed, an AM radio and a set of AA batteries is the whole news department.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Everything you normally use to find out what is going on needs either the cell network or the internet, and in a real outage both of those degrade before the radio does. Broadcast radio is one-way, the transmitters have backup power, and a receiver runs for a week on a couple of batteries.

The three bands and what each is for

NOAA weather band. Continuous forecasts, watches, warnings, tsunami alerts and civil emergency messages for this specific coastal zone. Not on an ordinary radio — it needs a weather band receiver, which is what makes the hand-crank emergency radios worth owning. Covered properly in what a weather radio does.

AM. The one people forget. AM signals travel much further than FM, especially at night when they bounce off the ionosphere, and the big regional stations run high power with generator backup. When the local FM transmitter on a hill has been off for a day, AM out of Portland or further is still coming in clearly. AM is where you find out whether this is a Lincoln County problem or a statewide one.

FM. The local stations, which is where the information you actually need lives: road closures, school closings, shelter and warming centre locations, and where to get water and ice. A regional AM station will not tell you which road out of Siletz is open. A local FM one might.

Build the list before you need it

The single most useful ten minutes of storm prep nobody does: sit down with a radio on a normal day, run the dial, and write down the frequencies that come in clearly at your house. Reception in these valleys is peculiar and specific — a station that is strong at the store may be nothing at all four miles up the river.

Write them on a card and tape it to the radio.

Write downWhy
Best NOAA weather channel at your houseReception varies by valley. Find the strong one now
Two or three local FM news or talk stationsClosures, shelters, local specifics
Two AM stations, one regional and one distantRegional picture and a backup when local is down
The public radio frequencyGenerally stays on air and covers the state
Any local low-power or tribal stationOften the most locally useful thing on the dial

The radio to own

One hand-crank unit with AM, FM and the weather band, an alert tone, a light and a USB output. That single device covers every case, and the crank means it works whether or not you remembered batteries.

Back it up with something dumber. A cheap AM/FM pocket radio and a pack of AA batteries costs almost nothing and will play for weeks. The vehicle radio is the other one people forget — it is a good receiver on a good antenna and it works whenever the truck does. Ten minutes with the engine running gets you the news and charges a phone at the same time.

How to listen without going mad

Set a rhythm rather than leaving it on all day. Top of the hour, and again in the evening. Continuous storm coverage is mostly repetition, and listening to it for four days straight is genuinely bad for your mood and your batteries.

Write down what you hear — road closures and their times, restoration estimates, where the water is being handed out. In a house with no lights and no clock you will not remember by evening whether they said Thursday or Tuesday.

What radio will not tell you

It will not tell you whether your particular road is open, and it will not tell you when your particular line is coming back. That information comes from neighbors, from whoever just drove in from that direction, and from the counter at the store — which is why the neighbor network is not a sentimental idea, it is the only source of local detail there is.

Radios, batteries and 12-volt adapters are on the storm wall. Ours have the weather band and the crank, and Eric will find the strongest local channel with you at the counter before you leave.
