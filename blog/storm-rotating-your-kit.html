Storm Ready

Rotating a Kit So It Still Works in Five Years

A kit is not a purchase, it is a habit, and the habit only sticks if you attach it to something you already do.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Kits fail quietly. Nobody notices that the water tastes of plastic, the bars went hard, the power bank is at zero and the headlamp has a corroded battery welded into it, because nobody has opened the tote in four years. A kit that has never been checked is not a kit, it is a box of assumptions.

Attach it to something you already do

The reason kit maintenance schedules fail is that they are their own appointment, and their own appointment is the first thing to get skipped. Tie it to a habit that already happens twice a year without anybody deciding to do it. The clock change works. So does the weekend you check the smoke alarms, or the first day you light the woodstove, or the start of hunting season.

Do the smoke alarms, the CO alarms and the kit in the same hour. It takes about forty minutes and then you are done for six months.

The twice-a-year list

  1. Batteries. Pull the alkalines out of anything that has been sitting. Test the lights. Replace anything corroded, and swap lithium primaries into the kit lights.
  2. Rechargeables. Charge every power bank, headlamp, jump pack and tool battery to full. Then the drawer version is at least a known quantity.
  3. Water. Look at the containers. If they are chlorinated tap water, sealed and stored dark, leave them. If they are well water, dump and refill. Replace anything cloudy, weeping or that has been sitting on concrete.
  4. Food. Check dates. Pull anything within six months of expiry and eat it, replacing as you go. This is why the kit should be food you actually like.
  5. Medication. Rotate anything expiring, and re-establish the two-week buffer on prescriptions.
  6. First aid. Replace what got used, check dates on ointments and any medications inside it.
  7. Fuel. Rotate the gas cans into the truck and refill with fresh stabilized fuel. Check propane cylinders.
  8. Documents. Update the phone list, the medication list and the insurance details. Numbers change.
  9. Clothing and footwear. Particularly children's, who will have grown a size. Check the boots and the rain gear still fit everybody.
  10. Cash. Put back what got borrowed. Everybody borrows it.

Test it, do not just inventory it

The one step that separates a real kit from a tidy one. Once a year, use it for an evening. Turn the breaker off after dinner and run the house on the kit for three hours.

You will find things. The lantern nobody can figure out. The camp stove missing its regulator. That the can opener is still in its packaging and is terrible. That there is exactly one headlamp for four people. That nobody knows where the water shutoff is. Every one of those is much cheaper to discover on a Tuesday evening in the autumn with the store still open.

Kids generally enjoy it, which is a bonus, and it makes the real thing considerably less frightening for them when it comes.

Keep a list on the lid

Tape a sheet to the inside of the tote lid: what is in it, what needs rotating and roughly when. Then the check is reading a list rather than reconstructing what past-you was thinking, and anybody in the house can do it.

Write down what you took out and did not put back. That single line is the difference between a kit that degrades and one that does not.

Everything on the rotation list is stocked here year-round, not just in storm season — see the storm wall. And the week after a blow is the easiest time to do all of this, which is covered in restocking after a storm.
