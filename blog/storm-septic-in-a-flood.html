Septic Systems in a Flood, and Why to Stop Flushing

A saturated drainfield cannot take another drop, and the place the water backs up to is your bathroom floor.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A septic system works by letting liquid soak away into the ground. When the ground is already full of water, it cannot. That is the entire problem, and it produces two distinct failures: sewage backing up into the house, and untreated effluent surfacing in the yard.

The rule that matters

If the ground over your drainfield is saturated or standing in water, minimize what you put into the system. Every gallon you send down the drain has nowhere to go and adds to the pressure in a tank that is already full.

That means: no laundry, no dishwasher, no long showers, and the yellow-mellow rule on flushing. It is unpleasant for a few days. It is considerably less unpleasant than a backup through the lowest drain in the house, which is usually a shower or a basement floor drain and is a professional cleanup.

Signs the system is in trouble

  • Drains running slow all over the house at once, rather than one fixture
  • Gurgling from drains or toilets when another fixture is used
  • Toilets that flush weakly or back up
  • Soggy ground, unusually green grass, or standing water over the drainfield
  • Sewage smell outside near the tank or field
  • The alarm going off on a pump system

That last one is the one people miss in an outage. If your system has a pump — either a pump tank pushing effluent uphill to a field, or a grinder pump — it stops working the instant the power does. The alarm that would tell you is also on power, unless it has a battery. On a pump system, an outage means the tank is filling with nothing leaving, and you have hours, not days.

If it backs up or floods

  1. Stop using water in the house. All of it.
  2. Do not open the septic tank lid. Never, in any circumstance. The gases in a tank are lethal within seconds and people have died leaning over an open tank.
  3. Do not pump the tank out while the ground is saturated. An empty tank in waterlogged ground can float right out of the ground, break its pipes, and turn a bad week into a replacement.
  4. Keep everyone, especially children and pets, away from any surfaced sewage in the yard.
  5. Do not drive or park on a wet drainfield. Saturated soil compacts easily and compacted soil does not drain, permanently.
  6. If floodwater got into the house, treat everything it touched as contaminated. Gloves, boots, bleach solution, and throw out porous things.

After the water goes down

Give it time. The drainfield needs the water table to drop before it will work again, and that can take days after the rain stops. Bring the household water use back gradually rather than doing six loads of laundry the first dry afternoon.

Then get it inspected. What you are checking for is whether silt got into the tank or the field, whether the baffles are intact, and whether the field was damaged. A system that flooded and was then pumped and inspected usually carries on for years. One that flooded and was ignored often fails within a couple of seasons.

If the system has an electrical component — a pump, a control panel, an aerator — have it checked before you rely on it, because those get wet and fail quietly.

The alternative that saves the system

If the power is out and the ground is saturated, an emergency toilet kit — a bucket, heavy liners, and something absorbent — takes the entire load off the septic system for as long as it needs. It is not glamorous and it works, and it is a lot cheaper than a drainfield.

Emergency toilet kits, heavy contractor bags, bleach and gloves are on the storm wall. If your water problem is coming in rather than failing to go out, the water intrusion piece is the one you want.
