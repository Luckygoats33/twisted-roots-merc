Storm Ready

Tarping a Roof Leak Before It Gets Dark

The tarp is the easy part; getting it to stay on in a coastal wind is the part that goes wrong.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A tarp on a roof is not a repair. It is a way to stop the inside of your house getting ruined while you wait however many weeks it takes to get a roofer out here after a big blow. Done badly it lasts one night, comes loose at three in the morning and flogs itself to pieces. Done properly it lasts until spring.

First, decide whether to go up at all

Wet moss on a coastal roof is about as slippery as ice, and a mossy north-facing roof in January is genuinely dangerous. Do not go up in wind, do not go up in the dark, do not go up alone, and do not go up on a steep pitch or anything above one story unless you actually know what you are doing.

An alternative that costs you nothing in dignity: put buckets under the leak, poke a small hole in a bulging ceiling to let it drain in one controlled place rather than bringing the whole sheet of drywall down, move what matters out of the room, and deal with the roof in daylight. Water in a ceiling is a problem. A person off a roof is a different category of problem.

What you need

ItemWhy
Tarp, at least 4 ft larger than the damage on every sideIt has to run past the ridge and past the eave
Two 8 ft 2x4s, or four for a big oneThese are what actually hold it. Not the grommets
Roofing nails or screws with washersScrews back out cleanly later
Rope or ratchet strapsBackup, and for tying off over the ridge
Utility knife, hammer or driver, work glovesObvious, always missing

The method that holds

The trick is that you do not fasten through the tarp into the roof. You roll the edge of the tarp around a board and screw the board down. That spreads the load along the whole edge instead of tearing out at the grommets, which is how every failed tarp job fails.

  1. Get the tarp over the ridge if you possibly can, at least a couple of feet down the far side. Water runs downhill and any upper edge that stops short of the ridge is a funnel pointed into your attic.
  2. Lay the tarp out, aligned, with the excess hanging past the eave at the bottom.
  3. At the top edge, roll the tarp around a 2x4 two or three full turns, like winding a flag onto a pole.
  4. Screw through the rolled bundle into the roof deck, into rafters if you can find them, every couple of feet. Yes, you are putting holes in the roof. They are above the tarp line, they are small, and they are not the problem you have today.
  5. Do the same at the bottom edge with a second 2x4, and at the sides on a wide one. Every free edge gets a board.
  6. Any loose edge left flapping will be gone by morning. Run a board, a strap, or at minimum a run of screws with washers along it.
  7. Do not weight it with bricks or firewood. Wind lifts the tarp, the weight comes off, and now you have a projectile.

The details that decide whether it lasts

  • Shingle-lap the seams. If you need two tarps, overlap them so the uphill one lies on top of the downhill one, minimum two feet.
  • Sweep the roof first. Fir needles, cones and grit under a tarp abrade it through in a couple of weeks of flapping.
  • Tension it. A tarp with slack in it flogs, and flogging is what destroys tarps and loosens fasteners. It should be drum-tight.
  • Nothing sharp underneath. A lifted shingle edge or a nail head will wear a hole from below.
  • Check it after every blow. Ten minutes with a screw gun beats a second interior soaking.

Then photograph it

Before you tarp, after you tarp, wide shots and close ones, and the inside damage too. Insurers expect you to mitigate further damage and will generally reimburse reasonable materials, but only with a record. There is a whole piece on this in taking photos your insurer will accept.

Tarps in 10x12 and 20x30, roofing nails, roof patch sealant, straps and the 2x4s are all here — tarps on the storm wall, lumber out in the yard. Ask and we will cut the boards to length for you before you go.
