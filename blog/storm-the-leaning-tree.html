Storm Ready

The Tree Leaning Toward Your House

Around here trees do not usually snap, they tip, and the ground tells you it is coming before the tree does.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Everyone out here has a tree they think about when the wind comes up. Most of them are fine. Some of them are not, and the difference is visible if you know what you are looking at.

Why they come down here specifically

Douglas fir and Sitka spruce on this coast are shallow-rooted relative to their height. The soil is often a thin layer over clay or hardpan, and roots spread wide rather than deep. After weeks of rain that soil goes to the consistency of pudding, and then a fifty mile an hour wind arrives from the southwest and the tree does not break — it levers over, root plate and all, and takes a slab of ground up with it.

That is why the wind number in the forecast matters less than the rain that preceded it. It is also why a tree that has stood through twenty winters can go over in a wind it has handled before.

What to actually look at

Walk your trees on a calm dry day and look for these, in roughly the order of how much they should worry you:

SignWhat it meansUrgency
Soil heaved or cracked on the uphill side of the trunkThe root plate is already movingImmediate. This is a tree that is failing now
Mushrooms or conks growing at the base or on the trunkRoot or heart rot. The fungus is the fruit of a much bigger problemHigh. Get somebody to look
A cavity, split, or seam running up the trunkStructural failure in progressHigh
A lean that is new, or a trunk curved at the base but straight aboveThe curved base is old and settled. A straight lean is recentNew lean is high, old curve is usually fine
Dead top, no needles in the upper thirdDying. Tops break out and fall a long wayModerate to high
Large dead limbs hung up in the canopyWidowmakers. They come down on a calm dayModerate, and do not stand under it
Ivy grown thick up the trunkHides defects, adds sail area and weightLow but pull it off
Roots cut by a driveway, trench or new padAnchorage removed on one sideDepends which side the wind comes from

Work out where it would land

The rough measure: a tree can reach roughly its own height in any direction, and a falling tree does not always go the way it leans, because the wind and the crown weight get a vote. Pace it out. If the tree is a hundred and twenty feet and your bedroom is eighty feet away, it is a target.

Then ask what else is in the fall zone — the power drop to the house, the propane tank, the well head, the drive that is your only way out. A tree that would land in the pasture is a firewood opportunity. The same tree pointed at your service drop is a different conversation.

Who to call, and when

If it is near the house, near a line, or over sixty feet, this is not a homeowner job and it is not a job for a friend with a saw. A tree that big holds a startling amount of stored energy and the ways it can go wrong are not intuitive. A certified arborist will assess it, and a good faller will put it exactly where they said they would.

If the tree threatens a power line, call the utility first, not a tree service. Trees contacting or threatening distribution lines are usually their responsibility and often their cost, and nobody should be putting a saw near an energized conductor.

Doing it before the wind rather than after

The cost difference is not subtle. Taking a hazard tree down on a dry weekend in summer is scheduled work at a normal rate. The same tree, on your roof, in January, in a week when every crew on the coast is booked, is emergency work at emergency prices, and you are also paying for a roof.

Late summer through early autumn is the window: dry ground, crews available, and enough time to buck and stack the wood so it is seasoned by the following winter.

If it comes down anyway

Then the questions are whether it is touching a line, whether anything under it is loaded and under tension, and whether you have any business cutting it — all of which is in clearing a tree off the drive. And your saw wants to be ready before that morning, not that morning: chainsaw readiness covers it.

Bar oil, mix, files, chaps, wedges and gloves are stocked year-round and stacked deeper in storm season — see the storm wall and the Fix It side.
