Clearing a Tree Off the Drive Without Getting Hurt

Every trunk lying on the ground is under tension somewhere, and reading which way it will move is the whole job.

4 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The tree is down, the drive is blocked, and you have a saw. The temptation is to start cutting in the middle. Do not start anywhere until you have walked around the whole thing and worked out what it is going to do when you cut it.

Three checks before the saw comes out

  1. Is it touching a power line, or is a line touching it? Assume every wire is live. That includes a wire lying in wet grass, and a phone or cable line that a power line has fallen across. Stay back the length of the tree, keep everybody else back, and call the utility. This is the one that kills people and it is entirely a matter of not walking up to it.
  2. Is anything above you? Look up. Broken limbs hung in the canopy, a top that snapped and is resting somewhere, another tree leaning on this one. A hung-up tree is the most dangerous thing in the woods and it is not a homeowner job.
  3. Where is the root plate? A tipped-up root wad is holding tonnes and it is spring-loaded. Cut the trunk free and the whole plate can slam back into its hole, and anything in that arc goes with it. Work from the top of the tree toward the roots, never the other way, and never stand in the pit.

Reading tension and compression

This is the skill the whole job turns on. Any log that is supported at its ends and unsupported in the middle is in compression on top and tension on the bottom. Any log cantilevered off a support — hanging over a bank, or held up by its own limbs — is the opposite: tension on top, compression on the bottom.

Cut into the compression side and the kerf closes on your bar and pins it. Cut into the tension side and the wood splits and the log moves, fast, usually toward you.

The answer is the two-cut method every time:

  • Supported at both ends (compression on top): cut down from the top about a third of the way, then finish from underneath. The bottom cut releases the tension into the relief you already made.
  • Supported at one end, overhanging (tension on top): cut up from underneath about a third, then finish from the top down to meet it.
  • Either way, leave a hinge of an inch or two and let the weight finish the break, or use a plastic wedge in the kerf to hold it open.

Working position

  • Stand on the uphill side, always. A log that rolls will roll downhill and it does not care that you are there.
  • Cut at waist height where you can. Bending over a saw at ankle height is where kickback goes into a leg.
  • Keep the tip of the bar out of the cut. The upper quadrant of the bar nose is the kickback zone and it is instantaneous.
  • Both hands on the saw, thumb wrapped around the front handle, elbows slightly bent.
  • Have a way out. Two clear escape paths, and nothing to trip over in either.
  • Never cut above shoulder height and never on a ladder. Ever.
  • Do not work alone if you can avoid it. If you must, tell somebody where you are and when you will call.

Limbing, in order

Take the limbs off the uphill side first, working from the base of the tree toward the top, keeping the trunk between you and the limb you are cutting where possible. Limbs on the underside are the springy ones — they may be holding the whole trunk up, and cutting the wrong one drops several hundred pounds of tree onto whatever is under it, including your foot.

Any limb that is bent into an arc is a spring pole. It has enough stored energy to break a jaw. Shave the outside of the bend with small cuts, working gradually along it to release it a little at a time, and stand out of the arc while you do it.

When to stop and call somebody

Stop if the tree is hung up in another tree, if it is on a roof or a vehicle with anybody's weight on it, if it is on or near a wire, if it is bigger than your bar and you are guessing, or if you are tired. Day-two tired is when the accidents happen, and there is no prize for clearing it before the crew gets there.

Get the photos before you cut, for insurance — see insurance photos. Chaps, wedges, files, gloves and bar oil are on the storm wall, and chainsaw readiness covers getting the saw right before the morning you need it.
