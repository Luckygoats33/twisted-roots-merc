Storm Ready

Two Weeks Ready: Building the Kit Oregon Actually Recommends

The state stopped saying seventy-two hours a while back, and if you have driven Highway 229 in a windstorm you know why.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Oregon emergency management does not tell people to be three days ready anymore. The guidance out here is two weeks ready, and the reason is geography. Small towns at the end of a road, one or two ways in, bridges over saturated ground, and a Cascadia scenario where outside help is measured in weeks rather than days. Siletz checks every one of those boxes.

Two weeks sounds enormous until you write it down. Then it is mostly water and a shelf.

Build it in four trips, not one

The reason most people never finish a kit is that they price the whole thing at once, wince, and put it off. Break it up.

  1. Water and light first. They are the two things with no substitute. A case of bottled water and a couple of five-gallon jugs, plus a headlamp and a lantern per person.
  2. Then the radio and the power bank. Information and communication. A hand-crank NOAA radio does not care that it has been in a closet for a year.
  3. Then food and the can opener. Buy the food you eat anyway, just more of it, and put it somewhere you will rotate it.
  4. Then warmth, first aid and sanitation. The unglamorous half. It is also the half that decides whether two weeks is miserable or merely boring.

Where it lives matters

A kit in the back of a closet under the Christmas decorations is not a kit, it is a memory. Put it in one place, low, near a door, in something you can carry. A plastic tote with a lid works. Label the outside so somebody else in the house can find it in the dark.

Keep the water separate and off concrete — concrete floors sweat and jugs sitting directly on them pick up odd tastes and can weep at the seams. A scrap of plywood under them fixes it.

The part nobody budgets for

Two weeks of prescriptions. Pet food. Diapers and formula if that applies. Contact lens solution. These are the items where running out is not an inconvenience, it is the emergency, and they are the ones that never make it onto a generic list.

We keep most of this stocked year-round and stack it deeper when the forecast turns — see what is on the wall right now before you drive over, and if there is something we should be carrying and are not, tell Carrie. That is genuinely how the shelf list gets written.
