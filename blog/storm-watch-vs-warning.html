Storm Ready

Watch, Warning, Advisory: What the Words Actually Mean

A watch means get ready, a warning means it is happening, and an advisory means it will not kill you but it will ruin your day.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The words are not interchangeable and they are not ranked by how scary they sound. They describe two different things: how confident the forecasters are, and how bad it is.

The three words

Watch. Conditions are favourable. It might happen, in the next day or two, somewhere in this area. A watch is issued when confidence is moderate and the timing is loose. This is your prep window, and it is the alert most worth acting on, because it is the only one that arrives while you still have time to do something.

Warning. It is happening now, or it is imminent and certain, and it is dangerous. Confidence is high, the area is specific, the timing is short. A warning is not a prep window. It is a take-action window.

Advisory. It is happening or about to, and it is a genuine inconvenience or hazard, but below warning criteria. Not life-threatening for most people, but capable of ruining a drive or taking down a limb.

ConfidencePossibleLikely or occurringOccurring or imminent
Timing12-48 hours outNow to 12 hoursNow to a few hours
SeverityCould be significantNuisance to moderateSignificant, dangerous
What you doPrepare. Fill water, fuel up, charge thingsAdjust plans, drive carefullyAct. Shelter, evacuate, stay off the roads

The ones that matter on this coast

  • High Wind Watch and Warning. The workhorse. Warning criteria is roughly sustained winds around forty miles an hour, or gusts around fifty-eight. This is the one that takes the power out.
  • Wind Advisory. Below that, and still enough to bring limbs down on saturated ground.
  • Flood Watch and Warning. Watch means the rain is coming and the rivers will respond. Warning means a specific river is forecast to reach flood stage, and the forecast will name the river and the stage.
  • Flash Flood Warning. Different animal. Sudden, fast, usually a creek or a slide-dammed drainage. Act immediately, get to high ground.
  • Coastal Flood Advisory and Warning. Tidal overwash of low-lying areas — see the king tide piece.
  • High Surf Advisory and Warning. Sneaker waves, run-up, log hazard. Stay off the beach and the jetties.
  • Winter Storm Warning. Rare at sea level here, common in the coast range passes. Read it as a road closure warning.
  • Tsunami Watch, Advisory, Warning. This one uses the same three words for something completely different. See earthquake and tsunami basics, and know that for a local quake the ground shaking is the warning and you do not wait for anything official.

What a watch is for

The whole value of the system is the watch, and most people ignore it because nothing is happening yet. That is exactly the point. When a high wind watch goes up for the coast, that is the afternoon to:

  1. Fill the water containers and the stock troughs.
  2. Fill the vehicles, and the gas cans if you run a generator.
  3. Charge every power bank, phone and rechargeable light.
  4. Do the yard walk and put away anything that flies — see wind-proofing the yard.
  5. Get cash out while the card readers work.
  6. Turn the fridge and freezer to their coldest setting.
  7. Refill prescriptions if you are near the end of one.

All of that takes an hour and it is the whole difference between a storm and an ordeal. By the time the warning is issued, the store is busy, the roads are worse, and half that list is no longer possible.

Where to get them

A NOAA weather radio with the county code programmed is the only source that does not need the cell network or the power grid. Phone alerts are good until they are not. Local AM and FM carry them too, which is covered in the radio stations piece.
