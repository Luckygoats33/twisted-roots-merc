Making Questionable Water Safe to Drink

Boiling, bleach and filters each fix a different problem, and using the wrong one is worse than doing nothing.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

There are three things in water you might need to deal with: living things, chemicals, and mud. No single method handles all three, which is why the advice sounds contradictory until you sort out which problem you have.

Start by getting the mud out

Every method below works better on clear water, and some do not work at all on cloudy water. Chlorine gets used up by organic material before it ever reaches the bacteria. Filters clog. So the first step is always the same: let it settle in a bucket for an hour, then pour the clear top off through a coffee filter, a bandana, or a clean t-shirt. This does nothing for safety by itself. It makes everything after it work.

Boiling

Boiling is the gold standard for anything alive — bacteria, viruses, giardia, cryptosporidium. Bring it to a rolling boil for one minute. That is the whole instruction. There is no elevation around Siletz that changes it. Longer does not make it safer, it just uses fuel.

Boiling does nothing for chemicals, fuel, or salt. In fact it concentrates them as the water evaporates. If your water source is downhill of a flooded shop with drums in it, boiling is the wrong tool.

Bleach

Plain unscented household bleach, five to six percent sodium hypochlorite, no additives, no scent, no splash-free formula. Check the label — the concentration varies and the dose depends on it.

WaterClear waterCloudy water
1 quart2 drops4 drops
1 gallon8 drops (about 1/8 tsp)16 drops (about 1/4 tsp)
5 gallons1/2 teaspoon1 teaspoon

Stir, then let it stand thirty minutes. It should smell faintly of chlorine when you are done. If it does not, dose it again and wait another fifteen. If it smells strongly of chlorine, let it sit uncovered for a while and it will gas off.

Bleach loses strength on the shelf — a jug that has been open in a cabinet for a year is noticeably weaker. Buy a fresh one for the kit and use the old one for laundry.

Filters

A pump or gravity filter is the practical answer for creek water, because it handles the sediment and the organisms in one step and the water comes out tasting like water rather than a swimming pool. What matters is the pore size. Anything rated to remove giardia and cryptosporidium covers what you would actually get out of a coastal creek. Viruses are smaller than most filters catch, but they are a much smaller risk in rural surface water here than in a flood carrying sewage.

If you have filtered flood water rather than creek water, filter it and then treat it with bleach anyway. Belt and suspenders, and floods carry things creeks do not.

What not to do

  • Do not drink flood water, filtered or otherwise, if you can avoid it at all. It has been through septic systems, fuel tanks, and everything on the ground between here and the top of the hill.
  • Do not use pool chlorine tablets. Wrong chemistry, wrong dose, stabilizers you do not want to drink.
  • Do not rely on a water heater tank without checking the bottom first. There are forty to eighty gallons in there and it is drinkable, but a heater that has never been drained has an inch of sediment in the bottom. Shut off the power or gas to it first, then draw from the drain valve.
  • Do not forget the toilet tank — the tank, not the bowl. Clean water, a couple of gallons, as long as nobody has put a blue puck in it.

Bleach is on the everyday shelf, pump filters are on the Hunt & Field wall, and if you would rather just store it in the first place, storing water when you are on a well covers that side. Both are worth having.
