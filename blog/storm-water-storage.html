Storing Water When You Are on a Well

No power means no pump means no water, and the fix costs about twenty dollars and takes an afternoon.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Town water and well water behave completely differently in an outage, and most people out here are on a well. When the power drops, your pump stops. What is left in the pressure tank is what you have, and that is usually somewhere between two and six gallons of usable water. One flush and a couple of hand washes.

How much and what in

The planning number is one gallon per person per day for drinking and cooking, and a separate, larger, dirtier pile for flushing and washing. For a two-person house working toward two weeks ready, that is twenty-eight gallons of drinking water. Six five-gallon jugs and a case or two of bottled gets you there.

Filling it right

  1. Wash the container with dish soap and rinse it until you cannot smell soap.
  2. Sanitize with a teaspoon of unscented household bleach in a quart of water. Slosh it over every interior surface, then empty it and let it air dry.
  3. Fill from the tap, all the way to the top, leaving as little air as possible.
  4. Cap it tight, label it with a marker, and put it somewhere dark and cool. Light grows algae. Heat and cold cycling stresses the plastic.
  5. Off the bare concrete. A pallet or a piece of scrap plywood.

Water from a chlorinated municipal tap will keep indefinitely stored this way. Water from your own well has no residual disinfectant, so treat stored well water as something to swap out a couple of times a year, or add a drop of unscented bleach per gallon when you fill.

The flush problem

Drinking water is the easy half. The gallons add up fast on the other side: an older toilet uses three and a half gallons a flush, a newer one about a gallon and a half. Two people doing what two people do is fifteen to twenty gallons a day if you flush every time, which nobody does after day one.

The practical answer is a bucket and a rain barrel. Pour a couple of gallons straight into the bowl fast and it will flush on gravity alone, no tank involved. That water can be dirty — rainwater, tub water, creek water. Save the clean stuff for your mouth.

The thing to do when the forecast turns

Before the wind arrives, not during: fill the tub, fill every jug that is empty, run the washing machine so you start the outage with clean clothes, and top off the animals' water. It costs you twenty minutes and it is the difference between an outage and a problem.

Jugs, bottled by the case, and the rest of the water shelf live at the front of the store. If you want to filter rather than store, a pump filter off the Hunt & Field wall handles creek water — there is more on that in making questionable water safe.
