Storm Ready

What a Store Can and Cannot Do in an Outage

We can stay open on a generator and take cash; we cannot conjure a pallet of D cells out of a closed highway.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

People are generous about what they assume a store can do in a storm and it is worth being straight about where the limits actually are, because knowing them changes what you do the day before.

What we can do

  • Stay open. The generator runs the coolers, the lights and the ovens. When the power is out here, the merc is a warm room with coffee and that is deliberate.
  • Take cash, and take a card if the hotspot holds. The register goes to a paper pad and a calculator, which works fine and is slower, so be patient with the line.
  • Stock deep before it hits. When the forecast turns, the reorder minimums jump — batteries by the case rather than the pack, tarps on a pallet on the floor, water stacked by the door. That is the whole point of the storm page, so you can see what is actually there before you drive over.
  • Keep the kitchen going. A hot meal and somewhere warm to sit for an hour matters more on day three than anything else we sell.
  • Be a message board. Who is out, which road is open, who needs a hand. Leave a note, take a note.
  • Let you charge a phone. There is a strip on the counter. Nobody has ever been charged for it.

What we cannot do

  • Restock mid-storm. Deliveries come over the same roads you do. If 229 is closed or the coast range is down, nothing is coming, and no amount of calling changes it. Whatever is on the shelf Friday afternoon is what there is until the road opens.
  • Hold things back for people who did not come in. We do not run a reserve list in a storm. It is not fair to the person standing in front of us with wet boots.
  • Be the last flashlight. We are a small store with a small back room. When the wall is bought out, it is bought out.
  • Sell fuel, propane refills without a tank, or prescriptions. Different licences, different businesses. We do exchange 20 lb tanks and stock 1 lb cylinders.
  • Fix your roof. We will sell you a tarp and lend you a screw gun and tell you how — see tarping a roof leak — but Eric is one man with a store to run.
  • Know when your power is coming back. We hear the same estimates you do, from the same radio.

What actually runs out, and in what order

From watching it happen, the order is remarkably consistent:

OrderWhat goesWhy it is first
1D cells, then AAEverybody's lantern takes a size they do not have
2Bar and chain oilNobody checks until the saw is running
3Bagged iceThe freezer panic on day two
4Tarps, then rope and strapsThe tarp goes fast, the tie-downs go faster
51 lb propane cylindersCamp stoves come out when the range will not light
6Bottled waterWells go down with the power
7Lantern batteries againBecause everybody bought lanterns on day one

None of that is a scolding. It is a shopping list for a dry weekend in the autumn, which is the entire argument of this section of the site.

Buy it in the calm

The honest ask: get the boring half of your kit on a quiet afternoon when nobody is in the store and nothing is happening. Batteries, a spare tarp, bar oil, a headlamp, some water. It costs the same in September as it does in the middle of a warning, except in September we have it.

Then when the wind comes, you come in for coffee and a cinnamon roll instead of standing in front of an empty peg hook.

Tell us what to carry

Every storm teaches us something we did not have. Carrie writes it down and it goes on the shelf permanently, for everybody, not just the person who asked. If you came in during a blow and we did not have the thing you needed, that is genuinely the most useful thing you can tell us — come see us or leave a note at the counter.

What happens after the road opens is its own subject: restocking after a storm.
