Storm Ready

What Actually Fails First When the Power Goes Out Here

The order things quit is more predictable than you'd think, and knowing it tells you what to do in the first ten minutes.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

An outage on this coast is not one event. It is a sequence, and it runs in roughly the same order every time. If you know the order you stop being surprised, and being surprised is what makes people do dumb things at nine at night with a candle.

The first sixty seconds

Lights, obviously. Then the things people forget are electric: the cordless phone base, the garage door opener, the microwave clock you use to tell time, and the router. Your gas furnace also stops right now, because the blower and the ignition board both run on house current. A gas fireplace with an old millivolt pilot will keep burning. A pellet stove will not — it needs power for the auger and the fan, and it will smolder and smoke until you open a window.

If your garage door is the only way your truck gets out, go pull the red release cord before you do anything else. Doing it in the dark, later, with a flashlight in your teeth, is worse.

The first ten minutes

If you are on a well, you have no water at all. Not low water — none. What is in the pressure tank gives you one toilet flush and a hand wash, and then that is the end of it. Fill something now: the tub, a couple of pots, the five-gallon jugs if you keep them. Town water in Siletz keeps running for a while because it is gravity and stored volume, but a long outage can still put a boil notice out.

Open the fridge exactly once, take out what you want for the next several hours, and then leave it shut. Every open costs you.

Hour two through hour eight

Cell service is the one people misjudge. Towers have battery backup, generally something like four to eight hours, and the bigger sites have a generator behind the fence. So your phone works fine at first and then quietly gets worse. When a tower is struggling, a text will often go through when a call will not, because a text is a few hundred bytes that will keep retrying in the background. Call once, then switch to texting.

Home internet dies sooner than that. The battery in a fiber terminal or cable modem is measured in a couple of hours, and half of them are dead batteries nobody has replaced.

Day one into day two

Now it is about temperature and food. A full freezer holds around forty-eight hours if you leave the door alone; a half-full one holds about half that. The house itself loses heat slower than people expect — a closed-up place drops a few degrees a day in coastal winter, not a few degrees an hour, because it rarely gets truly cold here. It gets wet, which is a different problem.

Card readers at every store between here and Newport are down or running on a phone hotspot, and the fuel pumps at the highway stations need power to move gas. This is the point where having cash in the house stops being quaint.

What this means for the first ten minutes

  1. Pull the garage door release.
  2. Fill water containers if you are on a well.
  3. Get one good light on per person — a headlamp beats a flashlight, because you need your hands.
  4. Close the fridge and put a note on it.
  5. Text somebody outside the area that you are fine, before the tower gets busy.
  6. Find the radio and find out how big this is.

That is it. Everything else can wait until you have light and know what you are dealing with. If any of that list made you realize something is missing, the wall is by the front door and we are open normal hours until the wind says otherwise — come see us.
