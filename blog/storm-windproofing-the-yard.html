Storm Ready

Wind-Proofing the Yard, the Trailer and the Outbuildings

Almost everything expensive that breaks in a windstorm was something loose that hit something solid.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Walk your property on a calm day and ask one question about every object: what does this do at seventy miles an hour. Most storm damage on a rural place is not the wind hitting the building. It is the wind picking something up and throwing it into the building.

The walk-around, in order of how far things fly

Patio furniture, especially chairsThrough a windowInside, or stacked and strapped to a post

Sheets of plywood, siding offcuts, roofing leaning on a wallGenuinely dangerous. These become bladesFlat on the ground, weighted, or inside
Ladders leaning upFalls, breaks things, and hurts somebodyDown and flat
Hanging baskets, wind chimes, flagsSnap lines, break windowsDown

The trailer

An empty utility trailer is a wing. It has a large flat area, it is light, and it sits on wheels that let it move. Trailers get pushed across yards and into fences regularly, and a travel trailer or an RV is worse because the sidewall area is enormous.

  1. Park it nose into the prevailing wind, which here is out of the south or southwest.
  2. Hitch it to the truck, or drop the tongue onto a jack and chock all four wheels.
  3. Put the stabilizer jacks down on an RV so it cannot rock.
  4. Retract every awning. An awning left out in a windstorm is a total loss and it will take a chunk of the sidewall with it.
  5. Strap anything on the deck. Loose gear in a trailer bed comes out.
  6. Ratchet straps over the frame to ground anchors or to a fixed object if you have a place to tie to.
  7. Move it out from under trees if there is anywhere to move it to. This is the one that matters most.

Outbuildings

Sheds fail in a predictable order: the door first, then the roof edge, then the whole building slides off its skids. All three are cheap to prevent.

  • Latch and secure the door. A shed door that blows open pressurizes the building from the inside and lifts the roof off. A hasp and a screw is enough.
  • Check the roof edge. Loose drip edge, lifted shingles, and unsecured metal panel edges are where the wind gets a grip. Screws with washers, not nails, on metal.
  • Tie it down. A shed on skids should be anchored — ground anchors and cable over the frame, or lag bolts into a pier. A shed with no anchoring at all is a large box waiting to move.
  • Clear what is against it. Anything leaning on a shed becomes a lever.

The greenhouse, the carport and the canopy

Pop-up canopies and portable carports are not designed for coastal wind and no amount of staking changes that. If a real blow is coming, take the cover off. A frame with no fabric survives; a frame with fabric becomes a tumbleweed made of steel tube. It takes fifteen minutes and it is the difference between owning one next spring and not.

Fences and gates

A solid privacy fence takes the full wind load and hands it to the posts. Check for posts that wobble — that is a post that is rotted at grade or a footing that has been softened by saturated ground — and gates that are not latched. A gate swinging in a wind tears its own hinges out and then lets the stock out, which is a much bigger problem than the gate.

Straps, bungees, ground anchors, zip ties, paracord, tarps, rope and fence hardware are on the Fix It wall, and posts and gravel are out in the yard. The animals are their own subject — see livestock and chickens in a windstorm.
