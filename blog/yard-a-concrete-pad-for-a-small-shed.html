Pouring a Small Pad for a Shed

The base under the concrete decides whether the pad lasts. Most of a good pad is gravel, a rake and a tamper.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A 8x10 pad is about the biggest thing a person should try to pour out of bags in one day, and even that is a long day. Here is the honest version of the job, including the part that determines whether it holds up, which is not the concrete.

Do the arithmetic first

Volume equals length times width times thickness, all in feet. A 8x10 pad at 4 inches thick is 8 x 10 x 0.333, which is 26.7 cubic feet. At 0.6 cubic feet per 80 lb bag, that is 45 bags. Forty-five bags is 3,600 pounds and about six hours of mixing.

Add ten percent for spillage and for the fact that your subgrade is not perfectly flat. At about 40 bags, ready-mix from town starts to look sensible, and above 60 bags it is not close. A short-load delivery costs more per yard but it arrives mixed, consistent and in twenty minutes. We will tell you that at the counter rather than sell you sixty bags.

The base is the job

  1. Strip the sod and topsoil until you are on firm subsoil. Topsoil compresses and holds water and it is the reason pads crack.
  2. Excavate deep enough for 4 inches of gravel plus 4 inches of concrete, so 8 inches below finished height.
  3. Slope the whole thing about a quarter inch per foot away from anything you do not want water running toward.
  4. Put down 4 inches of crushed gravel and compact it in two lifts. Rent a plate compactor if you can, use a hand tamper if you cannot, and do not skip it. Loose gravel is not a base.
  5. Check the base flat with a straightedge and a level. Every low spot you leave is extra concrete you buy.

Forms and reinforcement

Form the perimeter with 2x4s on edge, staked every three feet, screwed at the corners, and checked for square by measuring both diagonals. They should match. Set the forms to finished height and use them as your screed rails.

For a shed pad, welded wire mesh or a grid of rebar on chairs in the middle third of the slab thickness is worth doing. Wire laid on the gravel and pulled up as you pour is what most people do, and it half works. Chairs are better. Fiber-reinforced mixes help with surface cracking and do not replace steel.

The pour

Get help. A pad this size mixed from bags needs two people minimum, three is better: one mixing, one placing, one screeding. Start at the far corner and work back toward where you are mixing. Place, rod it against the forms to release air, then screed with a straight 2x4 riding both form edges, working it side to side as you pull.

Once the sheen goes off, float it, then tool the edges, then cut control joints about every eight feet at a quarter of the slab depth. Broom finish across the direction of travel so it is not slick when it is wet, which around here is most of the time.

Then leave it alone

Tent a tarp over it if rain is coming in the first several hours. Keep it damp for three days. Stay off it for a day, set the shed after a week. Full strength is a month out. See the curing piece for how to tent it without printing the tarp into the surface.

The alternative worth considering

A well-compacted gravel pad with a treated timber frame around it carries a small shed perfectly well, drains better than concrete, costs a third as much, and takes an afternoon instead of a weekend. For a 8x10 garden shed on this coast, that is often the better answer and nobody will tell you so at a big box store.

Check gravel and concrete stock, and for anything over about 40 bags, ask us for the ready-mix number.
