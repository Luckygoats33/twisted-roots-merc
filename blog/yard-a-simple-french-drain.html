The Yard

A French Drain That Works

A trench, a fabric sock, clean rock and a place for the water to go. That last one is the part people forget.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A french drain is a gravel-filled trench with a perforated pipe in it that collects groundwater and carries it somewhere else. It is one of the few genuinely effective things a homeowner can build to fix a wet yard, and it fails for one of two reasons: fines clogged it, or there was nowhere for the water to go.

Answer the outlet question first

Water runs downhill. A french drain does not pump. Before you dig anything, find the place the water will come out: a ditch, a swale, a lower part of the property, a storm connection if you have one legally. If there is no downhill outlet, a french drain will fill up and sit there, which is what you already have.

Do not discharge onto a neighbor's property. That is both a bad neighbor move and a legal problem, and out here the neighbors are the people you need in a windstorm.

Slope

Minimum 1 percent, which is 1 inch of fall per 8 feet. More is better. Set a string line with a line level from the high end to the outlet before you dig, and dig to the string. Eyeballing the slope of a trench in wet clay is not possible and everybody thinks it is.

The build

  1. Call before you dig. Utility locates are free and a shovel through a buried line is a bad afternoon.
  2. Dig the trench, typically 12 to 18 inches wide and 18 to 24 inches deep, to your string line.
  3. Line the trench with non-woven geotextile fabric, wide enough to wrap over the top with overlap. This is the part that determines whether the drain lasts five years or twenty five.
  4. Put 2 to 3 inches of clean 3/4 inch rock in the bottom. Clean, not minus. Fines will pack the voids and kill it.
  5. Lay perforated pipe, holes facing down. This confuses people every time. Holes down lets the pipe collect water from the bottom of the gravel and keeps the pipe from filling with silt from above.
  6. Cover with clean rock to within about 3 inches of the surface.
  7. Fold the fabric over the top of the rock, overlapping generously.
  8. Top with soil and sod, or leave the rock exposed if you want a surface drain as well.

Rigid pipe or the flexible black stuff

Rigid perforated PVC is better. It stays straight, it holds its slope, it can be snaked out with a drain cleaner when it silts up, and it does not sag. The corrugated flexible pipe is cheaper and easier to lay around corners, but the corrugations trap sediment and you cannot clean it effectively.

The pipe-in-a-sock product, corrugated pipe pre-wrapped in a fabric sleeve, is convenient and works acceptably in coarse soil. In our clay it clogs faster than a proper fabric-lined trench. Do the trench right and you will not need the sock.

The variations

  • Curtain drain: the same trench, placed uphill of a structure, to intercept water before it reaches the building. Often the right answer for a wet crawl space.
  • Footing drain: at the base of a foundation, below the slab level. This is excavation work and generally a contractor job.
  • Dry well: a gravel pit at the outlet end when there is no downhill discharge. It works in soil that percolates. In clay it is a hole full of water.

When it is not a drain problem

If the wet spot is directly under a downspout, fix the downspout. If the yard is wet because the grade slopes toward the house, regrade it. Both are cheaper than a drain and both should be done first. See the drainage piece.

And if you have water in a basement or standing under a house, get somebody who does this for a living to look at it before you dig. That is a foundation question and it is worth a professional opinion.
