The Yard

A Woodshed That Actually Keeps Wood Dry

Open on the right sides, a roof with real overhang, and the floor up off the ground. Everything else is decoration.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The mistake with a woodshed is building it like a shed. A shed keeps weather out. A woodshed has to keep rain off while letting air through, and those are almost opposite requirements. A closed woodshed on this coast will produce wood that is wetter in spring than it was in fall.

The four rules

  1. Roof with overhang. At least 12 inches all around, more on the weather side. Wind-driven rain here does not fall straight down.
  2. Open sides. At minimum the front and one end. Air has to come in one side and out the other or nothing dries.
  3. Floor off the ground. Four inches minimum, on gravel, so ground moisture does not wick up into the bottom row.
  4. Facing the prevailing dry-season wind. Around here that means the open face wants to be south or west, and the back wall against the winter southwesterlies is a judgment call you make on your own lot.

Sizing it

A cord is 128 cubic feet, stacked, and a cord stacked at typical density occupies about 4 feet deep by 4 feet high by 8 feet long.

Two rows deep is the practical maximum. Anything deeper and the middle does not get air. If you burn a lot, build long rather than deep.

A simple build

Six treated 4x4 posts, two rows of three, set on gravel in post holes or on precast pier blocks. Front posts taller than back posts to make the roof slope. 2x6 beams across the tops, rafters front to back at 24 inches, roof sheathing and metal roofing on top.

Metal roofing is the right choice here. It sheds moss, it does not need underlayment maintenance, and it lasts. Shingles on a low-slope roof under fir trees grow moss in three years.

The floor is a grid of treated 2x4s laid on gravel with gaps between them, not solid decking. You want air under the wood, and you want needles and bark to fall through instead of composting on the floor.

Leaving the back open or closed

Depends on where the weather comes from and where the shed sits. A common good solution is a back wall of 1x6 boards with a half-inch gap between each one. It blocks driven rain and still passes air. Solid plywood does not.

The same treatment works on the ends. Gapped boards over solid every time in this climate.

The two details people skip

First, keep the woodpile away from the house. Wood stacked against a wall holds moisture against siding and is an invitation to carpenter ants and rodents. Six feet away minimum, and further is better.

Second, do not tarp the sides. A tarp draped over a stack traps evaporating moisture underneath and the wood stops drying. If you must tarp, cover only the top and let both sides breathe. That is a temporary measure while you build the shed.

What goes in it and when

Split wood dries. Rounds do not, or barely. Split it, stack it, and give it a full dry season minimum, two for oak or big fir rounds. Read the seasoning piece for stacking and moisture targets.

Wood that is properly seasoned and properly stored is worth real money in avoided chimney cleaning and in heat you actually get. Wet wood burns cool, builds creosote and gives you a chimney fire in a windstorm. See the storm shelf for what to have on hand when the power goes.
