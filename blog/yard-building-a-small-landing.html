Building a Small Landing at the Bottom of the Stairs

The step at the bottom is where stairs rot and where people slip. A pad or a small platform fixes both.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Stringers ending in dirt is the most common stair detail out here and the worst one. The end grain of the stringer sits in soil, water splashes up onto the bottom tread, the mud gets slick, and in three years the bottom of the stairs is soft. A landing solves the whole cluster of problems and it is a half-day project.

Three ways to do it

TypeCostTimeBest when
Compacted gravel padLowest2 to 3 hoursCasual back steps, good drainage matters
Concrete padMiddleHalf day plus cureMain entry, heavy traffic, wheeled things
Framed wood platformMiddleHalf dayUneven ground, or you need height

Sizing it

A landing should be at least as wide as the stairs and at least 36 inches deep in the direction of travel. If a door swings out over it, add the door swing plus a place to stand. If somebody carries groceries in, a landing that only fits both feet is not enough.

Slope it about a quarter inch per foot away from the house or the structure. Water on a flat landing sits, freezes on the two cold nights we get, and grows moss the rest of the time.

The gravel pad version

  1. Excavate 6 inches down, a little wider than the finished landing on all sides.
  2. Lay landscape fabric in the hole. This is the step people skip and it is what keeps the gravel from disappearing into the clay over five years.
  3. Fill with 4 inches of crushed gravel in two lifts, compacting each one. Crushed, angular gravel locks together. Round river rock does not and will always feel loose.
  4. Top with a 1 inch layer of finer crushed material and compact again.
  5. Set the stringer bottoms on a treated 2x block or a paver so the wood never touches the gravel directly.

This drains, it does not crack, and it costs a fraction of concrete. On a shaded north side where a concrete pad would just grow moss, it is often the better answer.

The concrete pad version

A 4 foot by 4 foot pad at 4 inches thick is 5.3 cubic feet, or about 9 bags of 80 lb mix. That is a reasonable one-person pour. Base prep is the same as any slab: strip the topsoil, 4 inches of compacted gravel, then form and pour. See the pad piece for the full sequence.

Two details specific to a landing. Broom finish it across the direction of travel, not with it, so the texture catches a wet boot. And set a treated 2x4 sleeper or a couple of anchors in the wet concrete so you have something to fasten the stringers to, rather than drilling into cured concrete later.

The framed platform version

Sometimes the ground drops away and a pad would need a foot of fill. A small framed platform is easier. Four treated 4x4 blocks on gravel or on precast pier blocks, a 2x6 frame, joists at 16 inches, decking on top. Keep every piece of wood at least 2 inches off the soil, use ground contact rated material for anything low, and leave the underside open so air moves.

The temptation is to skirt it in so it looks finished. Do not close it up tight. A skirt with no ventilation makes a damp box, and that is where rot lives. Lattice or a gapped skirt is fine.

Handrail and light

If the stair has four or more risers, the handrail must continue to the bottom and end at the landing, not stop three feet up. And a light at an outside stair is worth more than most safety measures people spend money on. It gets dark here at four in the afternoon in the wet half of the year.

Gravel, concrete, treated 2x lumber and pier blocks are in the Yard. Landscape fabric, anchors and exterior fasteners are inside on the Fix It wall.
