Trellises, and Making One That Holds a Loaded Vine

A trellis fails in a windstorm when it is full of wet foliage, not in spring when it is empty. Build for the wet version.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A trellis looks like a light-duty project and it is not. A mature grape, a wisteria, or a hop vine loaded with wet leaves in a November gust presents a serious sail. That is when trellises come down, and they usually take the plant with them.

Match the structure to the plant

PlantSupport neededSuggested build
Peas, beans, cucumbersVery light, one season1x2 or twine frame, temporary
Clematis, sweet peaLight, permanent1x4 lattice on a wall frame
Climbing roseModerate, heavy when wet2x4 frame, 4x4 posts
GrapeHeavy, long-lived4x4 posts in concrete, wire
WisteriaVery heavy, will destroy weak framing6x6 posts, real beams, or a masonry wall
HopsTall and heavy, seasonalTall posts, guy lines, cable and twine

Wisteria is not a joke. It will crush a 2x4 arbor and pull the gutter off a house. If that is what you are planting, overbuild by a factor everybody will tell you is silly.

A simple freestanding panel trellis

  1. Two 4x4x8 treated posts, set 24 to 30 inches deep in concrete with a gravel base, 6 feet apart.
  2. 2x4 rails across the tops and near the bottom, fastened to the post faces with structural screws.
  3. Infill of 1x4 boards, vertical or in a lattice grid, spaced to suit the plant. Wider gaps for anything with tendrils, closer for twiners.
  4. Seal every cut end. Bevel or cap the post tops.
  5. Keep the bottom rail at least 6 inches off the ground so the infill is not wicking soil moisture.

Post setting is the difference between a trellis that stands and one that leans. Read the post piece and dig the holes bigger than feels necessary.

Wall-mounted, and the mistake everybody makes

A trellis flat against siding traps moisture behind it and rots the wall. It also makes the vine impossible to trim away when the house needs painting. Stand it off the wall at least 3 inches on spacer blocks, and hinge the bottom if you can, so the whole panel can be laid down away from the wall with the plant still on it.

Hinging a trellis sounds fussy and it is the single best detail on this list. The first time you need to paint or repair siding you will be glad.

Wire trellis for grapes

For fruit, wire is better than wood. Two 4x4 end posts set deep and braced against pull, intermediate posts every 8 feet, and 12 gauge galvanized wire strung at 30 and 60 inches with a turnbuckle at one end.

The end posts carry all the tension. They need to be set deeper than the line posts and angled slightly outward, or braced with a diagonal to a deadman. A grape row that is not braced at the ends will slowly pull the end posts inward until the wire sags.

Coastal considerations

  • Cedar for the visible infill. It stays straight and it will not stain.
  • Treated for the posts. Ground contact rated, no exceptions.
  • Hot-dip galvanized or stainless fasteners, since anything else stains cedar black.
  • Air movement matters. A solid trellis panel in a wet climate becomes a mildew farm and so does the plant on it. Leave gaps.
  • Prevailing wind is southwest in the storm season. Orient the flat face of a big trellis so it is edge-on to that if you have the choice.

Sizing the wood

Our racks stop at eight feet, which is a good height for a trellis and about the practical limit for something freestanding you built yourself. Anything taller wants engineering, guy lines, or a wall to lean on. 4x4x8 posts, 2x4s, 1x4s and cedar pickets are all out back, and cedar pickets in particular make handsome, cheap lattice infill once you rip them or space them out.

Screws, wire and turnbuckles are inside on the Fix It wall.
