Cedar or Pressure Treated: What Goes Where

Cedar for what you see and touch, treated for what touches the ground. The overlap is smaller than the internet suggests.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

This argument gets had in every hardware store parking lot in the Northwest. The short version is that the two materials solve different problems and the choice is usually made for you by whether the wood is going to touch dirt.

What each one actually is

Western red cedar is naturally rot resistant because of extractives in the heartwood. It is dimensionally stable, light, easy to cut, smells good, and weathers to a silver gray that most people like. The rot resistance lives in the heartwood. The pale sapwood on the outside of a board has very little of it, and a lot of the cedar sold as fence picket stock has a fair bit of sapwood in it.

Pressure treated lumber is usually Douglas fir or hemlock with a copper-based preservative forced into it under pressure. It is heavier, wetter when you buy it, more prone to twisting as it dries, and considerably more rot resistant in ground contact than any cedar. It is also harder on your fasteners and your skin.

The coastal wrinkle

Everything here is wetter for longer than the general advice assumes. We get months where wood never fully dries, and shaded north-side lumber can stay damp year round. That shifts the answer toward treated in every marginal case, and it means that cedar in ground contact, which might last fifteen years in a drier climate, gives you closer to six or eight.

It also means moss. Cedar and treated both grow it, on any surface that stays damp and shaded, and moss holds water against wood. See the moss piece.

Cost, honestly

Per board foot, cedar costs meaningfully more than treated for framing dimensions and about the same for thin fence stock. A cedar picket and a treated 1x6 are close in price. A cedar 4x4 costs considerably more than a treated 4x4 and performs worse in the one job a 4x4 usually has, which is standing in dirt.

Working differences

  • Treated lumber arrives wet and shrinks as it dries. Build with it tight and let it shrink, or build with gaps and end up with bigger gaps.
  • Cedar is soft. It dents, it splits at the ends, and it needs predrilling near board ends more than treated does.
  • Treated requires hot-dip galvanized or stainless fasteners. So does cedar, for a different reason, tannin staining.
  • Cedar sawdust bothers some people's sinuses. Treated sawdust you should not breathe at all, and you should not burn the offcuts.

What we stock and what we do not

We keep 2x4 and 2x6 in both plain fir and pressure treated at 8 feet, 4x4x8 treated posts, 1x4 and 1x6 boards, and 6 foot cedar pickets. That covers repairs and small builds.
