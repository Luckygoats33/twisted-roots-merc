Concrete, Sand Mix and Mortar Are Not the Same Bag

Three gray powders, three completely different jobs, and the one substitution that will crack out inside a year.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

The dry shed holds three families of gray bag and they get mixed up constantly. Somebody patching a step buys concrete mix and cannot get it thin enough. Somebody setting block buys sand mix and wonders why it will not hold a joint. Here is the whole thing in one place.

The difference is what is in the bag

Concrete mix wants to be thick

The gravel in concrete mix is what makes it strong and what makes it cheap. It is also what makes it useless in a thin section. Aggregate up to 3/8 inch cannot lock together in a layer an inch deep, so a thin pour of concrete mix has rocks sitting in a weak paste and it breaks up under a boot heel.

Two inches is the practical minimum, four is better for anything you walk on, and a shed pad or a footing wants four with the base done properly underneath. Read the shed pad piece for the base work, which matters more than the concrete does.

Sand mix goes where concrete cannot

No coarse rock means it flows into thin sections and feathers reasonably well at the edges. That makes it right for resurfacing a rough step, filling a shallow spall, setting a paver or a threshold, or bedding a flagstone. It is also the bag you want for a repair where you are trying to match a surface.

What it is not is a structural material. It has no coarse aggregate, so it is more prone to shrinkage cracking and it costs more per cubic foot than concrete. Do not pour a pad out of sand mix and do not fill a post hole with it.

Mortar is glue, not a structural filler

Type S mortar has lime in it, which makes it sticky, workable, and able to tolerate a little movement without cracking. It is designed to be spread in a 3/8 inch bed and squeezed between two masonry units. On its own, in a puddle, unrestrained, it is weak. That is by design. In a wall the block carries the compression and the mortar just transfers it and keeps the water out.

Type S is the right general choice on the coast for below-grade and at-grade work, retaining walls and anything that sees weather. There is more in the mortar piece.

The substitutions, ranked

  • Sand mix instead of concrete for a thin patch: correct, this is the intended use.
  • Concrete mix instead of sand mix for a thin patch: wrong. It will crumble. If it is all you have, screen the rock out with hardware cloth and you have improvised sand mix, badly.
  • Mortar instead of concrete anywhere structural: wrong. Weaker, more expensive, will not carry load.
  • Concrete mix instead of mortar for laying block: wrong. It will not stick, will not tool, and the rock keeps the joints from closing.
  • Sand mix instead of mortar for a couple of block: passable for a garden edging that carries nothing. Not for a wall.

Bonding, the part that gets skipped

Fresh cement does not stick to cured cement on its own. Any patch, in any of these products, needs the old surface clean, sound, free of loose material, and dampened but not puddled. A bonding agent brushed on first is cheap insurance and lives on the Fix It wall. Skipping it is why most patches pop off in the second winter.

All three families are in the dry shed, up on pallets and out of the weather. Check the counts, and if you need a specialty product, Toledo Do it Best has the depth. We keep one good version of each thing instead of six mediocre ones.
