Curing Concrete When It Will Not Stop Raining

Concrete does not dry, it cures, and the difference decides whether your pad holds up or dusts off in three winters.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Concrete does not dry. It cures. Cement and water react chemically and keep reacting for weeks, and the strength you end up with depends on that reaction having enough water, enough warmth and enough time. Understanding that one sentence answers almost every question people have about pouring on this coast, where it is wet eight months a year and rarely warm.

Rain is a problem at one specific stage

Rain on freshly placed concrete, before it has stiffened, is genuinely bad. Raindrops land on the surface, dilute the cement paste at the top, and you end up with a weak skin that dusts and flakes off in a couple of years. That surface layer is exactly the part that carries wear.

Rain on concrete that has already set is close to harmless and arguably helpful, because keeping it damp is what you want anyway. The window you have to protect is roughly the first four to eight hours for standard mix, and about the first hour for fast-set.

How to tarp it properly

Not flat on the concrete. A tarp lying on a fresh surface prints every wrinkle into it permanently and traps runoff in the low spots. Build a low tent: a couple of 2x4s laid on edge outside the form, a 2x4 ridge down the middle, tarp over the top, weighted at the edges with gravel bags. Six inches of air is plenty. We keep tarps and bungees on the storm shelf year round for exactly this reason.

Cold is the slower problem

The chemical reaction slows down as it cools and effectively stops below about 40 degrees. Concrete that freezes in its first day can be permanently damaged, and we do get those nights. On the coast the more common issue is not freezing, it is a stretch of days in the mid-forties where nothing seems to be happening.

  • Below 50 degrees, expect roughly double the set time. Plan the day around it.
  • Below 40 degrees, cover with a tarp and something insulating. Straw, moving blankets, even a stack of cardboard under the tarp.
  • Do not use hot water thinking you will help. You get a fast set on top and cold weak concrete underneath.
  • Do not pour on frozen ground. It will settle when the ground thaws and you will have a cracked pad.

Sun is not our problem, but wind is

The other failure is losing water too fast. That is rare here in winter and common on a dry, breezy stretch in late summer when the wind comes up the valley. If the surface is going gray and dull while you are still working it, you are losing water. Mist it, or lay plastic on it once it has set.

A realistic schedule

  1. Pour in the morning, not the afternoon, so you get warmth and daylight on your side.
  2. Finish, then tent it before you clean tools.
  3. Leave the tent on overnight and through the next day.
  4. Keep it damp for three days if the weather turns dry. In a normal wet week, the weather handles it.
  5. Foot traffic after a day or two. Vehicles after a week. Real strength at 28 days.

We stock 60 and 80 lb concrete mix in the dry shed and tarps inside. Check the count before you drive over. If your pour is big enough that a rain delay would ruin it, that is a sign to hire it out and let somebody with a power trowel and a crew watch the sky.
