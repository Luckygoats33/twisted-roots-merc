Cutting Treated Lumber and Sealing the Ends

The preservative only goes in about half an inch. Every cut you make opens a door, and a two dollar brush closes it.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Pressure treated lumber is not treated all the way through. In Douglas fir, which is most of what gets treated in the Northwest, the preservative penetrates roughly a half inch into the incised surface and the core stays untreated. The board is a treated shell around raw wood. Every cut you make opens that shell.

This is the single most skipped step in outdoor building, and it is the reason posts rot from the bottom cut and rails rot from the ends.

Where to seal

CutRiskSeal?
Bottom of a post in the groundVery high. Standing in wet soilAlways, two coats
Top of a post, exposed to skyHigh. End grain drinks rainAlways, and cap or bevel it
Rail ends butted to a postHigh. Water sits in the jointAlways
Notches and dadoesHigh. Traps water and debrisAlways
Countersunk screw holes in a post topModerateYes, a drop in the hole
Rip cut down a board faceModerate. Exposes untreated coreYes if it faces weather
Cut end of a deck board, coveredLower but realYes. It costs nothing

What to use

A copper naphthenate end-cut solution is the standard. It is green, it smells like a shop, and a quart does a whole fence. Brush it on saturating wet, let it soak, and hit it a second time on anything going below grade. It is not paint. You want it to absorb, not to build a film.

Clear wood preservative works too. Regular exterior paint or stain does not, because it seals the surface without putting any fungicide into the wood, and a film on end grain will eventually crack and then you have trapped water behind a seal. That is worse than nothing.

Cutting it well

  1. Cut outdoors, upwind, with a dust mask on. Treated sawdust is not something to breathe.
  2. Use a sharp carbide blade. Treated fir is wet and it will gum a dull blade and burn.
  3. Support the board on both sides of the cut so it does not pinch the blade. Wet treated stock is heavy and it closes on a saw kerf.
  4. Mark and cut on the incised face where you can, so the cleaner face shows.
  5. Brush the sealer on immediately while the cut is fresh and open. Ten minutes later it starts closing up.
  6. Sweep up the sawdust. Do not leave it in a garden bed, a chicken run or a driveway puddle.
  7. Bag the offcuts for the trash. Do not burn them, and do not save them for the woodstove.

Buy long, cut once, or buy short

The instinct is to buy an 8 foot post and cut it to 6 feet 6 inches. That is fine, but bury the factory-treated end and put your cut on top, where you can seal it, bevel it, and see it. The end going in the ground should be the one that came off the treating cylinder.

This also argues for planning your cuts so you do not end up with the fresh cut buried. It is a small thing that costs nothing to get right and is impossible to fix later.

Wet lumber, and why your fence has gaps now

Treated lumber comes off the truck saturated. It shrinks as it dries, most of it across the width, and a 2x6 can lose a quarter inch. Build tight and expect gaps, or wait. If you can stack it in a dry, shaded, ventilated spot with stickers between the layers for a few weeks, it will move less after it goes in the fence. Almost nobody does this and it is genuinely worth it on anything you care about.

Do not stack wet treated lumber flat on concrete or plastic. It will cup on one side and grow mold on the other.

End-cut preservative, brushes, blades and masks are on the Fix It wall. Treated 2x4, 2x6 and 4x4 stock is out back at eight feet and shorter.
