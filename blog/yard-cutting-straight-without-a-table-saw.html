Cutting Straight Without a Table Saw

A clamped guide board and a circular saw will beat a table saw on anything wide. The trick is measuring the offset once.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Most people out here do not own a table saw and do not need one. A circular saw with a sharp blade and a clamped straightedge will cut plywood and boards straighter than a small portable table saw will, because the work stays still and the tool moves.

The offset, measured once

The distance from the edge of your saw's base plate to the blade is fixed for that saw. Measure it once, write it on the saw with a marker, and never measure it again.

  1. Clamp a straight board to a scrap panel.
  2. Run the saw with the base plate riding tight against the board and cut through the scrap.
  3. Measure from the guide board to the cut line. That is your offset, typically somewhere between 1.25 and 1.75 inches depending on the saw.
  4. From then on: mark your cut line, measure the offset back from it, and clamp the guide there.

Which side of the blade you measure from matters. The blade has kerf, about an eighth of an inch. Keep the good piece on the side away from the waste, and set the guide so the blade removes material from the waste side of your line.

Crosscutting a 2x4 square

A speed square hooked over the edge of the board, held firmly with your off hand, and the saw base run along it. This takes three seconds and gives you a square cut every time. Freehanding a crosscut on a 2x4 and expecting square is optimism.

The main error people make is not holding the square down hard enough at the start of the cut. Press it down and against the board edge, start the saw, then push.

Blade and depth

  • Set the depth so the blade projects about a quarter inch past the material. Less blade exposed means less tearout, a safer cut and less binding.
  • Use a sharp blade. A dull blade wanders, burns and pushes back at you. Blades are cheap and are on the Fix It wall.
  • More teeth for plywood, fewer teeth for framing lumber. A 24-tooth blade in plywood tears the face badly.
  • Good face down when using a circular saw. The blade cuts upward through the material, so tearout happens on the top surface.

Support, which is where cuts go wrong

The offcut has to be supported or it will drop and pinch the blade before you finish, which either ruins the cut or kicks the saw back at you. Put the whole panel on a sheet of foam board on the ground, or across four 2x4s laid on the ground, and cut through into the sacrificial layer. Two sawhorses with the cut between them is the classic setup and the classic way to pinch a blade.

See the sawhorse piece for a setup that works.

Ripping a narrow strip

The hardest cut without a table saw. Two options. Clamp the guide across the piece you are keeping, so the saw rides on the wide part and the narrow strip falls off. Or clamp the whole board down to a bench with the narrow waste hanging over the edge and use an edge guide accessory on the saw. Do not try to ride the saw base on a two inch strip.

The honest note

For anything requiring repeated identical narrow rips, or joinery, or a lot of sheet goods, you want a table saw or you want somebody who has one. Siletz River Lumber will cut sheets to size. That is a service worth using and it costs less than the tool.
