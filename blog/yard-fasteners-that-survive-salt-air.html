Fasteners That Survive Salt Air

Modern treated lumber eats plain steel and coastal air finishes it. Here is what to use where, and how to tell the coatings apart.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Every outdoor project on this coast has the same failure mode, and it is almost never the wood. It is the metal. A deck built with the wrong screws will have sound boards and rusted-through fasteners in ten years, and pulling it apart is worse than building it was.

Two things are working against you here. The copper-based preservatives in modern treated lumber are corrosive to steel, considerably more so than the old arsenical treatments were. And salt air, which reaches well up the Siletz valley, accelerates everything.

The quick test in the aisle: hot-dip galvanized looks like it was dipped, because it was. The threads are slightly filled in and the finish is matte gray and irregular. Electro-galvanized looks like chrome. If it is shiny, it does not go outside.

The rule about mixing metals

Do not put aluminum against treated lumber, and do not put aluminum flashing against copper or a copper-treated post. Galvanic corrosion eats the aluminum. This is why aluminum flashing on a treated ledger board is a mistake and why you want a proper flashing tape or copper or stainless in that location.

Also do not mix stainless connectors with galvanized screws, or the other way around. The galvanized item corrodes first, preferentially, and faster than it would alone.

What to use where

  • Fence pickets to rails: hot-dip galvanized or coated exterior screws, 2 to 2.5 inch.
  • Rails to posts: hot-dip galvanized structural screws, 3 inch, or lags.
  • Deck boards: coated deck screws minimum, stainless if it is cedar and you care what it looks like.
  • Structural connectors, hangers, post bases: hot-dip galvanized as a floor, stainless within a mile of open water. And use the nails the connector is designed for, not deck screws. Joist hanger nails are a specific fastener for a reason.
  • Anything in cedar you will look at: stainless. Cedar tannins react with zinc and steel and produce black streaks that will not wash off.
  • Gate hinges and hardware: hot-dip or stainless, matched to the fasteners.

Screws versus nails

Screws for anything you might take apart, anything with withdrawal load, and any thin material near an end. Nails for structural connections where shear matters and where the code detail calls for nails, which includes joist hangers and hurricane ties. A deck screw in a joist hanger is not a substitute, and inspectors look for exactly that.

Predrilling

Within about an inch of the end of a board, predrill. Cedar splits, wet treated fir splits, and a split board holds nothing and lets water in. A pilot hole takes four seconds. Pulling and replacing a split picket takes fifteen minutes.
