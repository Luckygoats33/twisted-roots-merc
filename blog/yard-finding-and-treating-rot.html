Finding Rot Before It Finds You

A screwdriver and twenty minutes will tell you more about your deck and fence than a whole afternoon of looking at them.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Wood rot is a fungus. It needs four things: wood, oxygen, a temperature above about 40 degrees, and moisture above roughly twenty percent. You cannot remove three of those. The whole game is moisture, which is why on this coast everything outdoors is on a clock and the clock runs faster in the shade.

The screwdriver test

A long flathead screwdriver is the only inspection tool you need. Push the tip into the wood with moderate hand pressure, the amount you would use to open a paint can.

ResultMeaning
Tip barely marks the surfaceSound
Sinks 1/8 in, wood feels springyEarly decay. Dry it out and watch it
Sinks 1/4 to 1/2 inActive rot. Plan the repair
Sinks in to the handle, or the wood is stringyGone. Structural, replace it
Wood is dark, crumbly, cracks into cubesBrown rot, advanced. Replace

Where to look, in order

  1. Post bases at the ground line. Two inches below grade to two inches above. This is where nearly every post fails, because it has both moisture and air.
  2. The ledger board where a deck meets the house, especially at the ends and behind any flashing.
  3. Board ends and cut ends. End grain absorbs water many times faster than face grain.
  4. Under anything that sits on the deck. Planters, mats, a stack of firewood, a cooler somebody left out.
  5. Joist tops under the decking, at every screw hole.
  6. Stair stringers at the bottom cut where it meets the concrete or the ground.
  7. North-facing anything. It does not dry, from October through May.
  8. Behind fence rails where they lap the post and hold a wet seam.

The two kinds you will meet

Brown rot breaks down the cellulose and leaves the wood dark, dry-looking and cracked into cubes. It crumbles between your fingers. It is the common one in structural framing and it is the one that gets called dry rot, which is a misleading name since it needs water like everything else.

White rot leaves the wood pale, spongy and stringy. It is slower and more common in hardwoods and in wet exterior boards. Either way, the answer is the same.

Can you treat it

Sort of, and only in specific situations. Borate rods and liquid borate treatments will kill active fungus in wood that you then keep dry, and they are genuinely useful in a post or a beam that is early-stage and can be dried out. Drill a few holes, insert the rods, plug them, fix the water problem. This is standard practice and it works.

What it will not do is put strength back. Wood that has lost section is not restored by anything. Consolidants and epoxy fillers make a cosmetic repair on a trim board or a window sill. They are not a structural repair, and using them on a joist or a post is hiding a problem, not solving it.

Fix the water, or you will do this again

Every single time you find rot, ask what put water there and keep it there.

  • A downspout dumping next to a post
  • Grade sloping toward the structure instead of away
  • A gutter that has not been cleared and overflows on one corner
  • Blocked ventilation under a deck or a shed
  • Moss holding a permanent wet sponge against a board
  • Vegetation growing tight against wood, cutting off airflow
  • A flat top on a post, drinking rain through end grain

Fixing the drainage fixes the rot. Replacing the board without fixing the drainage buys you exactly as long as the last board lasted. See the drainage piece and the moss piece.

The inspection worth doing once a year

Pick a dry stretch. Take the screwdriver, a piece of chalk and half an hour, and walk the deck, the fence line, the shed skids and the porch posts. Mark what is soft. It takes twenty minutes and it turns a January emergency into a July project, which is a much better project.
