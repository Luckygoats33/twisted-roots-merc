Gate Hardware That Lasts on the Coast

Hinges, latches, drop rods and the difference between hot-dip galvanized and the shiny stuff that rusts by spring.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Gate hardware is the place where saving eight dollars costs you a Saturday. The gate is the only part of a fence with moving parts, it carries all its weight through two or three pieces of metal, and out here every one of those pieces spends its life wet.

The finishes, ranked honestly

FinishHow to spot itLife near the coast
Stainless steelDull silver, non-magnetic if 304 or 316Decades. Best and most expensive
Hot-dip galvanizedDull gray, slightly rough, uneven15 to 25 years
Powder-coated over galvanizedSmooth black or bronze, heavy15 years if the coating stays intact
Electro-galvanized or zinc platedBright, shiny, smooth, cheap2 to 5 years. Rust bleeds down the post
Painted plain steelGlossy, light, very cheapOne or two winters

The shiny bright hardware is the one on the peg at every store, and it is fine in Bend. Here it streaks rust down a cedar gate by the second spring. Hot-dip is the practical default. Stainless is worth it on a gate you use every day.

Hinges

  • Strap hinges spread the load across the gate frame and are the right choice for anything wider than about three feet or heavier than about forty pounds.
  • T-hinges are strap on the gate side, plate on the post side. Good general purpose.
  • Butt hinges belong on doors, not on outdoor gates.
  • Self-closing spring hinges are worth it on a gate that must not be left open, which around here means anything containing a dog or a goat.

Three hinges instead of two on a heavy gate. The load per hinge drops, and the top hinge is the one that fails, because it is in tension while the bottom two are in shear.

Fasteners are the weak link, not the hinge

A hinge is only as good as what holds it. The failure people see is not a broken hinge, it is screws pulling out of a post. Use lag screws or structural screws long enough to reach at least 2 inches into solid post material. If you are screwing into a 4x4, a 3 inch lag is right. If the top of the post is soft, the hardware will not save it and you need a new post.

Predrill lag screws. In treated fir a lag driven dry will split the post, and a split post holds nothing. Pilot hole about 65 percent of the shank diameter for the threaded portion.

Latches

LatchBest forWatch out for
Thumb latch, two-sidedYard gates you walk through dailyNeeds a fairly true gate to line up
Gravity or bar latchLivestock and field gatesNot secure. A cow figures it out
Slide boltDouble gates, one leaf fixedBinds if the gate sags at all
Lockable haspAnything that needs a padlockGet stainless or the lock seizes

A latch needs some tolerance. A gate moves seasonally, swelling in the wet and shrinking in the dry, and a latch with no slop will bind for six months a year. Slotted mounting holes are your friend.

Drop rods and double gates

On a double gate, one leaf is fixed while the other swings. That fixed leaf needs a drop rod into a pipe sleeve set in the ground or in concrete. Do not just drive the rod into dirt. Sink a piece of pipe flush with grade and the rod will find it every time instead of gradually enlarging a mud hole.

Small things that matter

  • Grease the hinge barrels once a year. A dry hinge wears its pin oval and then it has play.
  • Leave a half inch gap on the latch side and a quarter inch on the hinge side.
  • Keep the bottom of the gate at least two inches off the ground. It will sag some no matter what you do.
