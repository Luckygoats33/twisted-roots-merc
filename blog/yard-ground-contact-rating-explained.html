Ground Contact Rating, and Why the Tag Matters

Above ground and ground contact treated lumber look identical and are not. The difference is printed on a plastic tag most people throw away.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Two pressure treated 4x4s can sit on the same rack, look the same, feel the same and cost within a couple dollars of each other, and one of them will last three times as long in a post hole. The difference is the retention level of preservative, and it is stated on a small plastic tag stapled to the end of the board.

What the tag says

MarkingMeansUse it for
Above Ground, UC3A or UC3BLowest retention. Needs to drain and dryFence rails, deck boards, railings, trim
Ground Contact, UC4AHigher retentionPosts, joists near grade, skids, timbers on soil
Ground Contact Heavy Duty, UC4BHigher stillPermanent wood foundations, critical structural
UC4CHighest. Saltwater and severe exposureMarine and pilings

The tag also lists the preservative, usually a copper azole or micronized copper compound, and the retention number in pounds per cubic foot. You do not need to memorize the number. You need to read the two words at the top.

Where the line actually is

Ground contact is required any time wood touches soil, obviously. Less obviously, it is also called for when the wood is within six inches of the ground, when it is in a spot that cannot drain or dry, when it is in permanent contact with a surface that stays damp like a concrete slab or a wet leaf pile, and when it is difficult to replace.

That last criterion matters here. If replacing the board means dismantling something, use ground contact even if the geometry says above ground. On this coast, the extra few dollars buys you a decade.

  • Post in a hole: ground contact, no argument.
  • Bottom rail of a fence, eight inches off the dirt, shaded, wet nine months a year: use ground contact.
  • Deck joists over a crawl space that never dries: ground contact.
  • Deck boards in the sun and air with a gap between them: above ground is fine.
  • Skids under a shed sitting on gravel: ground contact.

Incising, and what those marks are

Douglas fir does not accept preservative easily, so treaters run it through a machine that punches thousands of small slits into the surface to let the chemical in. Those diamond-shaped marks all over a treated 4x4 are incisions, not damage. They also mean the treatment penetration is largely in the outer shell, roughly a half inch deep, and the core of the board is untreated.

That is the reason cut ends are so important. The moment you saw through a treated post, you have exposed untreated wood. Brush end-cut preservative on every cut, every time. It lives inside on the Fix It wall and there is more about it in the cutting piece.

Handling and disposal

Treated lumber is safe to handle and to build with, including for decks and play structures. Wear gloves, wash up before eating, wear a dust mask when cutting, and do not use the sawdust in a garden bed.

Do not burn treated scraps. Ever. Not in a woodstove, not in a burn pile, not in a fire pit. The copper compounds concentrate in the ash and the smoke is genuinely bad for you. Treated offcuts go in the trash. We get asked this a lot when people are clearing out a fence job.

What we carry

Our 4x4x8 posts are ground contact rated because that is the only sensible thing to stock for a post. The treated 2x4s and 2x6s are what they are and the tag will tell you. If you need a specific rating for something structural, check the tag at the rack and ask if you are not sure. Eric will look at it with you.
