Moss, Algae and Cleaning Outdoor Wood

Moss holds water against wood, which is the whole problem. Here is what removes it and what quietly damages the boards.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Moss is not just cosmetic here. A mat of moss on a deck board or a fence rail is a sponge that keeps that surface wet through the entire dry season as well as the wet one. Wood that never dries rots, and moss is the mechanism.

It also makes things slippery in a way that puts people on the ground. A mossy ramp or a mossy step in January is genuinely dangerous.

What you are actually looking at

GrowthLooks likeConcern
MossGreen, thick, cushionyHolds water. Remove it
AlgaeGreen or black film, slick when wetSlip hazard. Remove it
LichenGray-green crusty patchesSlow growing, mostly cosmetic
MildewBlack speckling on the surfaceCosmetic and a sign of dampness
Gray weatheringUniform silver colorNot growth. Normal and harmless

Removing it, from gentlest up

  1. A stiff brush and water. Works on light moss, does no damage. Start here.
  2. Oxygen bleach, sodium percarbonate. Mix, apply, let it sit fifteen minutes, scrub, rinse. Safe for plants and for the wood, and it is what deck cleaners mostly are.
  3. A dedicated wood cleaner or brightener. Same idea, sometimes with oxalic acid to lift the gray as well.
  4. Zinc or copper based moss killer. Slow but persistent. Good on roofs and shaded fence rails. Do not let the runoff reach the river or a pond, because copper is hard on fish.
  5. Chlorine bleach. Effective and the most damaging. It breaks down lignin, leaves the surface fuzzy, corrodes fasteners and kills whatever it runs off onto. If you use it, dilute it hard and rinse thoroughly.

Pressure washing, carefully

A pressure washer will strip moss off a deck in twenty minutes and it will also strip the soft spring wood out from between the growth rings if you get too close. That leaves a furry, ridged surface that holds more water and grows more moss. It is the most common way people damage their own deck.

  • Use a wide fan tip, 25 or 40 degrees. Never the zero degree tip.
  • Keep the nozzle at least 12 inches off the wood and keep it moving.
  • Work with the grain, along the board, never across.
  • Start further away than you think and close in gradually.
  • Under 1,500 psi for cedar. Treated fir tolerates a bit more.
  • Test on a spot nobody looks at first.

Fixing the cause

Moss grows where it is shaded and damp. If a section of deck or fence grows moss and the rest does not, something is keeping that section wet.

  • Overhanging branches. Limbing up a fir changes the light and the drip line dramatically.
  • Shrubs pressed against a fence, blocking airflow.
  • Fir needles and leaves left on a deck all winter. This is the big one. Sweep in fall and again midwinter.
  • Gutters overflowing onto one section.
  • Boards laid tight with no gap, so water sits between them.
  • Ground contact where the bottom of a fence or a post touches soil.

Sweeping a deck twice through the wet season does more for it than any product does. Needles hold water and they hold moss spores, and a deck that gets swept regularly grows very little.

When to do it

Clean in a dry stretch, then let the wood dry several days before you seal or stain it. Cleaning and sealing the same day traps moisture in the wood and the finish will fail. See the sealing piece for how long to wait.
