Plywood Grades in Plain Language

The letters are about the face veneer, the stamp is about the glue, and only one of those matters for a wet climate.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Plywood is labeled with a stamp that looks like code. It is not complicated once somebody explains the parts, and the part that matters most on this coast is not the part people ask about.

The letters

Plywood is graded on both faces, front and back. You will see a two-letter designation like BC or CDX. The first letter is the face you see, the second is the back.

GradeWhat it looks like
ASmooth, sanded, repairs neatly made. Paintable
BSolid surface, some repairs and small splits
CKnotholes up to 1 in, splits, sanding defects. Structural
DKnotholes up to 2.5 in, larger defects. Not for exposure

So BC plywood has a decent paintable face and a rough back. CDX has a C face, a D back, and X, which people misread constantly.

The X does not mean exterior

This is the single most common misunderstanding at a lumber counter. In CDX, the X means exposure, specifically Exposure 1, meaning the glue is waterproof but the panel is only rated to withstand exposure during construction. It is sheathing. It is meant to be covered.

Actual exterior plywood is stamped EXTERIOR, and it uses both waterproof glue and veneers rated for permanent exposure. Marine grade goes further, with no core voids and higher grade veneers, and costs accordingly.

Bond classificationMeansUse
InteriorNot moisture resistantInside, dry, permanently
Exposure 1Waterproof glue, temporary exposureSheathing under roofing or siding
ExteriorWaterproof glue and exterior veneersPermanently exposed surfaces
MarineExterior bond, void-free coreBoats, wet structural work

Leaving CDX exposed to weather for a winter here will delaminate the face veneer. It is fine for two weeks while you get roofing on. It is not siding.

The span rating

The two numbers separated by a slash on the stamp, like 32/16, are the span rating. The first number is the maximum rafter spacing in inches when used as roof sheathing, the second is the maximum joist spacing as subflooring. A 32/16 panel can go on rafters 32 inches apart or joists 16 inches apart. That number, not the thickness, is what you check when you are sheathing something.

Plywood or OSB

OSB is cheaper, flatter, and structurally equivalent when dry. The difference that matters here is what happens when it gets wet. OSB swells at the edges and does not recover. Plywood swells and largely returns. In a climate where a build might sit open through a rainy week, plywood is worth the difference, and it is definitely worth it anywhere the panel might see moisture over its life.

For a shed floor, a roof deck, or anything you cannot easily replace, use plywood.

Treated plywood

Pressure treated plywood exists in the same ground contact and above ground ratings as dimensional lumber. Use it for shed floors, planter boxes, anything on skids, and anything within six inches of the ground. Seal every cut edge, because the edges of a treated panel are exposed core and they are the first thing to fail.
