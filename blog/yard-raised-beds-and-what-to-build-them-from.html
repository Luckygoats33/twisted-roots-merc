Raised Beds and What Is Safe to Build Them From

The treated lumber question answered plainly, plus how many boards a bed takes and how much soil to buy.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Two questions come up every spring at the Yard. Can I use pressure treated lumber for a vegetable bed, and how much dirt does it take to fill one. Here are both.

The treated lumber question

The honest answer is that modern treated lumber, using copper azole or micronized copper preservatives, is considered safe for garden beds by most agricultural extension services, including the ones in this region. Copper does leach into the soil, but it stays in the few inches nearest the wood and plant uptake is very low. The arsenic-based treatment that people rightly worried about has not been sold for residential use in a long time.

The equally honest answer is that a lot of people are not comfortable with it regardless, and there is no reason they should have to be. Cedar exists, costs a bit more, and lasts a good while.

If you use treated and want a belt-and-braces approach, staple heavy plastic sheeting to the inside face of the boards before you fill. Do not line the bottom, you want drainage.

Materials for a bed

A 4x8 bed, two boards high, from 2x6 stock:

ItemQuantity
2x6x8 for the long sides4
2x6x8 cut in half for the ends2
4x4 corner blocks, 11 in long4, from one 4x4x8
Exterior screws, 3 inAbout 32

Eight-foot boards are exactly the right length for this, which is one of the reasons a rack that stops at eight feet is not the limitation people assume. A 4x8 bed is also the right size for reaching the middle from either side without stepping in it, which matters more than it sounds.

How much soil

Volume equals length times width times depth, in feet. A 4x8 bed at 11 inches deep is 4 x 8 x 0.92, which is 29.4 cubic feet, or about 1.1 cubic yards.

Bagged soil comes in 1.5 or 2 cubic foot bags, so a 4x8 bed is roughly 15 to 20 bags. At that point a yard of soil delivered from a local supplier is cheaper and better quality. Bags make sense for topping off, not for filling.

Siting and building

  1. Pick the sunniest spot you have. In a coastal valley with fir on three sides, that is the whole ballgame.
  2. Level the ground. A bed built on a slope holds soil deep at one end and shallow at the other.
  3. Lay cardboard on the grass instead of digging it out. It smothers the sod and breaks down.
  4. Assemble the frame with the corner blocks inside, screws through the face into the block.
  5. Fill with a mix, not straight topsoil. Compost, topsoil and something for drainage.
  6. Expect it to settle several inches in the first season. Plan to top it up.

Cedar pickets make good low beds and 2x6 makes good tall ones. Both are on the racks, along with 4x4 posts for corners and exterior screws inside. Bulk soil is not something we carry, and we will point you to somebody who does.
