Rebuilding One Section of Fence

Post to post, one bay at a time, with a materials table so you know what to put in the truck before you start pulling boards.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A whole fence is a project. One bay of fence, post to post, is a Saturday. Doing it a bay at a time is how most fences out here actually get replaced, and there is nothing wrong with that. The fence ends up in mixed condition for a few years and then it is all new and you never took a weekend off to do it.

Figure out what is actually bad

Grab the post on each end of the bay at head height and push. A post that flexes at the ground line is rotted, and no amount of new rails and pickets will help. Poke the post at grade with a screwdriver. If it goes in more than a quarter inch with hand pressure, the post is done.

Eighteen pickets and three treated 2x4s fits in a short-bed pickup, which is exactly why everything on our racks stops at eight feet. See the truck bed piece.

The order of work

  1. Take the pickets off the bay and stack them out of the way. Save any that are still good.
  2. Unscrew or cut the rails free at both posts.
  3. If the post is bad, dig it out. This is the worst hour of the job. A concrete plug in wet clay does not want to come up. Rock it with a bar, dig around it, and consider a come-along and a truck if it will not move.
  4. Set the new post. Gravel base, plumb, fast-set poured dry, water on top. Wait four hours before you hang anything on it.
  5. Cut the new rails to fit between posts, or lap them onto the post faces if that is how the fence was built. Set the top rail down about 8 inches from the picket tops and the bottom rail about 10 inches up from the ground.
  6. String a line at the top picket height across the bay so your picket tops follow the neighbors.
  7. Hang the pickets, two screws per rail, spacer block between each one.

Coastal details that matter

  • Keep the bottom of the pickets at least 2 inches off the ground. Cedar touching dirt wicks water and rots from the bottom.
  • Seal every cut end of treated lumber. The rail cuts especially, since they sit horizontal and hold water.
  • Rails hold water on their top face. If you can, bevel the top edge of the rail or set the rail on edge instead of flat.
  • Use hot-dip galvanized or stainless screws. Cedar has tannins and plain steel stains it black.

Where the honest line is

If more than a third of the posts are soft, you do not have a repair, you have a fence replacement, and doing it one bay at a time will take you three years and cost more in gas and small purchases than one load delivered.
