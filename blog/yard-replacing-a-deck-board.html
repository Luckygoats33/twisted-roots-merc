Replacing a Soft Deck Board

Cut it out at the joists, check what is underneath, and understand that one bad board is rarely just one bad board.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Somebody puts a foot through a deck board about once a year in this valley, usually in the same place they have been stepping carefully around for six months. The board itself is the easy part. What is under it is the reason to do this job properly instead of quickly.

Find the extent before you cut anything

  1. Take a screwdriver and probe every board, especially near the ends, near the ledger, under planters, and anywhere shaded.
  2. Mark every soft spot with chalk. Do not just mark the one that broke.
  3. Get underneath if you can and look up at the joists, the ledger and the post connections. A soft deck board sitting on a rotted joist is a different job.
  4. Look at why it got wet. A board that rotted in the middle of a sunny deck had water sitting on it from something. A planter, a mat, a downspout, a low spot.

Taking the old board out

Deck boards are fastened at every joist, typically 16 inches on center. Back the screws out if there are screws. If it is nailed, pry from the end with a flat bar and a scrap of wood as a fulcrum so you do not crush the neighbors.

If the board is too rotten to pry against, cut it. Set a circular saw to just barely deeper than the board thickness, about 1.25 inches for a 5/4 board or 1.75 for a 2x, and make two plunge cuts down the length near the middle. Take the middle strip out, then the two side pieces come free with less prying. Do not set the saw deeper than the board. You will cut into the joist you are about to rely on.

Look at the joist while it is open

Joist conditionWhat to do
Sound, dry, screwdriver will not penetrateNew board, done
Damp on top, sound underneathLet it dry, add joist tape, new board
Soft on top 1/4 in, solid belowScrape it back, treat, tape the top, new board
Soft through, or crumblesSister a new joist alongside. Bigger job
Rot at the ledger endStop. Read the ledger piece

Joist tape is a self-adhesive butyl strip you stick along the top of the joist before the decking goes back. It keeps water out of the screw holes, which is where joists actually rot. It is cheap, it takes ten minutes, and it doubles the life of the framing.

Cutting the new board

Measure the opening at both ends, because the neighbors may have moved. Cut a little long and trim. Board ends must land on a joist, centered, so each board end has full bearing and there is room for two fasteners.

If the old board spanned a joint you cannot reach, you can sister a short block of 2x lumber alongside the joist to give the new end something to sit on. Fasten it with structural screws into the existing joist and it is as good as the original.

Setting it

  • Crown up. Sight down the board edge and put the arc facing the sky so it sheds water instead of holding a puddle. See the piece on reading a board.
  • Predrill within two inches of the end, always.
  • Two screws per joist, an inch in from each edge, driven flush and not countersunk into a dish that holds water.
  • Match the gap to the neighbors. If the deck is old and the boards have shrunk, your new wet treated board will shrink too. Set it tight and let it move.

The color problem

A new board in an old deck looks like a new board in an old deck for about two years. You can speed the match with a thinned gray stain, or you can move the new board to a less visible spot and put an old sound board where you will see it. That trick is worth ten minutes.

When it is not one board

If a third of the deck probes soft, you are replacing a deck and you should plan it as one, with real lumber from Siletz River Lumber. And if the rot is at the ledger, where the deck attaches to the house, stop and read the ledger piece before you do anything else. That connection is what holds the deck onto the building.
