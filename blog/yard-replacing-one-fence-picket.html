Replacing One Fence Picket

A fifteen minute job that people put off for a year, and the two tricks that keep the new board from looking new forever.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

One broken picket in a run of eighty. It has been like that since the wind came through. This is the smallest possible fence job and it is the one we sell the most cedar pickets for, one and two at a time, which is exactly what the Yard exists to do.

Take the old one off properly

  1. Look at how it is attached. Nails through the face into the rails, or screws. Count the fasteners, usually two per rail, two or three rails.
  2. For screws, back them out. For nails, get a flat bar behind the picket at each rail and work it out evenly from top and bottom. Prying at one point splits the neighbors.
  3. If the picket is too far gone to pry against, break it out in pieces and then pull the nails out of the rails with a cat's paw or nippers.
  4. Pull every nail. A nail stub left in the rail keeps the new picket from sitting flat, and you will not see it until you have driven a screw.
  5. Check the rail while it is exposed. If the rail is soft where the picket was nailed, you have a bigger job. See the section rebuild piece.

Height matters more than people expect. If the existing fence is 6 feet of picket, a 6 foot picket drops right in. If somebody cut them all down to 5 feet 8 inches at some point, measure before you cut, and cut from the bottom so the dog ear on top still matches.

Hanging it

Set the new picket in place with the gap matching its neighbors. A scrap of 1x4 or a couple of framing nails used as spacers keeps the gap consistent, which is what your eye actually notices from the road.

Check the top against the pickets on either side, not against a level. A fence that was built following the ground is not level, and a picket set truly plumb in a fence that gently follows a slope will look wrong. Match the neighbors.

Fasten with exterior screws, not nails. Two per rail, an inch in from each edge, predrilled if you are within an inch of the end. Hot-dip galvanized or stainless deck screws, because cedar and plain steel produce black stains that bleed down the board within a season. Read the fastener piece if you want to know why.

Making it not look brand new

Two tricks. First, if you can, take the replacement picket and lay it out in the weather for a few weeks before you install it, top side up. It will start graying. Second, a wash of thinned gray exterior stain gets you most of the way in an afternoon, and it will keep weathering from there.

Or do nothing. Bare cedar goes silver on its own out here in about a year, faster on the west-facing side. In two years nobody can tell which board you replaced. This is the option we usually recommend, since it involves doing nothing.

While you have the flat bar out

Walk the fence. Push on each post at the top. Any post that moves more than an inch is going to be your next project, and it is a much better project in dry weather than in a January windstorm. Same for any picket that is cracked at a fastener, because that is the one the next gust takes.

Cedar pickets, 1x4 and 1x6 boards and exterior screws are in the Yard. We will sell you one picket. That is genuinely the point of the place.
