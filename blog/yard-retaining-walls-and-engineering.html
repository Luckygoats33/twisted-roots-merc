Retaining Walls, and the Height Where You Stop

Under four feet with good drainage is a weekend. Over that, it is an engineered structure and the failure mode is serious.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A retaining wall holds back a hillside, and a hillside that has been holding still for forty years can start moving the day you cut into the toe of it. This is the project on our list where the honest advice is most likely to be do less than you were planning.

The height rule

Most jurisdictions, including Lincoln County, set a threshold around 4 feet of retained height, measured from the bottom of the footing to the top of the wall, above which a permit and an engineered design are required. Below that, with no surcharge above it, a properly built wall is a homeowner project.

Surcharge is the word for any extra load on the soil behind the wall: a driveway, a parking area, a building, a slope continuing upward, a pool. Any surcharge drops that threshold, and a wall with a driveway behind it needs engineering at almost any height.

Under 3 ft, level ground behind, good drainageReasonable weekend project
3 to 4 ft, level ground behindDoable. Check with the county first

What makes a low wall work

  1. A compacted gravel base, 6 inches minimum, below the frost line and below the loose topsoil, extending wider than the block.
  2. The first course buried. Roughly one tenth of the wall height, or a minimum of one full course. A wall that starts at grade will kick out at the bottom.
  3. Setback. Each course stepped back about an inch, leaning the wall into the hill. Segmental block has a built-in lip that does this for you.
  4. Drainage rock behind. A foot of clean 3/4 inch rock the full height of the wall, with a perforated drain pipe at the bottom running to daylight.
  5. Fabric between the drainage rock and the retained soil, so fines do not migrate in and clog it.
  6. Backfill in lifts, compacted, not dumped in all at once.

Water is what breaks walls

Saturated soil is much heavier than dry soil and it exerts far more pressure. Almost every failed retaining wall on this coast failed because water built up behind it with nowhere to go. The drainage rock and the pipe are not optional details, they are the structural system. A beautifully built wall with no drainage will lean out within a few wet seasons.

The pipe has to daylight somewhere. Same rule as any drain: find the outlet before you build. See the french drain piece.

Materials

  • Segmental concrete block. Interlocking, no mortar, designed for this, and the most forgiving choice for a homeowner. Sold in town.
  • Treated timbers. Ground contact rated, with deadmen tied back into the hill every other course. They work and they have a service life, unlike block.
  • Mortared block. Rigid, needs a real footing, needs rebar and grout, cracks if anything settles. See the mortar piece for the small stuff.
  • Boulders and rockery. Very effective, needs machinery and someone who knows how to place them.

The alternatives worth considering

Two or three low terraced walls, well separated horizontally, instead of one tall one. Regrading to a gentler slope. Planting the slope with something deep-rooted, which is genuinely effective on a slope that is only slowly creeping. All three are cheaper than an engineered wall and all three are worth pricing before you commit.
