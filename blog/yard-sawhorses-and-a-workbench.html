Sawhorses and a Workbench You Can Build in an Evening

Two horses and a panel is a bench. Here are the dimensions worth getting right and the mistake that makes horses wobble.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Everybody who does more than one outdoor project ends up needing a flat surface at a usable height. You can spend a weekend on a proper bench or an evening on two sawhorses and a panel, and for most of what people do out here, the horses are the better answer because they stack against a wall when you are done.

The height that matters

SurfaceHeightWhy
Sawhorses for cutting26 to 30 inLow enough to see the line and lean on the work
General workbench34 to 36 inRoughly wrist height standing
Assembly table30 to 32 inLower so you can reach across
Bench for handplaning32 to 34 inYou want to get over the work

Stand with your arms at your sides and measure to your wrist crease. That is a good general bench height for you specifically. Sawhorses should be four to six inches lower than that, because you are usually cutting downward.

A pair of sawhorses from three 2x4s

The simplest design that does not wobble uses a 2x4 on edge as the top beam and four legs splayed in two directions. The splay is the whole trick. Legs that lean out in only one plane make a horse that racks side to side.

  1. Cut the top beam to 36 inches. That is a good length, long enough to support real work and short enough to carry one-handed.
  2. Cut four legs at about 26 inches, with the top and bottom cut at a matching angle. Somewhere around 15 degrees off vertical works well.
  3. Fasten the legs to the sides of the beam, angled outward along the length of the beam and also splayed outward across it. Both directions.
  4. Add a horizontal brace low on each pair of legs, screwed to the outside faces. This is what stops the racking.
  5. Set the horse on a flat surface and scribe the leg bottoms level, then cut. Do not skip this or it will rock.
  6. Repeat. Make them identical, because two horses at different heights are worse than one horse.

One 2x4x8 gives you the beam and part of the legs. Two 2x4x8s per horse is a comfortable budget, three for the pair with brace material left over.

Turning them into a bench

Lay a 2x4 foot project panel across, or two side by side for a 4 foot deep surface. Screw the panel down through into the beams if you want it stable, or leave it loose so you can lift it off. A doubled panel, two thicknesses glued and screwed together, makes a top solid enough to hammer on.

A sacrificial top is worth it. When you cut sheet goods you set the piece on the horses, set a scrap panel underneath, and let the saw blade cut into the scrap. Better a two dollar sacrificial panel than a groove in the beam of your horse.

A real bench, briefly

If you want a fixed bench: four 4x4 legs, a 2x4 frame at the top and a lower shelf frame, and a doubled panel top. Attach it to the wall studs on one side if you can, because a bench that is bolted to a wall is dramatically more rigid than one standing free, and that rigidity is what you actually notice when you are working.

Build it 24 inches deep. Deeper benches accumulate a layer of things you cannot reach.

Details worth having

  • A power strip screwed to the front rail, so cords come from the front, not across the top.
  • A holdfast hole or two, or just a couple of clamps hung on a nail nearby.
  • A pencil groove routed or chiseled in the front edge. Sounds precious. Saves ten minutes a day.
  • A shelf underneath, because everything ends up on the floor otherwise.

2x4s, 4x4s and project panels are all in the Yard. Screws, clamps, squares and saw blades are inside on the Fix It wall.
