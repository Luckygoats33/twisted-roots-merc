Setting a Mailbox Post That Survives the Winter

Heights and setbacks the carrier needs, plus the reason a rural mailbox post leans within two years and how to stop it.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Mailbox posts on a rural road take more abuse than fence posts. They get hit by mirrors, sprayed by graders, soaked by every passing log truck, and they stand alone with no fence to brace them. That is why they lean. A fence post has fifty feet of fence holding it in line. A mailbox post has nothing but the hole.

The dimensions the carrier cares about

MeasurementTarget
Bottom of box above road surface41 to 45 in
Face of box back from edge of road6 to 8 in
Box doorOpens toward the road, latch works one-handed
Address numbersAt least 1 in tall, on the box or the post, visible from the road
Post buried24 in minimum in our soil

Measure from the road surface, not from the shoulder or the ditch bank, which is a common miss on a road with a crown. If the carrier cannot reach it from the vehicle without leaning out or backing up, you will hear about it, and reasonably so.

Break-away is a real requirement

Roadside mailbox supports are supposed to yield if a vehicle hits them. A 4x4 wood post is generally accepted for that. What is not acceptable, and this comes up out here more than it should, is a mailbox welded to a length of pipe filled with concrete, or set on an old milk can full of rock. Those things are unforgiving in a way nobody thinks about until somebody slides off the road on a wet night. Use a 4x4 and let it break.

The build

  1. Dig 24 inches deep and about 10 inches across. Deeper in soft ditch soil.
  2. Four inches of landscape gravel in the bottom, tamped.
  3. Set the 4x4x8 pressure treated post on the gravel. Cut it to length after it is set, not before, so you can trim to the exact box height.
  4. Brace it plumb in two directions and check it twice.
  5. Pour fast-set in dry, add water on top, wait 40 minutes.
  6. Mound the backfill so it sheds water away from the post, not into a bowl around it.
  7. Attach the arm. A 2x4 or a 1x6 cut to length, notched or lapped over the post top, fastened with exterior structural screws.
  8. Mount the box, check the height off the road, and add the numbers.

The details that make it last

  • Cut the top at an angle or cap it. A flat post top drinks rain through the end grain. Even a slight bevel sheds it.
  • Seal every cut end. The mill treatment does not reach the middle of a 4x4. Brush end-cut preservative on the top and on any notch. See the piece on cutting treated lumber.
  • Use hot-dip galvanized or stainless fasteners. Treated lumber eats plain steel and salt air finishes the job.
  • Keep the grader in mind. A post at the very edge of the ditch will get clipped by a county grader blade eventually. An extra six inches back is free.

The lean, and the fix

A leaning mailbox post is usually not a rotted post. It is the whole concrete plug rotating in soft soil, because the hole was too small and the ditch bank is saturated. The fix is a bigger hole, not a stronger post. Twelve inches across and thirty deep, with gravel, and it will stay where you put it.

If yours is already leaning and the wood is sound, you can sometimes dig one side out, plumb it, and pour fresh concrete against the old plug. It works about half the time. The other half you are digging the whole thing out, which is a genuinely miserable hour with a shovel and a pry bar.

4x4x8 pressure treated posts, fast-set, gravel and exterior screws all sit together in the Yard because that is how the job goes. Mailboxes and numbers are inside on the Fix It wall.
