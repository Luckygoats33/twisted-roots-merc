The Yard

Setting a Post in Wet Coastal Clay

Gravel in the bottom, fast-set poured dry, and the honest reason posts around here rot from the ground line up instead of the bottom down.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Siletz dirt is not sand and it is not loam. Dig down two feet almost anywhere in the valley and you hit a gray-brown clay that holds water like a bathtub liner. That single fact explains most of what goes wrong with posts out here, and it explains why the advice you read from somebody in Arizona will get you a fence that leans in six winters.

Clay does not drain. A hole full of concrete with a post in the middle of it is a cup. Rain gets in around the top of the collar, runs down the side of the post, reaches the bottom of the cup, and sits there. The post is then standing in water for eight months of the year. Nothing you can buy at the Yard changes that. What you can change is whether the water has somewhere to go.

The hole is the whole job

Depth first. A third of the total post length in the ground is the rule people use, and it is a decent rule. For a 4x4x8 that means about 24 inches buried and roughly six feet standing. Width should be about three times the post, so eight to twelve inches for a 4x4. Wider at the bottom than the top is better if you can manage it in clay, because a hole that flares out cannot be pushed up by frost. We do not get much frost heave down here, but we get a lot of saturated soil, which behaves similarly and less politely.

Gravel, and why nobody skips it twice

Four to six inches of landscape gravel in the bottom of the hole, tamped with the end of the post. It is not there to hold anything up. It is there so the end grain of the post is not sitting in a puddle. End grain drinks water like a straw. A post that wicks water up from the bottom will be soft in the middle of the buried section long before the part you can see looks bad.

Set the post on the gravel, brace it plumb in two directions, and check it twice. Concrete does not negotiate and it does not care what you meant.

Pouring the fast-set dry

  1. Post is on gravel, braced, plumb, and you have checked it on two adjacent faces with a level.
  2. Pour the fast-setting concrete into the hole dry, straight out of the bag, to about three or four inches below finished grade.
  3. Add water on top, the amount the bag tells you, usually about a gallon per 50 lb bag. Pour it slowly so it soaks in instead of running off.
  4. Poke it a few times with a stick to let the water find the dry pockets in the middle.
  5. Leave it alone for 40 minutes before you hang anything, four hours before you lean on it.

Then backfill the last few inches with soil, mounded so it sheds water away from the post rather than toward it. A concrete collar poured flush and flat is a rain-catcher. Crown it or bury it.

What actually kills posts here

Not the bottom. The ground line. The zone from about two inches below grade to two inches above is where the wood gets both oxygen and constant moisture, which is exactly what rot organisms want. That is why ground-contact rated lumber matters and why the cheap incised stuff sold for above-grade use fails early in this climate.

We keep 4x4x8 pressure treated, fast-setting concrete and 50 lb landscape gravel on the same pallet in the Yard, because that is how the job actually goes. Check what is on the racks before you drive over, especially for treated stock after a wet week. If you are setting more than about a dozen posts, call Siletz River Lumber and get a real load delivered. We are not going to pretend to be a lumberyard.
