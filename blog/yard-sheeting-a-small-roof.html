The Yard

Sheeting and Roofing a Small Structure

Slope, overhang and the order the layers go on. Get the slope right and everything downstream is easier.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A shed roof in this climate does one job and does it every day for thirty years. The two decisions that determine whether it succeeds are the slope and the overhang, and both get made before you buy a single sheet of anything.

Slope

Roof slope is expressed as inches of rise per 12 inches of horizontal run. A 4-in-12 roof rises 4 inches for every foot across.

SlopeMinimum forNote
1/4-in-12Fully adhered membrane onlyNot a DIY roof
1-in-12Standing seam metal, some panelsCheck the panel manufacturer
3-in-12Exposed fastener metal panelCommon shed roof
4-in-12Asphalt shingles, standard installThe usual floor for shingles
2-in-12 to 4-in-12Shingles with double underlaymentAllowed but marginal here
6-in-12 and upAnythingSheds moss and needles better

On this coast, steeper is better than the minimum, always. Fir needles and moss accumulate on anything shallow, needles hold water, water finds fasteners. A 3-in-12 shed roof under trees will need cleaning twice a year. A 6-in-12 mostly cleans itself.

Overhang

Twelve inches minimum on the eaves, and do not skimp on the rakes either. Overhang keeps water off the walls, off the windows, off the door, and away from the foundation. It is the single cheapest weatherproofing decision available and people cut it to save a sheet of plywood.

On a small shed, a 16 to 18 inch overhang on the low side is even better, because that is where all the water goes.

The layers, in order

  1. Rafters, 16 or 24 inches on center, birdsmouth cut over the top plates, blocked at the ends.
  2. Sheathing. Half inch plywood or OSB for 24 inch spacing, seams staggered, gapped 1/8 inch, screwed or nailed to every rafter. Use plywood rather than OSB on anything exposed to our humidity, and see the plywood piece.
  3. Drip edge at the eave, on top of the sheathing.
  4. Underlayment. Synthetic underlayment or felt, lapped uphill, so every seam sheds onto the piece below.
  5. Drip edge at the rakes, on top of the underlayment. Eave drip goes under, rake drip goes over. This trips everybody up once.
  6. Roofing. Metal panels or shingles, starting at the low edge and working up.
  7. Ridge or closure, and any flashing where the roof meets a wall.

Metal versus shingles here

Metal wins on a small building in this climate, and it is not close. It sheds needles and moss, it handles a low slope, it lasts thirty to fifty years, and a small shed roof is a manageable amount of panel to handle alone. The downsides are cost, the need for a straight and square substrate, and the fact that you cannot walk on it easily.

Exposed-fastener panels use screws with rubber washers driven through the flat, not the rib, on most profiles. Drive them snug, not crushed. Over-driven washers squeeze out and leak in three years, and there will be a hundred of them.

Where the roof meets a wall

If the shed roof runs into the side of a house or a taller wall, you need step flashing or a continuous piece tucked behind the siding, not a bead of caulk along the joint. Caulk is a maintenance item, not a flashing detail. This is the leak that shows up in the fourth year and rots the wall behind it.

What we carry

Not roofing. Not full sheets. We keep 2x4 and 2x6 framing to eight feet, 2x4 foot project panels for patches and small decks, and fasteners inside.
