The Yard

Stacking and Seasoning Firewood

Split it, stack it single row with air on both sides, and give it a year. Wet wood is the reason people think their stove is bad.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Green wood is roughly half water by weight. Every bit of that water has to boil off before the wood burns, and the energy to boil it comes out of your fire. That is why unseasoned wood hisses, smolders, throws no heat and coats the chimney with creosote. The stove is fine. The wood is wet.

The number

Under 20 percent moisture content is the target. A cheap pin-type moisture meter costs less than a cord and settles every argument you will ever have about a load of wood. Split a piece and test the fresh face, not the outside, which is always drier.

MoistureBehavior
Over 30 percentHisses, will not stay lit, heavy creosote
25 to 30 percentBurns poorly, noticeably less heat
20 to 25 percentAcceptable. Not ideal
15 to 20 percentRight. Lights easily, burns clean, full heat
Under 12 percentBurns fast and hot. Fine, mix it

Drying times on this coast

Longer than the books say, because our air is humid most of the year and the drying season is short. Rough guidance for split wood, stacked properly, with a roof and airflow:

SpeciesSeasoning time here
Alder6 to 9 months
Big leaf maple9 to 12 months
Douglas fir9 to 12 months
Hemlock9 to 12 months
Oak18 to 24 months
Anything left in roundsYears, and it may rot first

The single biggest accelerator is splitting. Bark is a waterproof jacket. A round left whole barely dries at all and on this coast it will start to punk before it seasons.

How to stack

  1. Get it off the ground. Pallets, a grid of treated 2x4s on gravel, or a couple of 4x4s laid down. Bottom row on dirt is a row you lose.
  2. Single row, bark side up, if you have the space. A single row exposed on both faces dries roughly twice as fast as a double row.
  3. Leave finger gaps between pieces. A tight stack is a wall. Air needs to move through, not around.
  4. Cross-stack the ends, log cabin style, or drive stakes, so the stack does not come down in a gust. It will come down at the worst possible moment otherwise.
  5. Cover the top only. Roof, metal sheet, or a tarp weighted along the ridge with the sides open. Never wrap a stack.
  6. Orient the stack so the long faces catch the summer wind, not so it is tucked neatly against a wall out of the weather. Out of the weather also means out of the wind.

Where not to put it

Not against the house. Not in the crawl space. Not on the deck for more than a week's worth. Woodpiles are habitat, and what lives in them ranges from carpenter ants to yellowjackets to the occasional rodent, none of which you want migrating into the building. Six feet off the house is the usual minimum and further is better.

And bring in only what you will burn in a few days. A week of wood in the house is fine and dries a bit more. A month of wood in the house brings the insects in with it.

The first-year problem

Everybody who gets a stove has the same first winter: they buy a cord in the fall, it is green, and it burns badly. The fix is to be a year ahead, permanently. Buy or cut next winter's wood this spring, and burn what you stacked last year. Two stacks, always. The first year of getting ahead is the only hard one.

Kindling, matches, fire starters, moisture meters, gloves and stove gaskets live inside on the Fix It wall and on the storm shelf. Pallets, treated 2x4s and gravel for a proper base are out back, and if you want to build a real woodshed, read that piece.
