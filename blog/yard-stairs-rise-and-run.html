Stairs, Rise and Run, Explained Plainly

Total rise divided by the number of steps. Everything else about building stairs follows from getting that one division right.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Stairs intimidate people more than they should. The layout is one division problem and a bit of arithmetic. The reason it feels hard is that every step has to be identical, and the penalty for getting it wrong is a step people trip on for the next twenty years.

The two words

Rise is how far up you go per step. Run is how far forward you go per step. Total rise is the vertical distance from where you start to where you finish. Total run is the horizontal distance the stairs will occupy.

That 3/8 inch variation limit is the strict one and it is strict for a good reason. People climb stairs by feel. One step a half inch taller than the rest is a trip hazard forever.

The arithmetic, worked out

  1. Measure the total rise. From the finished ground or landing surface up to the finished deck surface. Use a straight board out from the deck and a level, and measure down. Say it comes to 44 inches.
  2. Divide by 7 to get a starting guess at the number of risers. 44 divided by 7 is 6.3. Round to 6 or 7.
  3. Divide the total rise by that whole number. 44 divided by 7 equals 6.29 inches per rise. 44 divided by 6 equals 7.33 inches per rise. Both are legal. Seven risers at 6.29 is a gentle stair, six at 7.33 is steeper and shorter.
  4. Pick your tread run. Say 11 inches.
  5. Count treads. There is always one fewer tread than riser, because the deck surface is the top riser's landing. Six risers means five treads.
  6. Total run equals treads times run. Five treads at 11 inches is 55 inches of horizontal space needed.

The bottom step trap

When you cut a stringer, the bottom riser comes out taller than the rest by exactly the thickness of the tread material, because the tread adds height to every step except the bottom one. You fix this by cutting the bottom of the stringer down by one tread thickness. If your treads are 1.5 inch 2x material, cut 1.5 inches off the bottom of the stringer. This is called dropping the stringer and forgetting it is the classic first-stair mistake.

Building it

  • Lay out stringers with a framing square and stair gauges clamped at your rise and run. Mark all the steps off one continuous layout, do not measure each one separately, or the errors accumulate.
  • Cut one stringer, test fit it, then use it as a template for the rest.
  • Stringers every 16 inches for 2x treads. Three stringers on a 36 inch stair.
  • The bottom of the stringers should land on concrete or a treated pad, not bare dirt. A stringer end in soil rots first, always.
  • Attach the top with a hanger or a ledger, not toenails.
  • Handrail on any flight of four or more risers, graspable, continuous, returned to the post at the ends so a sleeve does not catch.

Stringer stock has to be 2x12 for anything but the shortest stair, and we do not carry it. That is a Siletz River Lumber item. What we do carry is 2x6 for treads, 1x6 for risers, exterior screws and everything for a two or three step stair off a shed. Check the racks. And read the landing piece if the stair lands on dirt.
