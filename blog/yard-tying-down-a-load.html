Tying Down a Load So It Stays There

Two straps minimum, over the load and not just around it, and the reason bungee cords do not count.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

A board off the back of a truck on Highway 20 is somebody else's windshield. In Oregon, you are responsible for your load, and an unsecured load is a citation and, if it causes an injury, a great deal worse than that. It also takes about ninety seconds to do properly.

What is actually rated

DeviceWorking loadVerdict
1 in ratchet strapAround 500 lbGood for lumber and light loads
2 in ratchet strap1,000 to 3,300 lbThe general purpose answer
Cam buckle strap, 1 inAround 500 lbGood, less crushing force than a ratchet
Rope, 3/8 in polyDepends entirely on the knotFine if you know a trucker's hitch
Bungee cordEffectively nothingNot a tie-down. Ever
Twine or baling wireNothingNo

Bungees are for holding a tarp down against flapping. They stretch, which means the load can move, build momentum and then break them. Every load lost off a pickup that we have ever heard about was held down by bungees.

The rule of two directions

A load needs to be restrained forward, backward and sideways. Forward is the one people forget, and it is the direction that matters most, because hard braking is far more violent than acceleration.

  1. Push the load forward against the bulkhead so it cannot gain speed before it hits something.
  2. Run one strap across the load near the front, anchored to bed hooks on both sides.
  3. Run a second strap near the rear, same way.
  4. If the load overhangs the tailgate, add a strap that pulls it forward and down, not just across.
  5. Tighten until the load does not move when you shove it hard with both hands. Then check it again.

Over, not around

A strap wrapped around a bundle of lumber holds the bundle together. It does not hold the bundle to the truck. The strap has to go over the load and down to anchor points on the vehicle on both sides. Both things are worth doing on a stack of boards: one around to keep it together, two over to keep it in the truck.

Long overhang

Anything hanging more than 4 feet past the rear of the vehicle needs a red or fluorescent orange flag at the end, at least 12 by 12 inches, and a red light at night. Tape it or tie it so it will not come off at highway speed, which a loose rag absolutely will.

A long board bouncing on a rough road will walk itself backward out of the bed if the strap is only holding it down and not forward. Cinch one strap so it pulls the load toward the cab.

Bags and loose material

  • Bags of concrete go on the bed floor, forward, and they mostly stay put by weight alone. One strap over the top is still worth it.
  • Gravel loose in the bed needs a tarp over it, secured on all sides. Loose rock at 55 miles an hour chips paint on the car behind you.
  • Anything light and flat, plywood especially, needs a strap or it becomes a wing. A project panel will lift out of a bed at highway speed.
  • Yard debris, brush and anything that sheds needs a tarp. This is the most commonly cited unsecured load out here.

Strap care

Straps live in the truck and get wet, and they still last years if you do two things: keep them out of the sun when you can, and never use one with a cut, a fray or a burn in the webbing. A strap with a nick in the edge has lost most of its rating. Cut it up and throw it away so nobody grabs it in a hurry.

Do not run a strap over a sharp corner. Put a rag or a scrap of carpet between the strap and the edge of a board bundle.

Ratchet straps, rope, flags and tarps are inside on the Fix It wall and on the storm shelf. Come around back and we will help you load and check the straps before you pull out. We would rather do that than read about it.
