The Yard

Type S Mortar and Laying a Few Block

Enough to build a planter wall, a step riser or a short garden wall without hiring a mason or wrecking it.

3 minute read  ·  Twisted Roots Merc, Siletz

Nobody in Siletz is building a chimney on a Saturday. But people build a two-course planter wall, a short retaining edge along a driveway, a step riser, or a base under a woodshed post, and for that you need a bag of Type S mortar and about forty minutes of technique.

Why Type S

Mortar comes in types graded by strength and flexibility. Type N is the common general-purpose interior and above-grade mortar. Type S is stronger, bonds better, and holds up to moisture and lateral pressure, which is why it is the right pick for anything at or below grade in a climate that rains most of the year. We only stock Type S. It covers everything a homeowner here is likely to build, and stocking one good version of a thing is generally how we do it.

The footing comes first

Block sitting on dirt moves. Even a two-course garden wall wants a footing: a trench below the loose topsoil, six inches of compacted gravel, and either four inches of concrete or, for a wall that is genuinely just holding soil in a bed, a deeper compacted gravel base. If the wall is holding back more than about three feet of soil, stop and read the retaining wall piece, because that is an engineering question and not a weekend one.

Mixing it

Mortar wants to be stiffer than you think and stickier than concrete. Mix to about the consistency of thick peanut butter. Pull a furrow with the trowel and the sides should stand up without slumping in.

  1. Mix a full bag with roughly 3 quarts of water, adding the last of it slowly.
  2. Let it sit five minutes, then remix. This is called slaking and it makes the mortar noticeably more workable.
  3. Work out of a mortar board or a tub, not the wheelbarrow, so it stays close to hand.
  4. If it stiffens on the board within the working window, add a splash and remix. This is called retempering and it is fine once. Twice is pushing it. After about 90 minutes it is done and you throw it out.

Laying the block

  1. Dry-lay the whole course first with 3/8 inch gaps and confirm it fits the run. Adjust joint width slightly rather than cutting a block if you can.
  2. Snap a line or run a string. Every course gets checked against the string, not against the course below it.
  3. Throw a bed of mortar about an inch tall and a little wider than the block. Furrow it with the trowel point.
  4. Butter the head joint on the end of the block that is going down, then set it and tap it down to a 3/8 inch joint with the trowel handle.
  5. Check level along and across. Every block. This is where beginners lose the wall.
  6. When the mortar is thumbprint hard, tool the joints with a jointer or a piece of pipe. Concave joints shed water and this is not decorative in our weather.
  7. Brush the crumbs off with a soft brush once it has stiffened. Not while it is wet, you will smear it.

Honest limits

We stock Type S mortar in 60 lb bags and we do not stock block. Block, brick, cap stone and rebar are a Toledo Do it Best or a Newport masonry supply run. We are the place you come when you got halfway through and ran a bag short at four in the afternoon, which happens more than you would think.

If you are building anything that holds back a slope, supports a structure, or is taller than about three feet, hire it out or at minimum get an engineer to look at it. That honesty is worth more to you than a sale. See what is in the dry shed.
