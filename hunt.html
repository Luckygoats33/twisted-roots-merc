The department, in one sentence

A hunt wall. Not a gun counter.

Everything that goes with hunting and nothing that requires a federal license: shells and cartridges, knives and something to sharpen them on, game bags, blaze orange, glass, calls, and the pile of small things you discover you forgot at 5am on a gravel road with no signal.

Most of it hangs on one wall inside the mercantile door. The ammunition does not — that lives behind the counter, and you ask for it.

None of this is an apology. Knowing where a store stops is the useful part.

Firearms

We don't sell them. Selling firearms requires a Federal Firearms License and Twisted Roots does not hold one, so there is no gun counter here and there isn't going to be one.

Toledo and Newport both have shops that do this properly — the paperwork, the transfers, and people who have handled a thousand of them. Neither is a long drive, though it's far enough that you want to go once, knowing what you want. Ask at the counter and we'll tell you who we'd send our own family to.

Tags, licenses and seasons

Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife handles all of it, and the seasons and the draw move every single year. You'll notice this website doesn't print dates anywhere. That's deliberate. A stale date on a page nobody updates is worse than no date at all.

Start at myodfw.com before you plan anything, including the drive.

Telling you what's legal

Oregon's rules around ammunition, magazines and knives have changed more than once in recent years, and pieces of them have been in and out of court while people were still arguing about what they meant. Measure 114 is the obvious example. ORS 166 is the other one, and it has things to say about certain blades and about how you carry them.

So we're not going to stand behind a counter in a small town and give you a legal conclusion. Not because we're being coy — because we would be guessing, and you'd be the one holding the consequences.

Oregon State Police publishes the current firearms rules, and a real attorney is worth an hour of anybody's money if a lot is riding on the answer. If you ask us, that's what we'll tell you.

Ask, and it comes over the counter

Behind the counter

Ammunition isn't on an open shelf here. It sits behind the register and you ask for it by name, which is how it works in most small stores and how it's going to keep working in this one. We check ID. Nobody should take that personally — Carrie cards regulars she's known for years.

The rangefinder and the trail camera live back there too, for the unromantic reason that small expensive things walk off.

Counts come off the register, so this is what's actually in the drawer right now — not what a website thinks should be. If a box says one left, it means one, and somebody may be paying for it while you read this.

Inside the mercantile door

Arranged the way the day goes rather than the way a catalog is organized: what you cut with, then what you carry it out in.

Knives & sharpening

Everything here is a fixed blade or a plain folding work knife. If the replaceable-blade skinner looks like cheating, use one for a season and then tell us it's cheating.

The game cart lives out back with the yard stock. It's the one item in this department you should call about before driving over, because there are three of them and it's a long way to come for nothing.

The unglamorous half

Blaze orange, ears, eyes and gun care

Orange is the cheapest thing on this page and the only one anybody has ever thanked us for. It is worth knowing that ODFW has rules about visible orange for some hunters and some seasons, and that those rules are theirs to state and not ours — check before opening morning.

Blaze orange & safety

The cable lock is twelve dollars. We keep them stacked by the register year round and we will not be talked out of it.

Cleaning & sighting in

Sight in before the season, not on the second morning of it. Paper is ten dollars a dozen and a wasted weekend is not.

Glass, noise and arrows

Optics & calls

Good binoculars save more miles than good boots do. A 10×42 covers most of what this country asks of you — clearcuts, river bottoms, and a lot of standing still in the rain looking at a stump that might be something.

Archery

A small shelf, honestly. Field points, broadheads and string wax cover the things people come in for on the way past. We don't have a press and we don't tune bows.

For anything that needs a press or a paper tune, Newport. Same answer as the firearms question, same reason: it should be done by somebody who does it every day.

The part nobody plans for

Survival & navigation

Every year somebody spends an unplanned night out in the coast range, and it's almost never the person who meant to. Fog comes up the drainage, the light goes early, and a ridge you have walked forty times stops looking like itself.

A bivvy sack, a ferro rod and a whistle weigh less than a thermos and cost less than a tank of gas. The compass is there because batteries quit and phones do too, and the coast range does not care which.

Several of these are on the storm wall as well, and their reorder minimums jump when Carrie flips Storm Mode. Same gear, different bad day.

Free advice, worth what you paid

Getting one out, in order

Written down because the first time is always the time you can't remember any of it. Meat is ruined by heat and time, in that order, and almost never by anything else.

StepWhat you doWhat you need
1Deal with the tag first, exactly the way the tag itself tells you to. Read that wording at the kitchen table in September, not by headlamp.Your tag, and ten minutes ahead of time
2Get it off the ground and get the hide opened so heat can leave. A body holds warmth far longer than the air around it suggests.Fixed blade or gut hook, nitrile gloves
3Quarter it where it lies if it's an elk or the ground is bad. Two people and four quarters beat one person and an argument with gravity.Bone saw, folding skinner
4Bag every piece as it comes off. Bags keep flies and grit out and still let the meat breathe — plastic does the opposite of that.Game bags, quarter size
5Hang it in shade with air moving through, or get it into a creek in a bag. Cold and dry beats fast and warm every time.Drag rope, paracord
6Touch up the blade halfway through. A dull knife is how people cut themselves, and it happens at the tired end of the job.Diamond stone or pull-through
7Pack out heaviest first, while you still have legs and daylight. Or go get the cart, which is not cheating either.Frame pack, game cart, a friend who owes you
8Get it properly cold within hours, not overnight. If your fridge won't do it, the ice chest and a bag of ice from up front will.Cooler, ice, patience

Eric will walk through any of this with you at the counter, at whatever length you have time for, which is usually longer than you planned.

The week before

Before opening day

Short on purpose. These are the eight things people come in for at the last minute, in the order they usually realize.

  1. Check your tag and the current season and rules on ODFW's site. Do this first, because it decides everything after it.
  2. Sight in. Actually sight in, at the distance you'll actually shoot, with the ammunition you'll actually carry.
  3. Count your shells and then count them again. Everybody thinks they have a box and finds four.
  4. Put an edge on the knife now. Sharpening in the dark with cold hands is a different job.
  5. Find the orange. It's in the tote in the garage, under the Christmas lights, where it was last year.
  6. Fresh batteries in the headlamp, the rangefinder and the trail camera. Old ones go in the TV remote.
  7. Game bags, gloves and a rope in the truck. Not in the shed where they do nobody any good.
  8. Tell somebody where you're going and when you'll be back. Boring, free, and the one that matters.

While we're being honest

We stock the common ones deep, not every one shallow.

There are four rifle calibers on that shelf, plus .22, plus 12 and 20 gauge. That's it. They're the rounds people around Siletz actually feed their rifles, and we'd rather have fourteen boxes of .30-06 in October than one lonely box each of eleven things nobody here shoots.

Which means that if you shoot something unusual — and plenty of good rifles are unusual — we are going to look you in the eye and say the words "Toledo, probably." That's not us giving up on the sale. It's us saving you a second trip after we promise to order something and it takes nine days.

If enough people ask for the same round, it goes on the shelf permanently, the same way everything else here got there. Put it on the list at the counter, or use the button below.
