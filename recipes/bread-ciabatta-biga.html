Bread & Sourdough

Ciabatta With A Biga

A stiff overnight biga, 78% final hydration, and a dough you handle with a scraper and nothing else.

Two ciabatta, about 380 g each baked
Makes
35 minutes
Hands on
About 18 hours
Start to finish

A biga is a stiff yeasted preferment, around fifty percent hydration, left overnight. Stiff and cool favours the yeast over the acid-producing bacteria, so a biga contributes strength and a sweet, nutty flavour where a wet poolish contributes extensibility and a milder tang. Ciabatta wants the strength, because the final dough is wet, barely handled and expected to hold enormous holes. The name means slipper, which tells you how much shaping is involved: you cut it into rectangles and you do not touch it again.

What you need

Biga (the night before)

  • 300 g bread flour (about 2 1/2 cups)
  • 150 g cool water (about 2/3 cup)
  • 1 g instant dried yeast (about 1/4 tsp)

Final dough

  • 200 g bread flour (about 1 2/3 cups)
  • 240 g water (about 1 cup)
  • 10 g fine sea salt (about 1 3/4 tsp)
  • 2 g instant dried yeast (about 1/2 tsp)
  • 451 g biga, all of it
  • Semolina for dusting, about 3 tbsp

How

  1. Make the biga 12 to 16 hours ahead. Rub the yeast into the flour, add the water, and work it into a stiff, shaggy lump. It will not be smooth. Cover and leave at cool room temperature, around 18°C.

  2. The biga is ready when it has roughly tripled, is webbed with strands when you tear it open, and smells sweet and alcoholic. If it smells sharp and has collapsed into a puddle, the room was too warm and it is past its best — the bread will still work but will taste more acidic.

  3. Tear the biga into walnut-sized pieces into a mixer bowl or a wide tub. Add the final flour, yeast and 200 g of the water and mix until the biga pieces have broken down and the dough is uniform, four to six minutes in a mixer or eight by hand.

  4. Add the salt and the last 40 g of water in two goes, working each in until the dough comes back together. It should now be very slack but glossy, and stretch a long way before tearing. Totals are 500 g flour and 390 g water, so 78% hydration, with salt at 2%.

  5. Bulk for two to three hours with three sets of coil folds in the first ninety minutes. It is ready when it is domed, alive with bubbles, and trembles when you shake the tub.

  6. Dust the counter heavily with a mix of flour and semolina and pour the dough out. Do not degas it, do not knead it, do not shape it. Coax it into a rough rectangle with a scraper.

  7. Cut it in half with a scraper in one decisive stroke, and lift each piece onto a floured couche or a semolina-dusted parchment, stretching each gently into a slipper shape as you go.

  8. Prove 45 to 60 minutes uncovered, until puffy and slack. Ciabatta is proofed when the dough no longer holds a defined edge and a poke leaves a lasting dent.

  9. Heat the oven to 480°F (250°C) with a baking stone or heavy tray and a steam pan. Slide the loaves in, do not score them, and add steam.

  10. Bake 12 minutes with steam, then vent and bake 12 to 15 minutes more at 450°F (230°C), until the crust is blistered and deep gold, the loaves are light for their size, and an instant-read reads 208°F (98°C).

  11. Cool on a rack at least an hour. If the crumb is even and closed rather than wildly holey, the usual cause is degassing during the transfer. Use the scraper more and your hands less.

Notes

  • In a warm coastal summer the biga will run faster than the schedule says. Use cooler water and put it somewhere cold.
  • Semolina on the peel and the couche is what stops a dough this wet from welding itself to everything it touches.

Keeping it

One day. Ciabatta is a bread with a short life and it is designed to be split and filled at lunch.
