Bread & Sourdough

Baking Bread In A Damp Kitchen

What coastal humidity actually does to flour, dough and crust, and a calibration loaf to work it out on.

One loaf, about 830 g baked
Makes
35 minutes
Hands on
About 20 hours
Start to finish

Recipes are written by people in kitchens that are not yours. On this coast the air sits near saturation for months at a stretch, and open flour quietly takes on water from it — enough that a bag which behaved in August can want thirty grams less water in the middle of a wet winter. Nothing about that is a disaster, but it does explain the loaf that was fine last month and is soup this week. Here is what actually changes, what does not, and a plain loaf built to be measured against so you can find your own numbers.

What you need

Levain

  • 20 g mature starter
  • 45 g bread flour (about 6 tbsp)
  • 45 g water (about 3 tbsp)

Calibration loaf

  • 500 g bread flour (about 4 cups)
  • 320 g water to start (about 1 1/3 cups)
  • 40 g water held back (about 3 tbsp)
  • 10 g fine sea salt (about 1 3/4 tsp)
  • 110 g levain, all of it

How

  1. Understand the first variable. Flour is hygroscopic and a paper sack in a humid pantry gains moisture. That water counts as part of your hydration even though you never weighed it, so the dough behaves wetter than the recipe says. Keep flour in a sealed tub and the problem mostly goes away.

  2. Mix the flour with 320 g of water only, and rest 45 minutes. That is 64% on the flour in the bowl, stiffer than the loaf you are aiming at.

  3. Add the levain and salt, then add the held-back 40 g in two lots, watching what the dough does. If it takes both and still feels firm, your flour is dry and you can run wetter next time. If it goes slack and greasy after the first 20 g, stop there. All in, this is 555 g flour to 415 g water, or just under 75%, and with the held-back water left out it is 375 g to 555 g, or a shade under 68% — that is the range you are calibrating within.

  4. Take the dough temperature. This is the second variable and it matters more than humidity. For hand mixing, aim for 26°C in the finished dough and set the water by subtracting the flour, room and levain temperatures from three times your target: with flour at 16°C, room at 17°C and levain at 20°C, that is 78 minus 53, so water at 25°C.

  5. Four sets of folds 30 minutes apart, then bulk until risen by half with a domed and bubbled surface. Damp air does not change fermentation speed. Temperature does, and a cold damp house is slow for the second reason, not the first.

  6. Preshape, rest 25 minutes, shape tight, and set seam up in a banneton dusted with rice flour rather than wheat. Rice flour does not absorb ambient moisture and go pasty the way wheat flour does in a wet room, and this is the single most useful change for a coastal kitchen.

  7. Refrigerate 12 to 16 hours. A fridge is a dehumidifier, so the surface will dry slightly, which is good — a dry skin scores better.

  8. Heat a Dutch oven at 500°F (260°C) for 45 minutes. Score cold and bake covered 20 minutes at 480°F (250°C), then uncovered 22 to 26 minutes at 450°F (230°C), until the crust is dark brown and an instant-read reads 206°F (97°C).

  9. Bake it darker than you would inland. Crust is dried starch and it will pull moisture back out of the air within an hour of cooling; the extra few minutes buy you a crust that is still crisp at supper.

  10. Cool on a rack with air all around it, never on a board and never in a bag. Trapped steam plus damp air is how a good crust turns to leather in twenty minutes.

  11. Write down what you did: the water you ended up using, the room temperature, how long the bulk took. Three loaves of that and you will know what your own kitchen wants, which is worth more than any recipe.

  12. Two things that get blamed on humidity and are usually something else: a flat loaf is over-proofing or weak shaping, and a gummy crumb is under-baking or cutting it hot.

Notes

  • Salt draws water and clumps in damp air. Keep it dry and weigh it; a clumped teaspoon measures short.
  • Instant yeast, if you use it, deteriorates fast once opened in a humid house. Keep the packet in the freezer.

Keeping it

In a paper bag inside a bread box, not in plastic. On a wet coast, plastic keeps bread soft and turns the crust to rubber overnight.
