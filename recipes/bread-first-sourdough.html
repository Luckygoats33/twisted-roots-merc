Bread & Sourdough

Your First Sourdough Loaf

One loaf, 71% hydration, written for somebody who has never done this before.

One loaf, about 870 g baked
Makes
45 minutes
Hands on
About 24 hours, nearly all of it waiting
Start to finish

The first loaf is not the good loaf. The first loaf is the one that teaches your hands what the dough is supposed to feel like, and everything after that is adjustment. So this one is built forgiving: seventy-one percent hydration, which is wet enough to open up and dry enough to handle without swearing, a small amount of whole wheat for flavour and fermentation speed, and a long cold night in the fridge that does most of the work while you are asleep. Weights are in grams because cups measure the scoop, not the flour. If you own one thing before you start, own a scale.

What you need

Levain (the night before, or twelve hours ahead)

  • 20 g mature sourdough starter
  • 60 g bread flour (about 1/2 cup)
  • 60 g water, lukewarm (about 1/4 cup)

Dough

  • 440 g bread flour (about 3 1/2 cups)
  • 50 g whole wheat flour (about 1/3 cup)
  • 330 g water (about 1 1/3 cups)
  • 10 g fine sea salt (about 1 3/4 tsp)
  • 140 g levain, all of it from above
  • Rice flour for the banneton, about 2 tbsp

How

  1. Build the levain. Stir the starter, flour and water into a paste in a clean tub, cover loosely, and leave it somewhere around 78 degrees Fahrenheit (26°C). It is ready when it has risen, domed, and just begun to go flat in the middle — usually six to twelve hours depending on how warm the house is. Domed and rising is the window. Flat, sunken and smelling of nail polish means you missed it by a few hours; feed it again rather than pushing on.

  2. Mix the flours with the water only. No salt, no levain. Squeeze it together until there is no dry flour left, cover, and walk away for 45 minutes. This is the autolyse, and it is free gluten development — the flour hydrates and starts organising itself while you do nothing.

  3. Add the levain and the salt. Pinch and fold them through with a wet hand for two or three minutes until the dough stops looking streaky and starts feeling like one thing rather than two. It will be sticky. That is correct and it stops being true later.

  4. Bulk fermentation: four sets of stretch and folds, 30 minutes apart, then leave it alone. To fold, wet your hand, pull one side up until it resists, fold it over the middle, turn the tub a quarter and repeat four times. By the fourth set the dough should stretch a long way before it wants to tear. If it tears instead of stretching, it is under-developed — give it another twenty minutes and one more fold.

  5. Total bulk is done when the dough has grown by about half, feels airy and domed rather than slack and flat, and shows bubbles at the edge where it meets the tub. In a warm kitchen that is four hours from mixing. In a cold coastal house in winter it can be seven. Watch the dough, not the clock.

  6. Tip it onto a bare counter, no flour. Preshape into a loose round by dragging it toward you against the surface until it holds a shape, then rest it uncovered for 20 minutes. If it puddles flat immediately, it needs more tension; drag it up again.

  7. Shape properly: flip it, fold the sides to the middle, roll it up from the bottom, and roll the seam under so the surface is tight enough to be shiny. Dust a banneton or a colander lined with a tea towel heavily with rice flour, and set the loaf in seam side up.

  8. Cover and refrigerate overnight, twelve to sixteen hours at fridge temperature, around 4°C. Cold does two useful things: it develops flavour, and it firms the dough so a beginner can score it without it collapsing.

  9. Put a Dutch oven with its lid on into a cold oven and heat to 500°F (260°C). Give it a full 45 minutes after the oven says it is up to temperature. Cast iron lies about being ready long before it is.

  10. Tip the cold loaf onto parchment, score one confident slash about a centimetre deep at a shallow angle, and lower it into the pot. Lid on, drop the oven to 475°F (245°C), and bake 20 minutes. Take the lid off and bake another 22 to 25 minutes, until the crust is properly dark brown — not tan, dark — and an instant-read pushed through the base reads 205°F (96°C).

  11. Cool on a rack for at least two hours. It will tick and crackle as it cools, which is the crust contracting and is a good sign. Cutting it hot gives you a gummy centre and no more bread than if you had waited.

  12. Read the crumb. Tight and dense with a gummy line at the base means under-fermented or cut too early. Wide ragged holes with a flat pale loaf means over-fermented. A gap under the top crust usually means the shaping was loose. Change one thing next time, not three.

Notes

  • Bread flour, not all-purpose, for the first few. The extra protein is a wider margin for error.
  • No Dutch oven: bake on a preheated tray with a metal bowl inverted over the loaf for the first twenty minutes.
  • A kitchen scale that reads in single grams costs less than the flour you will waste without one.

Keeping it

Cut side down on a board for two days. After that, slice it and freeze it — refrigerating bread stales it faster than the counter does.
