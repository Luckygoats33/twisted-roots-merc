Bread & Sourdough

Keeping A Starter, And Bringing One Back

How to feed it, how to park it, and how to revive the one that has been in the back of the fridge.

One maintained starter plus a jar of usable discard
Makes
5 minutes
Hands on
2 to 5 days for a full revival
Start to finish

A starter is not a pet and it does not die easily. It is a stable culture of bacteria and yeast in a low pH environment, which is to say it is a hostile place for anything that would kill it. The grey liquid on top is alcohol, the culture is hungry, and pouring it off costs you nothing. What actually kills a starter is mould with colour and fuzz, and that is rare. Nearly everything else is a feeding problem, and feeding problems are fixed by feeding it.

What you need

Routine feed

  • 20 g existing starter
  • 40 g bread flour (about 5 tbsp)
  • 40 g water at about 30°C (about 3 tbsp)

Revival feed

  • 10 g of whatever is left, scraped from the middle not the surface
  • 25 g whole rye flour (about 3 tbsp)
  • 25 g water at about 30°C (about 2 tbsp)
  • A pinch of rye flour extra on the second day if it is sluggish

How

  1. The routine: discard down to 20 g, add 40 g flour and 40 g water, stir until no dry flour remains, mark the level. At warm room temperature it peaks in five to seven hours and is at its strongest between the dome and the first sign of flattening.

  2. To park it: feed it, let it work for an hour so the yeast is active, then lid it and put it in the fridge. A week between feeds is comfortable, three is survivable, and a month is a revival job rather than a feed.

  3. For a long absence, smear a thin layer on parchment, let it dry hard, then crumble it into a sealed bag. Dried starter keeps for a year and rehydrates with equal weight water plus a feed.

  4. Reviving. Pour off the dark liquid. Take 10 g from the middle of the mass, not the crust on top, and feed it 25 g rye and 25 g water. Leave it somewhere warm, around 78 degrees Fahrenheit (26°C).

  5. Feed it that way twice a day for two to four days. The first feeds will do almost nothing, then it will rise slowly, then it will rise fast. When it doubles inside six hours, twice running, it is back.

  6. Do not bake with it until it passes that test twice. A weak starter makes a dense loaf and you will blame your shaping instead of the culture.

  7. Know what is actually wrong. Smells like acetone or paint thinner: starving, feed it more often. Smells like sour milk and never rises: too cold, move it. Thin and soupy: too much water or too warm, stiffen the feed. Pink, orange or fuzzy growth: throw the whole thing out and start again, that one is real.

  8. Keep the discard in a covered container in the fridge and use it within a week, in flatbread, pancakes or crackers. Cold discard has no lift left but plenty of flavour, and it is free.

Notes

  • Starter kept at fridge temperature, around 4°C, gets sourer over time because the acid-producing bacteria stay active longer than the yeast does.
  • Two identical tubs, alternating, means you never have to wash a crusted one in a hurry.
  • Weigh the feed. Eyeballed feeds drift, and drifting is why a starter that worked all autumn suddenly does not.

Keeping it

Fridge, lid on, fed once a week. A skin on top is not a problem — stir it in or scrape it off, both work.
