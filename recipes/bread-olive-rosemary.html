Bread & Sourdough

Olive And Rosemary Loaf

Kalamatas folded in late, rosemary in the dough, salt dialled back because the olives bring their own.

Two loaves, about 610 g each baked
Makes
45 minutes
Hands on
About 20 hours
Start to finish

Two things go wrong with olive bread. The olives get folded in at the start and are shredded into grey streaks by the time the dough is developed, and the loaf comes out too salty because nobody adjusted for a hundred and eighty grams of brined fruit. So: olives in at the last fold, patted properly dry, and the dough salt down to one point seven percent. Rosemary goes in early, chopped fine, because whole needles in a slice are like eating a hedge. Seventy-one percent hydration, which the oil in the dough makes feel lower than it is.

What you need

Levain

  • 20 g mature starter
  • 80 g bread flour (about 2/3 cup)
  • 80 g water (about 1/3 cup)

Dough

  • 600 g bread flour (about 4 3/4 cups)
  • 400 g water (about 1 2/3 cups)
  • 12 g fine sea salt (about 2 tsp)
  • 20 g olive oil (about 1 1/2 tbsp)
  • 2 tsp rosemary needles, chopped fine
  • 180 g levain, all of it
  • 180 g pitted kalamata olives, halved and patted dry (about 1 1/4 cups)

How

  1. Build the levain to peak.

  2. Halve the olives and spread them on a tea towel for an hour. Wet olives leak brine into the dough, which slackens it and makes the crumb around each one grey and dense.

  3. Mix flour and water, rest 45 minutes, then add levain, salt, oil and rosemary and work through until uniform.

  4. Two sets of folds at 40 minute intervals. On the third set, spread the dough out flat, scatter the olives over it, and roll it up like a letter. Two more gentle folds spread them the rest of the way.

  5. Bulk until risen by half with a domed, bubbled surface. Totals are 690 g flour and 490 g water, so 71% hydration, and the salt at 12 g works out to 1.7%.

  6. Divide, preshape, rest 25 minutes. Tuck any olive that breaks the surface back inside — exposed olives burn and stick to the pot.

  7. Shape into rounds or batards, seam up in floured bannetons, and refrigerate 10 to 14 hours.

  8. Heat a Dutch oven at 500°F (260°C) for 45 minutes. Score cold, bake covered 20 minutes at 480°F (250°C), then uncovered 20 to 24 minutes at 450°F (230°C), until the crust is deep brown and an instant-read reads 205°F (96°C).

  9. Expect this loaf to colour faster than a plain one. The oil and the olive sugars push the crust along, so check it earlier than you would a lean loaf and pull it when the colour is right rather than when the timer says so.

  10. Cool at least 90 minutes. Olive bread is the one people are most tempted to cut hot and the fat makes the warm crumb pasty.

Notes

  • Green olives work but need a rinse. Anything stuffed with pimento is a different and worse bread.
  • A tablespoon of the brine in place of a tablespoon of the water is a good trade if your olives are mild.

Keeping it

Three days on the counter. It is a good loaf for grilling in a pan with more oil on day three.
