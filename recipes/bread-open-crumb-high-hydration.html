Bread & Sourdough

High Hydration, Open Crumb

Eighty-five percent water, bassinage, and an honest account of what actually makes the holes.

One large loaf, about 1 kg baked
Makes
60 minutes
Hands on
About 22 hours
Start to finish

Open crumb is mostly not about water. It is about strong flour, thorough gluten development, a bulk taken far enough to be slightly frightening, and shaping that adds tension without expelling the gas you spent all day making. Water helps because a slack dough allows bubbles to expand, but plenty of people push hydration up, keep everything else the same, and get a flat loaf with a wet streak. This runs at eighty-five percent with the last of the water added in stages once the gluten is already built, which is the only way that number is manageable at home.

What you need

Levain

  • 20 g mature starter
  • 100 g bread flour (about 3/4 cup plus 1 tbsp)
  • 100 g water (about 1/3 cup plus 1 tbsp)

Dough

  • 460 g high-protein bread flour, 13% or better (about 3 3/4 cups)
  • 50 g whole wheat flour (about 1/3 cup)
  • 357 g water for the mix (about 1 1/2 cups)
  • 60 g water held back for bassinage (about 1/4 cup)
  • 12 g fine sea salt (about 2 tsp)
  • 220 g levain, all of it

How

  1. Build the levain and use it young, at about three-quarters of its full rise. A young levain has more yeast activity and less acid, and acid degrades gluten, which is exactly what you cannot afford at this hydration.

  2. Mix the flours with 357 g water only. Rest one hour. The dough should already feel cohesive rather than sloppy at this stage.

  3. Add the levain, work it in, rest 20 minutes, then add the salt. Now slap and fold on the counter for five to eight minutes until the dough is smooth, tightens visibly as you work it, and passes a windowpane — pulled thin enough to see light through without tearing.

  4. Bassinage: add the held-back 60 g of water 20 g at a time, working each addition fully in before the next. The dough will break down into a slurry and come back each time. If it will not come back after five minutes of work, stop and leave the rest of the water out.

  5. Four sets of coil folds at 45 minute intervals, handled gently. From here you are protecting gas, not building strength.

  6. Bulk until the dough has grown by 75 percent, is domed and jiggly, and large bubbles are visible against the side of the tub. Totals are 620 g flour and 527 g water, which is 85% hydration, with salt at 1.9%. This is the step people cut short and it is the main reason open crumb does not happen.

  7. Turn out gently on a lightly floured counter. Preshape with a scraper and the lightest hand you have, and rest 30 minutes.

  8. Shape without degassing: fold the sides in, roll it up, and stitch the seam rather than dragging the whole thing across the counter. Into a well-floured banneton, seam up.

  9. Refrigerate 12 to 16 hours. This dough is much easier to handle cold and effectively impossible to score warm.

  10. Heat a Dutch oven at 500°F (260°C) for 45 minutes. Score one decisive slash, bake covered 22 minutes, then uncovered at 460°F (240°C) for 22 to 26 minutes until the crust is very dark and an instant-read reads 208°F (98°C).

  11. Cool three hours minimum. A high hydration loaf holds a lot of steam and the crumb needs all of that time to set.

  12. If you get one enormous tunnel under the crust and dense bread below it, the shaping was loose. If the whole crumb is even but tight, the bulk was short. If it spread flat and the crumb is wild and irregular, it went too far.

Notes

  • All-purpose flour will not do this. Protein at 13% or higher is the entry requirement.
  • In a damp week, hold back more of the bassinage water. Flour that has been sitting open in a coastal kitchen has already taken on some of its own.

Keeping it

Two days, and it is at its best on the first. This is a loaf that rewards being eaten promptly.
