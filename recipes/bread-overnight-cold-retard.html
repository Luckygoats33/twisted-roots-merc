Bread & Sourdough

The Overnight Cold Retard

Two loaves shaped at night, baked in the morning, 74% hydration.

Two loaves, about 690 g each baked
Makes
50 minutes
Hands on
About 22 hours
Start to finish

Cold proofing is the single change that makes home sourdough look like bakery sourdough. Chilled dough is firm enough to score cleanly, the crust blisters because the surface starch behaves differently after a long cold rest, and the flavour deepens because the bacteria keep working after the yeast has mostly stopped. It also decouples shaping from baking, which is the practical part: you shape when you have time at night and bake when you want bread. Two loaves, because if the oven is on for one it should be on for two.

What you need

Levain

  • 20 g mature starter
  • 70 g bread flour (about 1/2 cup plus 1 tbsp)
  • 70 g water (about 1/4 cup plus 1 tbsp)

Dough

  • 700 g bread flour (about 5 2/3 cups)
  • 100 g whole wheat flour (about 3/4 cup)
  • 570 g water (about 2 1/3 cups)
  • 16 g fine sea salt (about 2 3/4 tsp)
  • 160 g levain, all of it

How

  1. Build the levain six to ten hours before you mix. Use it when it is domed and lively rather than sunk.

  2. Mix flours and water and rest 45 minutes. Add levain and salt, pinch through, and rest 30 minutes. Totals here are 880 g flour and 650 g water — 74% hydration — with salt at 1.8%.

  3. Four sets of folds at 30 minute intervals. By the last set the dough should hold a smooth dome for a few seconds after you stop touching it. If it slumps immediately it needs more time or another fold.

  4. Bulk until risen by half with visible bubbles under the surface and a domed, jiggly feel when you shake the tub. Four to six hours from mixing in a normal kitchen.

  5. Divide in two, preshape each into a loose round, rest 25 minutes uncovered.

  6. Shape into batards or rounds, seam side up in floured bannetons, and cover. Give them 30 minutes at room temperature before they go in the fridge — that head start matters if your fridge runs cold.

  7. Refrigerate ten to sixteen hours at around 4°C. A poke on the cold dough should spring back slowly and leave a shallow dent. If it springs straight back it is under-proofed and wants another hour on the counter tomorrow.

  8. Heat the oven and a Dutch oven to 500°F (260°C), a full 45 minutes. Bake the loaves one at a time and leave the second in the fridge until its turn.

  9. Score cold, straight from the fridge. Bake covered 20 minutes at 480°F (250°C), then uncovered 20 to 24 minutes until the crust is deep brown and the base sounds hollow rather than dull when tapped. An instant-read reads 205°F (96°C) at the centre.

  10. Cool completely on a rack. If the crust softens as it cools, the loaf was slightly underbaked; next time take it four minutes further and accept the darker colour.

Notes

  • Blistering comes from cold dough into a very hot pot. A warm shaped loaf will not blister no matter what else you do.
  • If your fridge is packed, a covered tub on a cold porch works in winter as long as it stays below about 8°C.

Keeping it

Both loaves keep two days on the counter. Freeze the second one sliced the day it is baked rather than watching it go stale.
