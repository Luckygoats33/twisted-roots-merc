Bread & Sourdough

Forty Percent Rye With A Rye Sour

A dense-crumbed caraway rye built on a separate rye sour, at 68% hydration.

One large loaf, about 1 kg baked
Makes
40 minutes
Hands on
About 22 hours
Start to finish

Rye has almost no useful gluten and a great deal of pentosan gum, which means it holds water like a sponge and builds structure by a different mechanism than wheat. It also needs acid: without it the starch-degrading enzymes in rye keep working during the bake and leave you with a sticky, unset crumb. A separate rye sour handles that. Forty percent is the point where you get real rye flavour and a tight moist crumb while the wheat still does the lifting, which makes it the most useful rye ratio for anybody not running a proper rye oven.

What you need

Rye sour

  • 20 g mature starter
  • 150 g medium rye flour (about 1 1/4 cups)
  • 150 g water at about 30°C (about 2/3 cup)

Dough

  • 420 g bread flour (about 3 1/3 cups)
  • 120 g medium rye flour (about 1 cup)
  • 316 g water (about 1 1/4 cups plus 1 tbsp)
  • 14 g fine sea salt (about 2 1/2 tsp)
  • 8 g caraway seeds, lightly crushed (about 1 1/2 tsp)
  • 320 g rye sour, all of it

How

  1. Build the rye sour 12 to 16 hours ahead and leave it at cool room temperature. Rye sour looks like thick porridge and does not dome the way a wheat levain does. It is ready when it is domed with fine bubbles, smells sharply sour, and has just started to crack across the top.

  2. Mix everything together at once. Rye does not benefit from an autolyse and it does not need one.

  3. Work the dough for three or four minutes only. It will feel tacky and clay-like and will never be smooth or elastic. Chasing a windowpane in a rye dough is chasing something that does not exist.

  4. Bulk for two to three hours with one fold at the hour mark, until it has risen by about a third and the surface is pocked with small bubbles. Totals here are 700 g flour and 476 g water, so 68% hydration, and salt is at 2% — rye needs the full amount to slow the enzymes.

  5. Shape with wet hands into a tight oval on a wet counter, smoothing the surface with water rather than flour. Set it seam down in a heavily floured oval banneton, or into a buttered 9 x 5 inch tin if you would rather not gamble.

  6. Proof 60 to 90 minutes at room temperature. Rye is proofed when fine cracks open across the surface and a poke leaves a dent that does not spring back. Waiting for a wheat-style dramatic rise will take you well past it.

  7. Heat the oven to 450°F (230°C) with a tray for steam. Do not score deeply; rye tears where it wants to. A few shallow docks with a skewer are enough.

  8. Bake 15 minutes with steam, then vent the oven, drop to 400°F (205°C) and bake 40 to 50 minutes more, until the crust is very dark, the loaf sounds hollow underneath, and an instant-read reads 208°F (98°C). Rye is one of the few breads where a lower internal temperature genuinely leaves a gummy centre.

  9. Cool it for a minimum of eight hours, and a full day is better. Rye crumb sets by cooling, not by baking, and a rye loaf cut warm is sticky and ruined.

  10. If the crumb is dense and wet with a dark seam near the base, it was cut too early or underbaked. If it is crumbly and dry, the sour was too young and there was not enough acid.

Notes

  • Medium rye is the workhorse. Dark rye ferments faster and gets stickier; light rye behaves more like wheat and tastes like less.
  • Crush the caraway lightly with the flat of a knife. Whole seeds pass through; crushed ones flavour the loaf.

Keeping it

Wrapped in a cloth on the counter, this improves for two days and keeps for five. It is the best keeper here by a distance.
