Bread & Sourdough

Same-Day Sourdough

Mixed after breakfast, out of the oven by supper, at 70% hydration.

One loaf, about 900 g baked
Makes
40 minutes
Hands on
9 to 10 hours
Start to finish

Sourdough does not require two days. It requires enough fermentation, and you get that either from time or from a large, young, warm levain. This loaf uses nineteen percent prefermented flour and a deliberately warm kitchen, which cuts the schedule to a single day. The trade is flavour: a fast loaf is milder and less complex than one that spent a night in the fridge, and it will not blister. What it will do is be finished and cooled by the time people are hungry, which some days is the whole requirement.

What you need

Levain (early morning, 4 hours ahead)

  • 30 g mature starter, recently fed and lively
  • 100 g bread flour (about 3/4 cup plus 1 tbsp)
  • 100 g water at about 30°C (about 1/3 cup plus 1 tbsp)

Dough

  • 435 g bread flour (about 3 1/2 cups)
  • 50 g whole wheat flour (about 1/3 cup)
  • 305 g water at about 30°C (about 1 1/4 cups)
  • 11 g fine sea salt (about 2 tsp)
  • 230 g levain, all of it

How

  1. Build the levain warm and use it young — at three to four hours, when it has roughly doubled and is still climbing. A young levain is sweeter and stronger. A peaked one works but pushes the sour up.

  2. Mix everything at once, levain and salt included, using warm water. Skip the autolyse; you are trading a little extensibility for an hour of clock. Work it in the bowl for three minutes until cohesive.

  3. Rest 20 minutes, then slap and fold on the counter for two minutes until the dough goes from ragged to smooth and starts sticking to itself rather than to your hands.

  4. Three sets of coil folds at 40 minute intervals. Keep the tub somewhere around 78 degrees Fahrenheit (26°C) — an oven with the light on is the usual trick.

  5. Bulk finishes at four to five hours from mixing, when the dough is up by two-thirds, domed, and full of bubbles you can see through the side of the tub. Warm dough moves faster than you expect at the end, so start checking at three and a half.

  6. Preshape, rest 20 minutes, shape tight, and proof at room temperature in a floured banneton for 60 to 90 minutes. The poke test rules here: a floured fingertip should leave a dent that fills back in slowly and not quite completely.

  7. Heat the Dutch oven at 500°F (260°C) for 45 minutes while the loaf proofs. Chill the shaped loaf in the freezer for the last 15 minutes if you can — cold skin scores better.

  8. Score and bake covered 20 minutes at 480°F (250°C), then uncovered 20 to 25 minutes until the crust is a solid brown and the internal temperature is 205°F (96°C).

  9. Cool two hours. A same-day loaf is more fragile than a retarded one and cutting it warm does more damage.

  10. If the crumb comes out tight and the loaf is squat, the bulk was short — warm ferments finish faster and people still stop at the four-hour mark out of habit. Judge it by rise and bubbles.

Notes

  • This is the schedule to use when your starter is at its strongest, not when it is recovering.
  • A proofing box, or a cooler with a jug of hot water in the bottom, makes a January kitchen behave like a July one.

Keeping it

Best the day it is baked. It goes stale a day sooner than a cold-proofed loaf, which is the other half of the trade.
