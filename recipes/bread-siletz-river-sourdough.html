Bread & Sourdough

Siletz River Sourdough

The house loaf: two big rounds at 75% hydration, with rye and whole wheat in the mix.

Two loaves, about 840 g each baked
Makes
55 minutes
Hands on
About 26 hours
Start to finish

This is the loaf that goes on the rack at six and is usually gone by nine. It is mostly strong white flour with a hundred grams of whole wheat and fifty of rye, which is enough to give it colour and a bit of pasture flavour without making it heavy. Hydration sits just over seventy-five percent, high enough for an open, custardy crumb and low enough that you can shape it without a bench scraper welded to your hand. Everything about it is ordinary technique done attentively, which is the only trick there is.

What you need

Levain

  • 20 g mature starter
  • 100 g bread flour (about 3/4 cup plus 1 tbsp)
  • 100 g water (about 1/3 cup plus 1 tbsp)

Dough

  • 800 g strong bread flour (about 6 1/2 cups)
  • 100 g whole wheat flour (about 3/4 cup)
  • 50 g whole rye flour (about 1/3 cup)
  • 690 g water (about 2 3/4 cups plus 2 tbsp)
  • 19 g fine sea salt (about 1 tbsp)
  • 220 g levain, all of it

How

  1. Build the levain the evening before and use it in the morning at its peak — domed, smelling of yogurt, not yet collapsing.

  2. Mix the three flours with 620 g of the water. Hold back 70 g. Rest one hour covered. Holding water back is worth the trouble: the flour tells you during mixing whether it wants all of it, and in a wet coastal week it often does not.

  3. Add the levain, work it through, rest 20 minutes, then add the salt with the held-back water a splash at a time. The dough will loosen and go slippery and then come back together. If it will not take the last of the water without turning to soup, stop and leave it out.

  4. Four sets of stretch and folds, 45 minutes apart. Judge development by the windowpane: pull a corner thin and hold it to the light. Translucent without tearing is done. Tearing at the edges means keep going.

  5. Bulk ends four to six hours from mixing, when the dough has grown by half to two-thirds, the surface is domed and blistered, and it wobbles as one mass. Totals are 1060 g flour and 800 g water, so 75.5% hydration, with salt at 1.8%.

  6. Divide into two pieces of about 940 g. Preshape round, rest 30 minutes uncovered so a skin forms.

  7. Shape tight, seam up into floured bannetons, 20 minutes at room temperature, then into the fridge for 12 to 16 hours at around 4°C.

  8. Heat the oven with a Dutch oven inside to 500°F (260°C) for 45 minutes. Bake one loaf at a time.

  9. Score the cold loaf with one long off-centre slash held at a shallow angle. Bake covered 22 minutes at 480°F (250°C), then uncovered 22 to 26 minutes at 450°F (230°C), until the crust is mahogany brown and the internal temperature reads 206°F (97°C).

  10. Reheat the pot for at least 15 minutes before the second loaf goes in. A cool pot is the usual reason the second loaf is flatter than the first.

  11. Cool both on racks a minimum of two hours. Cut into one before that and the crumb will smear rather than slice.

  12. If the ear does not open and the crust is smooth and dull, either the score was too shallow and too flat, or the loaf was over-proofed and had nothing left to push with. A poke on the cold dough that does not spring back at all points to the second.

Notes

  • Rye ferments faster than white. If your kitchen runs warm, drop the rye to 30 g rather than shortening the bulk.
  • Sold whole at the counter, and it makes the best of the sandwiches here, which is not a coincidence.

Keeping it

Cut side down on a board, three days. It toasts well on days two and three, which is when most of it gets eaten anyway.
