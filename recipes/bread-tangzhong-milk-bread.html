Bread & Sourdough

Tangzhong Milk Bread

A cooked flour paste that lets a soft loaf hold far more liquid, and stay soft for four days.

One 9 x 5 inch loaf, about 750 g baked
Makes
40 minutes
Hands on
About 5 hours
Start to finish

Tangzhong is one part flour cooked with five parts liquid until it thickens into a paste. Gelatinised starch holds several times its weight in water and holds onto it through baking and for days afterward, which is why a milk bread made this way is still soft on day four while a plain enriched loaf is stale on day two. It is five minutes of work at a stove and it changes the entire loaf. This one is a straight-yeasted same-day bread, shaped as three coils in a tin so it pulls apart along the seams.

What you need

Tangzhong

  • 25 g bread flour (about 3 tbsp)
  • 125 g whole milk (about 1/2 cup)

Dough

  • 375 g bread flour (about 3 cups)
  • 145 g whole milk, lukewarm (about 2/3 cup)
  • 1 large egg, about 50 g
  • 50 g caster sugar (about 1/4 cup)
  • 8 g fine sea salt (about 1 1/2 tsp)
  • 6 g instant dried yeast (about 2 tsp)
  • 45 g unsalted butter, soft (about 3 tbsp)

Finish

  • 1 egg beaten with 1 tbsp milk, for the wash
  • 10 g butter for brushing after baking (about 2 tsp)

How

  1. Whisk the tangzhong flour and milk together cold in a small pan, then cook over medium heat, stirring constantly, until it thickens to the consistency of loose mashed potato and the whisk leaves tracks that hold — about 65°C, and two to three minutes. Scrape it into a bowl and cool it to lukewarm.

  2. Combine the dough flour, sugar, salt and yeast, keeping the salt and yeast on opposite sides of the bowl until you mix. Add the milk, egg and the cooled tangzhong.

  3. Mix to a shaggy dough, then knead 8 to 10 minutes until smooth. It will be tacky. Resist adding flour — the tangzhong makes it feel wetter than it is.

  4. Add the soft butter in three lots, kneading each fully in. The dough will slacken and look broken each time and will come back. When it is done it should pull into a thin translucent windowpane without tearing.

  5. Bulk in a covered bowl until doubled, 60 to 90 minutes in a warm kitchen. The liquid comes to about 80% of the flour weight, which reads high until you remember the tangzhong has locked most of it up.

  6. Knock back gently and divide into three equal pieces of about 275 g. Roll each into an oval, fold the long sides in, then roll it up tightly from the short end into a coil.

  7. Set the three coils side by side, spirals facing out, in a buttered 9 x 5 inch tin.

  8. Prove covered 45 to 70 minutes, until the dough crowns about 2 cm above the rim and a light poke springs back slowly leaving a shallow mark.

  9. Heat the oven to 350°F (175°C). Brush with the egg wash, gently — an aggressive brush will deflate a soft dough at this stage.

  10. Bake 32 to 38 minutes, until the top is deep glossy brown, the loaf has pulled slightly from the sides of the tin, and an instant-read in the centre reads 190°F (88°C). Cover with foil at 25 minutes if the top is running ahead.

  11. Turn out immediately and brush the hot crust with butter, which keeps it soft as it cools.

  12. Cool at least two hours before tearing into it. If the crumb is dense and the pull-apart seams did not form, the shaping was loose or the prove was cut short.

Notes

  • The same dough makes excellent burger buns at 90 g a piece, baked 16 to 18 minutes at 375°F (190°C) to the same internal temperature.
  • Tangzhong works in most enriched doughs. Take 5% of the flour and 25% of the liquid, cook them together, and reduce both elsewhere in the recipe by the same amounts.

Keeping it

Four days in a sealed bag at room temperature, which is the whole point of the method. Freezes cleanly sliced.
