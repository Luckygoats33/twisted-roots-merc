Bread & Sourdough

Wholewheat Levain

Eighty-five percent whole grain at 82% hydration, without the density that usually comes with it.

Two loaves, about 640 g each baked
Makes
45 minutes
Hands on
About 20 hours
Start to finish

Wholewheat bread is usually bad for two reasons: not enough water, and too much time. Bran is sharp and it cuts gluten strands, so a whole grain dough needs more water to stay extensible and less fermentation before that damage compounds into a brick. This runs at eighty-two percent hydration, which sounds alarming until you handle it and find that the bran has drunk most of it. The bulk is deliberately short. The fifteen percent white flour is there for structure and it is the difference between a loaf and a doorstop.

What you need

Levain

  • 20 g mature starter
  • 70 g whole wheat flour (about 1/2 cup plus 1 tbsp)
  • 70 g water (about 1/4 cup plus 1 tbsp)

Dough

  • 600 g whole wheat flour, stone ground if you have it (about 5 cups)
  • 120 g bread flour (about 1 cup)
  • 576 g water (about 2 1/3 cups plus 1 tbsp)
  • 16 g fine sea salt (about 2 3/4 tsp)
  • 160 g levain, all of it

How

  1. Build the levain on whole wheat. It will move faster than a white one — whole grain carries more of its own enzymes and minerals — so check it at five hours rather than eight.

  2. Mix the flours and all the water and leave one full hour. A long autolyse matters more here than in any white loaf, because the bran needs time to soften before you develop anything. Skip it and the dough will feel like wet sand.

  3. Add levain and salt and squeeze through thoroughly. The dough will be sticky and slightly gritty and will not come clean off your hand. That is the bran, not a mistake.

  4. Three sets of coil folds, 40 minutes apart. Strength comes slowly and then all at once; by the third fold it should hold a dome and stop spreading flat in the tub.

  5. Bulk only until risen by about half, three and a half to four and a half hours warm. Totals are 800 g flour and 656 g water, which is 82% hydration, with salt at 2%. Whole wheat over-ferments quietly — it goes slack and smells faintly of cheese before it looks wrong, so err early.

  6. Divide, preshape gently, rest 20 minutes. Handle lightly; there is less gluten here to repair after rough treatment.

  7. Shape and set seam up in bannetons dusted with rice flour. Whole wheat sticks harder than white, so use more than looks sensible.

  8. Refrigerate 10 to 14 hours at around 4°C.

  9. Heat a Dutch oven at 500°F (260°C) for 45 minutes. Score cold, bake covered 22 minutes at 475°F (245°C), then uncovered 20 to 24 minutes until the crust is dark brown with a reddish cast and an instant-read reads 207°F (97°C).

  10. Cool three hours. Whole grain crumb sets slowly and a loaf cut at one hour will read gummy even when it is baked through.

  11. If the crumb is tight and the loaf is short, the two likely causes are not enough water — flour varies, and stone ground varies most — or a bulk that ran long. Add 20 g water before you add time.

Notes

  • Fresh-milled flour drinks noticeably more water than bagged, and it ferments faster. Expect to adjust both.
  • Bran can be softened further by scalding 100 g of the flour with 200 g boiling water the night before and cooling it — a soaker buys you a lighter crumb at the cost of a day.

Keeping it

Three days on the counter. Whole grain loaves stale from the outside in and revive well with five minutes in a hot oven.
