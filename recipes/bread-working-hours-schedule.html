Bread & Sourdough

Sourdough On A Working Schedule

A 74% loaf timed around a shift, not around somebody who is home all day.

One loaf, about 900 g baked
Makes
35 minutes, split into four short visits
Hands on
About 36 hours, none of it during working hours
Start to finish

Most sourdough writing assumes you are standing next to the tub for six hours on a Saturday. Most people are not. This one is arranged around a normal working day: you touch the dough before you leave in the morning, again when you get home, and again the following evening, and every long fermentation happens either while you are at work or while you are asleep. It is not a compromise loaf. The cool overnight bulk and the long cold proof are what a slow schedule buys you, and they make better bread than rushing it in a warm kitchen would.

What you need

Levain (built before work, day one)

  • 20 g mature starter
  • 80 g bread flour (about 2/3 cup)
  • 80 g water, cool (about 1/3 cup)

Dough (mixed that evening)

  • 450 g bread flour (about 3 2/3 cups)
  • 50 g whole wheat flour (about 1/3 cup)
  • 345 g water (about 1 1/3 cups plus 1 tbsp)
  • 10 g fine sea salt (about 1 3/4 tsp)
  • 180 g levain, all of it

How

  1. Morning, day one, five minutes. Build the levain with cool water and leave it on the counter, not anywhere warm. Built cool and left at ordinary room temperature it takes ten to twelve hours to peak, which puts it ready when you walk back in the door.

  2. Evening, day one. Check the levain: domed, webbed with bubbles, only just starting to sink. If it has sunk hard, it is past peak — usable, but the loaf will be sourer and rise a little less.

  3. Mix the flours and water, leave 30 minutes, then add levain and salt and pinch through until uniform. Total flour here is 590 g and total water 435 g, which is 74% hydration, and the salt at 10 g is 1.7% — deliberately just under the usual two, because a long cool ferment does not need holding back much.

  4. Three sets of folds at 30 minute intervals while you eat. Then cover and leave it out for an hour, and put the whole tub in the fridge overnight.

  5. Morning, day two, two minutes. Take the tub out and leave it on the counter before you go. It will finish its bulk slowly through the day as it warms — this is the step people distrust and it works. It is done when it has grown by half and the surface is domed and bubbled.

  6. Evening, day two. Preshape on a bare counter, rest 20 minutes, shape tight, and put it seam up in a floured banneton. If the dough feels slack and refuses to hold a round, it went slightly too far during the day; shape it tighter and expect a flatter loaf.

  7. Straight back into the fridge, at least two hours and up to sixteen. From here you bake whenever you like, including at ten at night, which is often when there is finally time.

  8. Heat a Dutch oven at 500°F (260°C) for 45 minutes. Score the cold loaf and bake covered 20 minutes, then uncovered at 460°F (240°C) for 20 to 25 minutes more, until the crust is dark and the internal temperature reads 205°F (96°C).

  9. Cool at least two hours before cutting, which means the loaf is ready for the next morning rather than for tonight. Plan on that.

  10. If your evenings are unreliable, the dough will wait in the fridge shaped for a second night. It will be sourer and the crust will blister more. Neither is a fault.

Notes

  • The whole schedule shifts an hour or two in either direction without breaking. Cold slows everything down and forgives the rest.
  • In a cold house the day-two counter bulk can take a full nine hours. In a warm one, watch it after five.

Keeping it

Two days cut-side down on a board, then sliced and frozen. Toast it straight from frozen.
