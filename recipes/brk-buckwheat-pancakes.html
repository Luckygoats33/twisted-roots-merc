Breakfast & The Griddle

Buckwheat Pancakes

Earthy, dark and nutty, with enough wheat flour in them to still be a pancake and not a hockey puck.

About 12 pancakes
Makes
20 minutes
Hands on
30 minutes
Start to finish

Buckwheat is not wheat and is not even a grass; it is a seed related to rhubarb, with no gluten at all. That matters practically. All-buckwheat pancakes have nothing to hold gas, so they cook up dense, crumbly and grimly virtuous. Half buckwheat and half all-purpose gives you the flavour — mineral, faintly bitter, deeply nutty — with a structure that still rises and holds together on the spatula. The other adjustment is sweetness. Buckwheat is assertive enough that a little molasses or brown sugar in the batter stops it tasting austere.

What you need

Dry

  • 150 g buckwheat flour
  • 150 g all-purpose flour
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 3/4 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 teaspoon fine salt

Wet

  • 500 ml buttermilk
  • 2 large eggs
  • 50 g butter, melted
  • 2 tablespoons molasses or dark brown sugar

Griddle and table

  • 2 tablespoons butter for greasing
  • Maple syrup, to taste
  • A pinch of flaky salt for the top, optional

How

  1. Whisk the two flours, baking powder, soda and salt together well. Buckwheat flour is heavier and settles, so give it more mixing than you would give plain flour.

  2. Whisk the buttermilk, eggs, melted butter and molasses together until the molasses is fully dispersed and no dark threads remain at the bottom of the bowl.

  3. Combine with a few folds, stopping while lumpy. Buckwheat has no gluten but the wheat half does, and the same rule applies — overmixed batter gives tough pancakes.

  4. Rest fifteen minutes. Buckwheat flour is thirsty and absorbs liquid slowly; batter that seemed correct straight after mixing will have thickened noticeably by the time it hits the griddle. Loosen with a splash of buttermilk if it has gone stiff.

  5. Heat a griddle to 350°F (175°C) — slightly cooler than for a white-flour pancake, because the sugars in buckwheat and molasses brown faster and will go past dark before the middle sets. Test with a drop of water: it should bead and skate for a couple of seconds.

  6. Ladle 60 ml portions and cook until bubbles break over the surface and the edges dull from glossy to matte, about two minutes. The colour underneath is hard to read on a dark batter, so go by the edges and the bubbles rather than by peeking.

  7. Flip once. Cook until the pancake springs back in the centre when pressed and feels light rather than heavy on the spatula, about ninety seconds.

  8. Serve with a lot of butter. Buckwheat is low in fat and tastes lean; butter is doing structural work here, not decoration. A little flaky salt on top plays against the molasses.

Notes

  • Going further: up to seventy percent buckwheat is workable if you add a second egg for structure. Past that they crumble when you turn them.

  • Buckwheat flour goes rancid faster than wheat flour because of its fat content. If it smells sharp, dusty or like old paint rather than nutty, it is past it and no recipe will hide it. Keep it cold.

  • Naturally leavened variant: replace 100 g of the flour and 100 ml of the buttermilk with 200 g of sourdough discard and let the batter sit overnight. The sour suits buckwheat better than it suits white flour.

Keeping it

Cooked pancakes freeze well for two months, stacked with parchment. The batter does not hold overnight once leavened — the powder spends itself and the buckwheat swells into paste.
