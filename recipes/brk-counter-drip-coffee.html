Breakfast & The Griddle

Counter Drip Coffee, Done Right

Grind, ratio, water temperature. Three variables, and the machine on the counter is not one of them.

About 1 litre, 4 mugs
Makes
5 minutes
Hands on
10 minutes
Start to finish

People spend a lot of money trying to buy their way out of bad coffee, and the counter drip machine takes most of the blame for a result it did not cause. Coffee has three variables that matter and one that mostly does not. The three are the ratio of coffee to water, the grind size, and how long the water is in contact with the grounds. The one that mostly does not is the machine, provided it gets the water hot enough. Get the ratio right, grind fresh and to the correct size, keep the water in the right range, and a twenty-dollar drip machine makes coffee that is entirely fine.

What you need

Coffee and water

  • 60 g whole bean coffee, for 1 litre
  • 1 litre filtered water
  • 1 paper filter, or a permanent basket filter

Useful, not essential

  • 1 kitchen scale reading to 1 g
  • 1 burr grinder
  • 1 thermal carafe, 1 litre

How

  1. Start with the ratio and treat it as fixed: 60 g of coffee per litre of water, which is about 4 level tablespoons of ground coffee per 500 ml. Weigh it once and mark your scoop if you are not going to keep weighing. Almost all weak drip coffee is a ratio problem, and almost everyone under-doses.

  2. Grind immediately before brewing. Ground coffee loses its aromatic compounds within about fifteen minutes of grinding and goes noticeably stale within a day. This is the single largest improvement available to most people, ahead of buying better beans.

  3. Grind to the texture of coarse sand or raw sugar for a flat-bottom drip basket. Too fine and the water stalls in the bed and over-extracts into something bitter and drying; too coarse and it runs straight through and you get thin, sour, tea-like coffee.

  4. Use filtered water if your tap water tastes of anything. Coffee is about ninety-eight percent water, and chlorinated or heavily mineralised water comes through clearly in the cup. Distilled water is the other extreme and makes flat coffee — some mineral content is needed for extraction.

  5. Water should hit the grounds between 195°F (91°C) and 205°F (96°C). Most drip machines land in that range. If yours makes coffee that is always sour and thin no matter what you do, it is probably brewing too cool, and no adjustment to grind will fix it.

  6. Rinse the paper filter with hot water before adding the grounds and discard the rinse water. Paper tastes like paper, and it takes about four seconds to deal with.

  7. Level the coffee bed flat in the basket before starting. A sloped bed means water runs through the shallow side and barely touches the deep side, which gives you over-extracted and under-extracted coffee in the same pot.

  8. Brewing should take four to six minutes for a full pot. Much faster and the grind is too coarse; much slower and it is too fine. This is the one diagnostic that tells you which way to adjust.

  9. Get it off the hot plate immediately. A hot plate continues to cook coffee, driving off aromatics and stewing the remaining compounds into something flat and bitter, and it does noticeable damage within twenty minutes. Pour it into a thermal carafe rinsed out with hot water first, so that it stays good for a couple of hours.

  10. Taste it black before you add anything. Bitter and drying means over-extraction — go coarser. Sour, thin and lacking body means under-extraction — go finer or add more coffee. Those two adjustments cover almost every problem a drip machine will hand you.

Notes

  • Blade grinders chop rather than grind, producing a mix of dust and boulders. The dust over-extracts and the boulders under-extract at the same time, and the cup tastes muddled. If you use one, pulse in short bursts and shake the grinder between them.

  • Beans go stale, not bad. They are best from about three days to three weeks after roasting. Keep them in a sealed opaque container at room temperature. The freezer works for long storage but not for daily beans, because repeated condensation ruins them.

  • Cleaning matters more than it sounds. Coffee oils go rancid on the basket, the carafe and inside the machine, and rancid oil tastes exactly like it sounds. Wash the removable parts with soap regularly and run a descaling cycle every couple of months.

Keeping it

Brewed coffee is at its best for about twenty minutes and drinkable from a thermal carafe for two hours. It does not improve. Leftover coffee cooled and kept cold makes decent iced coffee for two days, and freezes into cubes that chill iced coffee without watering it down.
