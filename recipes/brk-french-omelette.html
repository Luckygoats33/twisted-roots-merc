Breakfast & The Griddle

The French Omelette

Pale, rolled, no browning, soft in the middle. A two-minute egg dish that takes a couple of tries to learn.

1 omelette
Makes
6 minutes
Hands on
6 minutes
Start to finish

An American diner omelette is browned, folded in half and stuffed. A French omelette is pale as a new pillow, rolled into a cylinder, and holds barely anything but eggs and butter. It is worth learning because it is the fastest hot food there is and because it exposes everything: too much heat and it browns, too little and it slumps, too much stirring and it becomes scrambled eggs in a coat. Three eggs, a nonstick pan of about 20 cm, and roughly two minutes. Expect to be bad at it twice before you are good at it.

What you need

Omelette

  • 3 large eggs
  • 1 tablespoon (14 g) unsalted butter
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • Black pepper, to taste

Optional, inside

  • 20 g gruyere or sharp cheddar, grated fine
  • 1 teaspoon chopped chives
  • 2 tablespoons soft goat cheese

How

  1. Beat the eggs and salt hard with a fork until entirely uniform and slightly foamy on top. Any unmixed white will show as a rubbery white ribbon in the middle of a very pale omelette, where it has nowhere to hide.

  2. Set a 20 cm nonstick pan over medium heat and add the butter. When it has melted and is foaming steadily but has not begun to colour, swirl it up the sides. If it browns, the pan is too hot for this dish; wipe it and start again.

  3. Pour in the eggs and immediately start stirring with a silicone spatula, small fast circles, while shaking the pan gently back and forth with the other hand. Keep it moving for about twenty seconds, until the eggs look like very loose, small-curd scramble with no liquid pooling.

  4. Stop stirring. Smooth the top flat with the spatula and let the omelette sit for fifteen to twenty seconds, until the underside has just set into a single sheet and the surface is still glistening and slightly wet. Scatter the cheese or herbs down the centre now.

  5. Tilt the pan away from you to about forty-five degrees. Use the spatula to fold the near third of the omelette over the middle, then knock the handle so the far edge lifts and rolls over on itself. Do not chase perfection here; a sloppy roll still tastes right.

  6. Roll it out onto a warm plate, seam side down. It should be pale yellow all over with no brown spots, and give a little when pressed. If you cut it open, the centre should be creamy and just short of runny.

  7. Rub the top with the butter wrapper or a small knob of butter to give it a shine, grind pepper over it, and eat it right away.

Notes

  • It browned: heat too high, every time. The window between properly foaming butter and browning butter is about fifteen seconds, and if you get distracted you missed it.

  • It broke when you rolled it: you let the bottom set too long and too firm, so it lost the flexibility to fold. Or the pan is not as nonstick as it once was.

  • Filling discipline. Anything wet — tomatoes, mushrooms straight from the pan, raw spinach — will steam inside the roll and split it. Cook fillings first and drain them well, and use a spoonful, not a handful.

Keeping it

None whatsoever. This dish exists for about ninety seconds.
