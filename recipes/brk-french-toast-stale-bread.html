Breakfast & The Griddle

French Toast from Stale Bread

Day-old bread is not a compromise here, it is the requirement. Fresh bread turns to sludge in the custard.

8 slices, 4 servings
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
40 minutes
Start to finish

French toast is a bread pudding cooked flat, and it succeeds or fails on how much custard the bread will take up without collapsing. Fresh bread is already saturated with its own moisture, so it has nowhere to put the custard and disintegrates in the dish. Stale bread has lost water and its starch has partly recrystallised into a firmer structure, so it drinks the custard deep and holds together in the pan. Cut it thick, soak it longer than feels reasonable, and cook it slower than you would cook a pancake so the middle sets before the outside burns.

What you need

Custard

  • 4 large eggs
  • 300 ml whole milk or half and half
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 1/4 teaspoon grated nutmeg
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 tablespoon bourbon or dark rum, optional

Bread

  • 8 slices day-old bread, 2.5 cm thick — sourdough, challah or brioche

Cooking and serving

  • 3 tablespoons butter, plus more as needed
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • Maple syrup and butter, to taste
  • 1 tablespoon powdered sugar, optional

How

  1. If your bread is only a day old and still soft, dry it out first: lay the slices on a rack in a 250°F (120°C) oven until the surfaces feel dry and papery and the edges have firmed, about fifteen minutes. It should not colour. This step replaces two days of sitting on the counter.

  2. Whisk the custard ingredients together until completely uniform, with no strands of unmixed white left. Unmixed white is what shows up as those pale rubbery ribbons on the surface of bad French toast. If you are unsure, pour it through a strainer.

  3. Soak the slices in a wide dish, about a minute a side for a firm sourdough and more like thirty seconds for a rich brioche. The bread should feel heavy and completely saturated when lifted, with no dry white core visible if you press the edge. Under-soaking gives you eggy toast; over-soaking gives you a slice that will not survive the spatula.

  4. Heat a heavy skillet or griddle over medium — around 325°F (165°C), lower than you would use for pancakes. This is a thick, wet slice with a raw egg mixture in the middle, and it needs time. Add butter and a little oil, which raises the smoke point and stops the butter solids scorching.

  5. Lay the slices down and cook without moving them until the underside is a deep even gold and the edges look set rather than wet, four to five minutes. Slow browning gives an even crust; a hot pan gives you dark spots and a cold middle.

  6. Flip and cook the second side the same way. The custard in the centre needs to set: it is done when the middle feels springy and firm rather than squishy under a fingertip, and an instant-read thermometer pushed into the centre of a slice reads 160°F (71°C), the temperature at which an egg custard is both set and safe.

  7. Wipe the pan and use fresh butter between batches. Burnt milk solids from the first round make the second round taste scorched.

  8. Hold finished slices on a rack in a 200°F (93°C) oven rather than stacked on a plate. Serve with butter, syrup, and powdered sugar if anybody is in that mood.

Notes

  • Baked version for a crowd: soak the slices, lay them in a buttered dish, pour over any remaining custard, and bake at 350°F (175°C) until puffed and set and the centre reads 160°F (71°C). No standing at the pan, slightly less crust.

  • The bread is the recipe. Enriched breads like challah and brioche give a soft, cake-like result; a firm sourdough gives more chew and a slight tang that keeps it from being cloying. Sandwich bread from a bag is too thin and too soft, and turns to paste.

  • Overnight option: soak the slices, lay them in a single layer on a tray, cover and hold cold overnight. Cook straight from the cold, adding a minute per side.

Keeping it

Cooked slices hold cold for three days and reheat in a 350°F (175°C) oven until hot through and crisp at the edges. They freeze for two months on a tray, then bagged, and go straight from freezer to toaster. Leftover custard containing raw egg must be kept cold and used within a day.
