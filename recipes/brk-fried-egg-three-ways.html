Breakfast & The Griddle

A Fried Egg, Three Ways

Crisp and lacy, gently basted, or over easy without breaking the yolk. Same egg, three different pans.

2 eggs per person
Makes
5 minutes
Hands on
5 minutes
Start to finish

A fried egg is three different recipes wearing the same name, and most disappointment at the counter comes from ordering one and getting another. Crisp-edged means hot fat and browned frilly whites with a runny yolk. Basted means moderate heat and spoonfuls of butter poured over the top until a film of white veils the yolk. Over easy means a flip, thirty seconds, and a yolk that still runs when you cut it. Here are all three, along with the actual differences: how hot the pan is, how much fat is in it, and whether you touch the egg at all once it goes in.

What you need

Every version

  • 2 large eggs, as fresh as you have
  • A pinch of flaky salt
  • Black pepper, to taste

Fat, pick one

  • 1 tablespoon (14 g) butter, for basted or over easy
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil or bacon fat, for crisp-edged

How

  1. Crack each egg into a small cup first. This is not fussiness — it lets you slide the egg in flat instead of dropping it from height, which is what spreads the white halfway across the pan and gives you those thin overcooked skirts nobody wanted.

  2. Crisp-edged. Heat oil or bacon fat in a skillet over medium-high until it shimmers and a drop of water skitters. Slide the eggs in. They should sizzle hard immediately. Tilt the pan and spoon the hot fat over the edges of the whites until they lift, blister and go lacy brown, and the white nearest the yolk has turned opaque. Yolk stays liquid. Lift out with a thin spatula.

  3. Basted. Melt butter over medium-low until it foams but does not colour. Slide the eggs in, then tip the pan toward you so the butter pools at the low edge. Spoon it over the whites and the top of the yolk, again and again, until the whites are set through and the yolk has taken on a pale film like a cataract. The yolk under that film is still soft.

  4. Over easy. Butter over medium. Slide the eggs in and leave them completely alone until the whites are opaque and the very edges are just set and matte, with only a small clear puddle left around the yolk. Slide the spatula fully under the whole egg, lift barely off the pan, and turn it over with a short flick of the wrist rather than a toss. Thirty seconds on the second side. Slide it back out yolk-side up.

  5. Salt after cooking, not before. Salt sitting on a raw white draws water out and leaves pale spots that look like the pan was dirty.

  6. Serve on something warm. A fried egg on a cold plate is lukewarm within a minute, and a lukewarm yolk is a texture almost nobody enjoys.

Notes

  • Why the yolk broke on the flip: you lifted with the spatula only halfway under, so the egg hinged over the edge of the metal and split. Get the whole egg on the spatula before you commit.

  • Old eggs spread. The thick white around the yolk thins as an egg ages, so an older egg goes flat and wide in the pan. It tastes the same. It just will not look like the picture.

  • The white next to the yolk is the last part to cook. If you are serving someone who dislikes any clear or slippery egg white, basted is the version you want, because you are pouring hot fat directly onto that spot.

Keeping it

None. A fried egg is a thing you make while the toast is in the toaster and eat standing up if you have to.
