Breakfast & The Griddle

Hash Browns That Actually Crisp

Rinse the starch, wring them dry, and then leave the pan alone. Most hash browns fail from being stirred.

4 servings
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
35 minutes
Start to finish

Grey, soggy, steamed hash browns are the standard, and there are only three reasons for it. Surface starch, which turns to glue and holds water against the potato. Water itself, which has to boil off before anything browns, so the potato steams in its own moisture the whole time. And stirring, which breaks every crust the moment it forms. Rinse, wring, and then have the discipline to leave the pan alone for four full minutes. Russets, because their low moisture and high starch is exactly what you want here — waxy potatoes stay dense and never go crisp.

What you need

Potatoes

  • 900 g russet potatoes, about 3 large
  • 1 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

Fat

  • 3 tablespoons clarified butter, bacon fat or a neutral oil
  • 1 tablespoon butter, added at the end

How

  1. Peel the potatoes if you like — the skins are fine to leave on but they brown faster than the flesh and go bitter. Grate them on the large holes of a box grater straight into a bowl of cold water.

  2. Swirl the potatoes in the water with your hand. It will go cloudy white within seconds. Drain, refill, and repeat until the water stays nearly clear, usually two or three changes. That cloud is free surface starch, and it is what glues shreds together into a grey paste.

  3. Now wring them out. Pile the potatoes into the middle of a clean kitchen towel, gather the corners, and twist hard over the sink. Keep twisting past the point where it feels productive. You want them to feel dry and springy, like damp confetti rather than wet noodles. Every millilitre left in has to boil off in the pan before browning starts.

  4. Toss the shreds with the salt and pepper. Salting earlier would have pulled out more water while you worked, which sounds helpful but leaves you wringing a second time.

  5. Heat the fat in a heavy skillet, cast iron or carbon steel, over medium-high until it shimmers and a single shred dropped in sizzles hard immediately. A quiet pan means the potatoes will sit there and absorb the fat instead of frying in it.

  6. Spread the potatoes into an even layer no more than 1.5 cm deep and press once with a spatula. Then leave it. No stirring, no peeking for the first four minutes. Lift one edge with a spatula and look: you want a solid deep golden crust, not pale patches.

  7. When the underside is uniformly browned and the cake lifts as one piece, flip it — in halves if the whole thing is too unwieldy — add the last tablespoon of butter around the edge, and cook the second side until it is equally browned and the middle of the cake feels tender when a knife goes in without resistance.

  8. Slide onto a rack, not paper, for the minute before serving. Paper traps steam against the crust you just spent twenty minutes building.

Notes

  • They went grey while you worked: that is oxidation, harmless, and the cold water bath prevents it. It does not affect the finished colour once they brown.

  • They fell apart on the flip: the crust was not fully set. A properly browned potato cake holds together on its own starch and will lift as a single disc. If it tears, put it back for another minute.

  • For a crowd, cook them in batches and hold the finished cakes on a rack in a 200°F (93°C) oven. Stacked hash browns steam each other soft within five minutes.

Keeping it

Best in the first ten minutes and honestly not much good after. Grated, rinsed and wrung potatoes hold cold in a sealed container under a damp towel for a day. Cooked hash browns reheat only in a hot dry pan or a hot oven; a microwave turns them to mush.
