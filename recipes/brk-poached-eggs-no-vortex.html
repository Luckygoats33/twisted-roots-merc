Breakfast & The Griddle

Poached Eggs Without the Vortex

Skip the swirling whirlpool. Strain the egg, use a wide pan of barely moving water, and it works every time.

4 eggs
Makes
12 minutes
Hands on
15 minutes
Start to finish

The swirling whirlpool trick is a workaround for a problem you should just remove. Every egg has two whites: a thick one that clings to the yolk and a thin, watery one that has broken down with age. The thin white is what turns into the ragged grey cobweb in the water. Tip the egg into a slotted spoon or a fine strainer for a few seconds and that thin white drains straight through, and then the egg holds its shape in still water with no spinning, no vinegar theatrics, and no need to poach them one nervous egg at a time.

What you need

Eggs and water

  • 4 large eggs, the freshest you have
  • 2 litres water
  • 1 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 tablespoon distilled white vinegar (optional, see notes)

To serve

  • 4 slices sourdough, toasted
  • A pinch of flaky salt and black pepper
  • 2 teaspoons butter for the toast

How

  1. Fill a wide, shallow pan — a straight-sided saute pan or a deep skillet, not a stockpot — with about 5 cm of water. Add the salt, and the vinegar if you are using it. Depth matters more than volume: the egg needs to sink clear of the bottom and still have room above it.

  2. Bring the water up and then back down until it sits at a bare tremble, with a thin thread of steam and only the occasional bubble releasing from the bottom. If it is rolling, it will tear the whites apart. Boiling water is the single most common reason poached eggs come out shredded.

  3. Crack one egg into a slotted spoon or a fine mesh strainer held over the sink. Give it five seconds. The loose, watery white runs off and leaves you with a compact egg. Tip it into a small cup. Repeat for each egg.

  4. Lower each cup to the surface and let the egg roll out gently. Space them apart. Four eggs in a wide pan is comfortable; six is crowded and they weld to each other.

  5. Leave them alone. Do not stir, do not poke. After about three minutes, lift one out on a slotted spoon and press the top of the white with a fingertip. It should feel set and slightly springy, with the yolk underneath still soft and yielding. Set is what you are looking for, not a clock.

  6. If a white still slips loose and jellyish under your finger, put it back for another thirty to forty-five seconds and test again. For a firm yolk, go to five minutes total.

  7. Lift each egg onto a folded kitchen towel or a plate lined with paper for a few seconds. This is not optional if there is toast involved — an unlanded poached egg carries a tablespoon of water with it and turns bread to mush.

  8. Butter the toast, set the eggs on top, and season at the table. A poached egg gets almost no seasoning during cooking, so it needs more salt at the end than you would think.

Notes

  • Vinegar speeds up how fast the outer white sets, which helps with older eggs. It also faintly flavours them and leaves the whites a little tough and chalky. If your eggs are fresh and you strain them, leave it out.

  • Make-ahead for a crowd: poach the eggs a few minutes short, drop them straight into cold water, and hold them cold. To serve, slide them into hot water off the heat for about a minute, until they are warmed through and the white no longer feels cool at the centre. This is exactly how restaurants get twelve of them on the pass at once.

  • Fresh eggs are not a preference here, they are the recipe. The older the egg, the more thin white there is to lose, and past a couple of weeks even straining will not save the shape.

Keeping it

Poached eggs hold cold in water for up to two days and reheat in hot water in a minute. They do not freeze.
