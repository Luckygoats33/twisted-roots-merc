Breakfast & The Griddle

Sausage Gravy

A blond roux, real drippings and enough black pepper to notice. Gravy that tastes of flour is gravy that was rushed.

About 1.2 litres, enough for 8 split biscuits
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
25 minutes
Start to finish

Bad sausage gravy has one flavour and one texture problem, and both come from the same place. The flavour problem is raw flour — a pasty, chalky background note that comes from dumping flour into fat and adding milk before the starch has had time to cook. The texture problem is gluey thickness, from too much flour compensating for a roux that was never given the chance to work. A roux is equal weights of fat and flour, cooked until it smells like toasted crackers rather than raw dough, and for a white gravy that takes about two minutes and no colour past pale straw. Everything else here is seasoning.

What you need

Gravy

  • 450 g bulk breakfast sausage
  • 50 g all-purpose flour
  • 50 g fat total — sausage drippings topped up with butter
  • 900 ml whole milk, warmed
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons coarse black pepper, or more
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt, to taste
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground sage
  • A pinch of cayenne
  • A pinch of freshly grated nutmeg

To serve

  • 8 hot split biscuits
  • 1 tablespoon sliced green onion, optional

How

  1. Break the sausage into a heavy skillet over medium heat and leave it alone for a couple of minutes before you start stirring. You want browned crust on some of it, not uniformly grey crumbles — the brown is most of the flavour of the finished gravy.

  2. Break it up and keep cooking until no pink remains anywhere and the pieces are firm. Ground meat needs an internal temperature of 160°F (71°C); check the thickest crumble with an instant-read thermometer if you are not certain.

  3. Tip the pan and look at the fat. You want 50 g. Spoon out any excess or add butter to make up the difference. Guessing this is why gravies come out greasy or gluey.

  4. Sprinkle the flour over the meat and fat and stir constantly. For the first minute it will look like wet sand clinging to the sausage. Keep going until it smells like toasted crackers and has gone from raw-white to the colour of pale straw, about two minutes. Do not let it brown — a brown roux tastes better and thickens worse, and this gravy needs the thickening.

  5. Pour in about a quarter of the warm milk and stir hard. It will seize into a thick paste. That is correct. Work out every lump against the bottom of the pan while it is still thick, because lumps are impossible to chase once it thins out.

  6. Add the rest of the milk in two more additions, stirring smooth each time. Bring it up to a bare simmer, with slow bubbles breaking at the surface, and hold it there for four or five minutes, stirring often, until it coats the back of a spoon and a finger drawn across leaves a clean track that does not immediately close.

  7. Season now, at the end. Pepper first and generously — this is a peppery gravy or it is nothing — then salt to taste, sage, cayenne and nutmeg. Sausage varies wildly in salt, which is why you wait.

  8. If it has gone too thick, loosen it with warm milk a splash at a time. If it is too thin, hold it at a simmer a few minutes longer rather than adding more flour. Serve it over split hot biscuits immediately, since it thickens further as it sits.

Notes

  • It tastes of flour: the roux was not cooked out. Next time give it the full two minutes and trust the smell — raw dough versus toasted cracker is an obvious difference once you have noticed it.

  • It is gluey: too much flour for the volume of milk. The working ratio is about 50 g flour and 50 g fat per litre of milk for a gravy that pours; more flour gives you wallpaper paste.

  • Warm the milk. Cold milk into a hot roux drops the pan temperature and makes lumps far more likely. It also takes twice as long to come up to a simmer.

Keeping it

Holds cold for four days and thickens into a solid block; reheat gently with a splash of milk, stirring, until it pours again. It does not freeze well — the starch and dairy separate on thawing and the texture goes grainy. Scale it up freely for a crowd; it is one of the few things that behaves the same at four times the volume.
