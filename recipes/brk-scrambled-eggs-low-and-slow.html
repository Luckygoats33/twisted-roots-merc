Breakfast & The Griddle

Scrambled Eggs, Low and Slow

Soft curds and no puddle in the plate. The whole trick is a pan cooler than you think it should be.

2 plates, or 3 if there is toast
Makes
10 minutes
Hands on
12 minutes
Start to finish

Most scrambled eggs are ruined in the first thirty seconds, by a pan that was already too hot when the eggs hit it. Egg protein sets, then keeps contracting, and squeezes out the water it was holding. That is where the grey pebbles and the puddle underneath come from. Nothing you do afterwards fixes it. The repair is not a trick, it is patience: low heat, a spatula that never stops moving, and taking the pan off the burner while the eggs still look slightly underdone, because they keep cooking on the way to the table. Ten minutes. It is the cheapest thing on any menu and the fastest way to tell whether a kitchen is paying attention.

What you need

Eggs

  • 6 large eggs
  • 1 tablespoon (14 g) unsalted butter
  • 1 tablespoon whole milk, half and half or water (optional)
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • Black pepper, to taste

To finish, if you want it

  • 1 tablespoon chopped chives or green onion tops
  • 30 g sharp cheddar, grated

How

  1. Crack the eggs into a bowl and beat them with a fork until the colour is completely uniform and no ropy strands of white are left hanging off the tines. Forty strokes is about right. Any streak of unmixed white shows up later as a chewy pale patch.

  2. Salt the eggs now, not at the end. Salt breaks up some of the protein bonds before they ever set, which gives you a softer curd and, as a bonus, keeps the eggs from weeping. Beat in the tablespoon of milk if you are using it. It buys you a little margin for error, nothing more.

  3. Set a nonstick or a well-seasoned skillet over medium-low and drop in the butter. Wait until it has stopped foaming and smells faintly nutty. If it browns or the foam disappears with a hiss, the pan is too hot — pull it off, wipe it out, and start the butter again.

  4. Pour the eggs in. Count to ten. You want a thin skin to just begin setting on the bottom, not a sheet of cooked egg. If you hear any sizzle at all, lift the pan off the burner for twenty seconds and put it back.

  5. Now push. Drag a silicone spatula slowly across the bottom of the pan, folding the set egg over on itself and letting the loose egg run underneath. Keep the spatula moving the entire time. Small curds mean you stirred often, large soft folds mean you stirred slowly — both are correct, pick one and be consistent.

  6. Somewhere around the four minute mark the eggs will go from liquid to glossy, thickened, and still visibly wet in the seams between the curds. That is the moment. If you wait until they look like what you want on the plate, they are already overdone.

  7. Take the pan off the heat and keep stirring until the curds tighten very slightly and no longer slump flat in the pan, about twenty seconds. Residual heat in the metal finishes the job. Fold in the cheese and the chives here if you are using them, so the cheese softens rather than melts into grease.

  8. Get them onto warm plates immediately. Eggs held in a pan for two more minutes are a different and worse dish. Grind pepper over the top at the table.

Notes

  • If they seized anyway: the pan was hot. There is no recovery — scrambled eggs that have squeezed out water will not reabsorb it. Chop them, fold them into a tortilla with hot sauce, and call it a burrito.

  • Cast iron works but it holds heat like a brick, so pull it off the burner earlier and expect it to keep cooking harder off-heat than a thin pan does.

  • Doubling: up to a dozen eggs in a wide skillet is fine. Past that the layer gets too deep to move and the bottom sets before the top knows anything is happening. Use two pans.

Keeping it

Eat them now. Scrambled eggs do not hold, do not reheat, and do not freeze. The only honest make-ahead is to beat and salt the eggs the night before and keep them covered and cold; they will scramble a little more tenderly the next morning for having sat in the salt.
