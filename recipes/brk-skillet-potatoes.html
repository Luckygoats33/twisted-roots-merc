Breakfast & The Griddle

Skillet Potatoes with a Roughed-Up Crust

Boil them with a little baking soda, shake the pan hard, then fry. The surface does the rest.

6 servings
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
45 minutes
Start to finish

The best breakfast potatoes are not cut smaller or fried longer, they are given more surface. Boiling potatoes in slightly alkaline water breaks down the pectin on the outside of each piece into a thin starchy slurry, and shaking them dry in the pot roughs that slurry up into a fuzzy coating. Every one of those ragged edges is somewhere for fat to work, and the result is a crust with actual thickness to it rather than a thin browned skin. The interior stays creamy because it was cooked in water, not fried into submission.

What you need

Potatoes

  • 1.4 kg Yukon gold potatoes, cut in 3 cm chunks
  • 2 litres water
  • 1 tablespoon fine salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda

Frying and seasoning

  • 5 tablespoons bacon fat, duck fat or olive oil
  • 1 large onion, cut in thick wedges
  • 1 red bell pepper, cut in 2 cm pieces
  • 4 cloves garlic, smashed
  • 2 teaspoons smoked paprika
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons coarse black pepper
  • Fine salt, to taste
  • 2 tablespoons chopped parsley or chives

How

  1. Put the potatoes in a pot with the water, salt and baking soda and bring to a boil. The baking soda is a quarter teaspoon per litre and no more; overdo it and the potatoes go soapy and disintegrate.

  2. Boil until a knife slides into a chunk easily but the pieces still hold their shape when you lift one out, about ten minutes from the boil. Test one. Under-boiled potatoes never get creamy inside and over-boiled ones collapse in the pan.

  3. Drain thoroughly and return the potatoes to the dry hot pot. Let them sit off the heat for a minute so the surface steam escapes, then put the lid on and shake the pot hard, four or five good jolts. Take the lid off: the outsides should look scuffed and pasty, almost mashed at the edges. That is the crust in raw form.

  4. Heat the fat in a large heavy skillet over medium-high until it shimmers. Add the potatoes in a single layer, cut faces down where you have a choice, and leave them without stirring until the undersides are deep golden and release cleanly from the pan, about six minutes.

  5. Turn them, add the onion and pepper around the edges, and keep going, turning every few minutes, until the potatoes are browned on most sides and the onion has gone soft and caught colour at the tips.

  6. Add the smashed garlic in the last two minutes, once the pan has calmed down slightly. Garlic added at the start is black and bitter by the end. Cook until it smells sweet and nutty.

  7. Off the heat, toss with the paprika, black pepper and salt to taste. Adding paprika over direct heat scorches it and turns it acrid, which is why it goes on at the end.

  8. Scatter with parsley and serve straight from the pan. Held in a covered dish they lose the crust within ten minutes; a rack in a low oven keeps them for twenty.

Notes

  • Sheet pan version for a crowd: after the shake, toss the potatoes in the fat and roast at 425°F (220°C), turning once, until the edges are dark and crisp and a chunk crushes easily under a spatula. Same result, no standing at the stove, about forty minutes.

  • Waxy versus starchy: Yukon golds are the middle ground and the safe pick. Russets crisp harder but break up. Red potatoes hold their shape and never really get creamy.

  • Do not crowd. Two batches in a skillet beats one pan piled two deep, which steams and comes out pale and greasy at the same time.

Keeping it

Boiled and shaken potatoes hold cold, uncovered, for two days and actually fry better after a night in the cold air, because the surface dries. Fried potatoes are best immediately, reheat acceptably in a hot skillet, and are hopeless from a microwave.
