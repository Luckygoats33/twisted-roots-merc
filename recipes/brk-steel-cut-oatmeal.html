Breakfast & The Griddle

Steel Cut Oatmeal Worth Eating

Toast the oats first, salt them properly, and stop stirring. Most oatmeal is bad because nobody bothered.

4 bowls
Makes
10 minutes
Hands on
35 minutes
Start to finish

Oatmeal has a reputation as penance, and mostly it earns it, because it is usually made by boiling oats in water without salt and stirring them into paste. Three changes fix it. Toast the dry oats in butter until they smell like biscuits, which builds a nutty flavour that water alone will never produce. Salt at the start so the seasoning gets into the grain rather than sitting on top. And leave it alone while it simmers — stirring beats starch out of the oats and turns the pot gluey. Steel cut oats stay in distinct chewy pieces, which is the entire reason to use them.

What you need

Oatmeal

  • 200 g steel cut oats
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 750 ml water
  • 250 ml whole milk
  • 1 teaspoon fine salt

Finishing, per bowl

  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar, maple syrup or honey
  • 2 tablespoons cream or milk
  • A pinch of flaky salt
  • 60 g berries, or a chopped apple cooked in butter
  • 1 tablespoon toasted walnuts or hazelnuts

How

  1. Melt the butter in a heavy saucepan over medium heat and add the dry oats. Stir them in the butter until they darken by a shade and smell distinctly toasty and biscuit-like, three or four minutes. Stop before they smell scorched; there is a short window between nutty and burnt.

  2. Pour in the water and milk. It will hiss and steam hard. Add the salt now — a full teaspoon, which is more than most people use and about right. Salt added at the end sits on the surface and tastes of salt, rather than seasoning the grain.

  3. Bring to a boil, then drop the heat until the surface is just moving with slow bubbles. A hard boil scorches the milk on the bottom of the pan and you will taste it in every bowl.

  4. Simmer uncovered for about twenty-five minutes, stirring only two or three times in total, scraping the bottom each time. Undisturbed oats stay separate and chewy; constantly stirred oats give up their starch and turn to wallpaper paste.

  5. It is ready when the grains are tender all the way through with a little chew left at the centre, and the liquid around them has thickened into something creamy that just holds a spoon track for a second before closing. If it is still soupy, keep going; if it has gone stiff, add a splash of milk.

  6. Take it off the heat and let it stand five minutes with the lid on. It thickens noticeably in that time, so pull it slightly looser than you want it.

  7. Serve in warm bowls with sweetener, cream and a pinch of flaky salt on top. Salt on top of a sweetened bowl is what makes it taste like food rather than like an obligation.

  8. For fruit, cook a chopped apple in butter with a pinch of cinnamon until soft and browned at the edges before spooning it over, or use raw berries, which need nothing.

Notes

  • Overnight method: bring the oats, liquid and salt to a boil at night, cover, kill the heat, and leave the pot on the stove. In the morning warm it through with a splash of milk. About five minutes of work at six in the morning.

  • Rolled oats are a different recipe — they cook in five minutes and go soft rather than chewy. They are not worse, but do not use these ratios or timings for them.

  • Scaling for a crowd: this multiplies cleanly up to about four times in a wide heavy pot. Beyond that the depth means the bottom scorches before the top is cooked; use a low oven at 325°F (165°C) in a covered dish instead, stirring once, until the grains are tender.

Keeping it

This is one of the rare breakfasts that is genuinely better made ahead. Cook a full batch, hold it cold in a flat container for five days, and cut portions off the block. Reheat with a good splash of milk over low heat or in a microwave, stirring, until loose and steaming. It also freezes in portions for three months.
