Breakfast & The Griddle

The Siletz

Egg, bacon, sharp cheddar and house sauce on a biscuit. The most ordered thing in the building.

6 sandwiches
Makes
45 minutes
Hands on
1 hour 15 minutes
Start to finish

This is the sandwich people order without looking at the board, and the reasons it works are boring and structural. The egg is cooked flat in a ring so it sits inside the footprint of the biscuit instead of hanging off the sides. The bacon is baked, not fried, so it lies straight and does not pull out of the sandwich in one piece and take everything with it. The cheese goes on the hot egg so it melts and glues the stack together. And the sauce is on the top half only, so the bottom biscuit stays structural. Cold biscuits reheat badly, so build these to order.

What you need

Biscuits

  • 360 g all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon baking powder
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons fine salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 170 g cold unsalted butter, cut in 1 cm cubes
  • 300 ml cold buttermilk, plus a little for brushing

House sauce

  • 120 g thick mayonnaise-style dressing
  • 2 tablespoons ketchup
  • 1 tablespoon whole grain mustard
  • 2 teaspoons pickle brine
  • 1/2 teaspoon smoked paprika
  • A pinch of cayenne

The stack

  • 12 slices thick-cut bacon
  • 6 large eggs
  • 6 slices sharp cheddar, about 25 g each
  • 1 tablespoon butter for the griddle
  • Salt and black pepper, to taste

How

  1. Heat the oven to 450°F (230°C). Whisk the flour, baking powder, salt and baking soda together in a wide bowl.

  2. Add the cold butter and flatten each cube between your fingers into a rough sheet. You are after flat flakes of butter the size of a thumbnail, not sandy crumbs. The flakes turn to steam in the oven and that steam is what gives you the layers you pull apart.

  3. Pour in the buttermilk and stir with a fork just until no dry flour remains. The dough will look shaggy and unpromising. Overworking here is the reason biscuits come out dense and cakey.

  4. Tip onto a floured counter, pat into a rectangle about 2 cm thick, fold it in thirds like a letter, turn ninety degrees, and repeat twice more. Pat out to 2.5 cm and cut six rounds with a sharp cutter, pressing straight down without twisting — twisting seals the edge and the biscuit rises lopsided.

  5. Set them on a tray with sides just touching, brush the tops with buttermilk, and bake until they are risen tall and deep golden on top, and the sides look dry and set rather than pale and damp, about fifteen minutes. Cool on a rack so the bottoms do not steam.

  6. While the biscuits bake, lay the bacon flat on a rimmed tray and bake it in the same oven until the fat has rendered out and gone translucent and the meat is stiff enough to hold its shape when lifted with tongs. It crisps further as it cools. Drain on paper and cut each slice in half.

  7. Stir all the house sauce ingredients together. Taste it. It should be sharper than you expect on its own, because it is fighting a biscuit, bacon and cheese.

  8. Cook the eggs flat. Butter a griddle or wide skillet over medium, set metal rings or wide-mouth cutters on it, and crack one egg into each. Break the yolk with a fork and spread it. Cook until the white is opaque and the surface is no longer wet, then lift the ring, flip and give it fifteen seconds more. Season as they come off. Lay a slice of cheddar on each hot egg so it slumps.

  9. Split the biscuits with a serrated knife, sauce the top halves only, and build: bottom biscuit, egg with its melted cheese, four half-slices of bacon crossed so every bite gets some, top biscuit. Press down once, firmly, and hand it over.

Notes

  • The ring matters more than it sounds. A free-form fried egg spreads to twice the diameter of the biscuit, and everything past the edge either hangs out or gets folded into a rubbery wad. A 9 cm ring, a tuna tin with both ends removed, or an egg cooked in a square and cut down all work.

  • Sauce on the top half only. Sauce on the bottom soaks straight through and the biscuit fails halfway through the sandwich.

  • For a crew, cook the eggs on a full griddle in one pass, keep the biscuits warm in a low oven, and build them all at the last minute in a line. Assembling early is how you get a soggy sandwich in twenty minutes.

Keeping it

Biscuits are best within four hours of the oven and are noticeably worse the next day; freeze the shaped, uncut dough rounds instead and bake them from frozen with about four minutes added. Bacon holds cold for four days. Assembled sandwiches do not keep — the bread goes soft and the egg goes tough.
