Breakfast & The Griddle

Yeasted Overnight Waffles

Mix the batter before bed. Crisp shell, open interior, a flavour baking powder cannot produce.

8 waffles, about 18 cm
Makes
15 minutes
Hands on
12 hours, almost all of it unattended
Start to finish

A baking powder waffle is a pancake in a grid. A yeasted waffle is a different food: the overnight ferment produces alcohol and acid that bake off into a smell somewhere between bread and beer, breaks down some of the starch so the inside stays custardy and open, and leaves a shell that stays crisp for several minutes instead of going limp on the way to the table. It takes ten minutes of work the night before and none in the morning, which is the actual selling point. The batter also improves for a second day in the cold.

What you need

The night before

  • 375 ml whole milk, warmed to just above body temperature
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons instant yeast
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 250 g all-purpose flour
  • 115 g butter, melted
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons fine salt

In the morning

  • 2 large eggs
  • 1/4 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil for the iron, if it needs it

To serve

  • Butter and maple syrup, to taste
  • 150 g berries, optional

How

  1. Warm the milk to just above body temperature — comfortably warm on the inside of your wrist, not hot. Stir in the yeast and sugar and let it sit five minutes, until it smells faintly of bread and the surface has clouded. If nothing happens, the yeast is dead and there is no point continuing.

  2. Whisk in the flour, then the melted butter and salt, until smooth. It will be a loose batter, closer to heavy cream than to pancake batter.

  3. Cover the bowl loosely and leave it on the counter overnight, eight to twelve hours in a normal cool kitchen. Use a bowl at least twice the volume of the batter. It rises considerably and then falls back, and finding it on the floor is a poor start to a morning.

  4. In the morning the batter should be domed or freshly collapsed, covered in bubbles, and smell yeasty and faintly sour. If your kitchen is cold and it looks sluggish, give it an hour somewhere warmer.

  5. Whisk in the eggs one at a time, then sprinkle over the baking soda and whisk again. The batter will foam up and lighten within seconds; the soda neutralises some of the overnight acid and gives a final push of lift in the iron.

  6. Heat the waffle iron fully. It is hot enough when a drop of water on the plate hisses and skips immediately. An iron that is not properly preheated is the main cause of a pale, soft waffle that sticks.

  7. Pour enough batter to cover about two-thirds of the plate; it spreads. Cook until the steam coming out of the seam has slowed to almost nothing and the waffle is deep golden brown and lifts away from the plates without resistance. The steam is the better signal — it means the surface water has gone, which is the same thing as saying the crust has formed.

  8. Move finished waffles straight onto a rack, never a plate. Trapped steam under a waffle destroys the crust in under a minute. If you are holding them, use a rack in a 200°F (93°C) oven and they stay crisp for twenty.

Notes

  • Overnight on the counter is safe here — this is a fermenting batter, actively acidifying, in the same category as bread dough. If your kitchen runs hot, use the cold overnight instead and add an hour on the counter in the morning.

  • Savoury version: leave out the sugar, fold grated sharp cheddar and black pepper into the finished batter, and serve with a fried egg on top.

  • The iron matters more than the recipe. A deep Belgian iron gives a thick shell and a very open interior; a thin classic iron gives a crisper, denser waffle. Both work, the timing does not transfer.

Keeping it

The batter keeps cold for three days and gets more sour and more interesting. Cooked waffles freeze extremely well: cool completely on a rack, freeze flat, then bag them and reheat straight from frozen in a toaster until crisp and hot through. They are better from the toaster than from a microwave by a wide margin.
