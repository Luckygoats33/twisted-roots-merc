Fish & Shellfish

Fish and Chips

Two fryings for the chips, one for the fish, and a batter mixed at the last possible second.

Serves 4
Makes
50 minutes
Hands on
1 hour 30 minutes
Start to finish

The chips are the harder half and nobody admits it. Potatoes have to be cooked through at a low temperature and then blistered at a high one, with a rest in between, or you get grease-logged sticks that were raw in the middle ten seconds before they burned. The fish is comparatively easy: dry it, dust it, batter it, fry it once.

Everything here depends on oil temperature holding steady, which means a thermometer and small batches. A pot of oil that drops when the food goes in is the reason home frying gets a bad name.

What you need

Chips

  • 2.5 lb (1.1 kg) russet potatoes, cut in 1/2 inch batons
  • 2 tbsp white vinegar for the soaking water
  • 8 cups water for soaking
  • 2 tsp fine sea salt for finishing

Fish

  • 4 pieces rockfish, lingcod or true cod, 6 oz (170 g) each, skin off
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour for dusting
  • 1 tsp fine sea salt

Batter

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1/2 cup rice flour or cornstarch
  • 1 tsp baking powder
  • 1/2 tsp fine sea salt
  • 1.5 cups beer, very cold, or cold sparkling water

Frying

  • 8 cups neutral frying oil, or enough for 3 inches in the pot
  • 1 malt vinegar bottle for the table

How

  1. Soak the cut potatoes in cold water with the vinegar for 30 minutes, then drain and dry them completely on towels. The soak rinses off surface starch; the drying keeps the oil from erupting.

  2. Heat the oil to 325°F (165°C). Blanch the chips in batches until they are floppy, pale and cooked through, giving no resistance when you press one against the side of the pot. They should take on no colour at all at this stage.

  3. Lift them onto a rack and let them cool at least 20 minutes. This rest lets moisture migrate outward and is why the second fry crisps instead of steaming. You can hold them here for hours.

  4. Bring the oil up to 375°F (190°C). Dry the fish hard, salt it, and dust it in the plain flour, shaking off the excess. Batter will not stick to a wet fillet and will slide off in the pot in one sad sheet.

  5. Now mix the batter, and not before. Whisk the dry ingredients, pour in the cold beer, and stir just until it comes together with lumps still visible. The consistency should coat a spoon like thin pancake batter. Overmixing builds gluten and gluten makes armour, not crust.

  6. Dip one fillet, let the excess run off for a second, and lower it into the oil tail first, holding for a moment before you let go so it sets rather than sinking to the bottom.

  7. Fry two pieces at a time until the batter is deep golden and rigid, the bubbling has slowed noticeably, and the fish inside reaches an internal temperature of 145 degrees Fahrenheit (63°C) with flesh that flakes cleanly. Expect five or six minutes for a thick piece.

  8. Rest the fish on a rack in a low oven while you finish the chips. Return the chips to the 375°F (190°C) oil in batches until they are blistered, deep gold and audibly hard when they knock against the spider.

  9. Salt the chips the instant they come out, while the surface is still greasy enough to hold it. Salt applied a minute later just falls off.

  10. What goes wrong: crowding the pot. Every piece you add drops the oil temperature, and oil below about 325°F (165°C) soaks in instead of sealing. Fry less at a time than feels efficient.

Notes

  • Rockfish, lingcod and true cod all work. Halibut is a waste of money in batter and dries out faster than any of them.
  • Beer contributes carbon dioxide and a little alcohol, which flashes off fast and helps drying. Cold sparkling water does most of the same job if you would rather not use beer.

Keeping it

Neither half keeps. Fried fish is a thing you eat standing up. Strain and save the oil once it has cooled, and it is good for several more rounds of fish before it starts smelling like the last thing you cooked.
