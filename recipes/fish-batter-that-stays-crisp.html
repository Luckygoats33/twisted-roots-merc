Fish & Shellfish

A Fry Batter That Stays Crisp

Why the first bite is always good and the fifth one usually is not.

Enough for about 2 lb (900 g) of fish
Makes
10 minutes
Hands on
20 minutes
Start to finish

Batter goes soft for one reason: steam. Water inside the fish turns to vapour, the vapour has to get out through the crust, and a dense crust traps it until the whole shell turns to wallpaper paste. Everything a good batter does is aimed at building a shell that is rigid, dry and porous enough to let that steam leave.

Three levers do most of the work. Cut the gluten with a starch that cannot form any. Keep the batter cold so it does not develop while it waits. Get carbon dioxide into it so the coating fries with holes in it. This page is the batter and the reasoning, ready for any fish you like.

What you need

Dry

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 3/4 cup rice flour
  • 1/4 cup cornstarch
  • 1.5 tsp baking powder
  • 1 tsp fine sea salt
  • 1/2 tsp onion powder

Wet

  • 1.5 cups sparkling water or lager, refrigerator cold
  • 2 tbsp vodka, optional, and it does help

For dusting and frying

  • 1/2 cup rice flour for the dredge
  • 8 cups neutral frying oil

How

  1. Understand the dredge first. A thin coat of plain rice flour on dry fish gives the batter something to grip. Skip it and the batter slides off in the oil, every time, and you spend the evening fishing shreds out with a spider.

  2. Whisk the dry ingredients together and chill the bowl. Chill the liquid too. Cold slows gluten development, and it widens the gap between batter temperature and oil temperature, which means the shell sets faster on contact.

  3. Heat the oil to 375°F (190°C) before you mix anything wet. The batter should wait for the oil, not the other way round.

  4. Pour the cold liquid into the dry and stir with chopsticks or a fork until it is just combined and still lumpy. Lumps are fine. Smooth batter is overworked batter, and it fries into a chewy skin.

  5. Check the consistency by lifting the fork. It should fall in a ribbon that sinks back in about two seconds. Too thick and it fries into bread; too thin and it fries into lace.

  6. Dredge the dry fish in rice flour, shake it off, dip, and lower it into the oil. Fry until the coating is rigid, pale gold rather than dark, and the bubbling around the piece has dropped from a furious boil to a lazy simmer. That change in sound is the water leaving; when it slows, the inside is cooked.

  7. Confirm with a probe. Fish is done at an internal temperature of 145 degrees Fahrenheit (63°C), opaque and flaking. Batter hides everything, so check the first piece of every batch until you know your timings.

  8. Drain on a rack over a tray, never on paper towel, and never stacked. Steam trapped under a piece of fish will undo the crust in under a minute.

  9. If you are holding a batch, keep it on the rack in an oven at 200°F (95°C) with the door cracked. That keeps drying the shell instead of steaming it.

  10. What goes wrong, in order of likelihood: wet fish, warm batter, cold oil, crowded pot, paper towel. Fix them in that order.

Notes

  • The vodka works because alcohol evaporates faster than water and inhibits gluten. Two tablespoons is enough to notice; more thins the batter without helping further.
  • For a gluten-free version, use all rice flour and cornstarch in place of the wheat flour. It is arguably crisper and slightly more fragile.

Keeping it

Mix the dry blend in quantity and keep it in a jar in the cupboard for months. Mixed batter is good for about twenty minutes and then it is a different, worse batter.
