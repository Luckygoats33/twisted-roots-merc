Fish & Shellfish

Canning Salmon, Pressure Canner Only

Fish is a low-acid food, and a boiling water bath cannot make it safe. Not ever.

Depends on your canner and jar size
Makes
1 hour of packing
Hands on
Most of a day
Start to finish

Home canned salmon is one of the genuinely great things you can do with a heavy season. It is also the single most dangerous recipe in this collection, so this page tells you how to pack the jars and then sends you somewhere else for the numbers, on purpose.

Fish is low acid. Water at a rolling boil tops out at 212 degrees Fahrenheit (100°C) at sea level and that is nowhere near hot enough to destroy Clostridium botulinum spores. Only a pressure canner, holding roughly 240°F (116°C) under pressure, does that. There is no workaround, no amount of lemon juice, and no old family method that changes this.

What you need

Fish

  • 10 lb (4.5 kg) fresh salmon, gutted, bled, chilled since it came out of the water
  • 1 tsp canning salt per pint jar, optional and for flavour only

Equipment

  • 1 dial or weighted gauge pressure canner, tested for accuracy
  • 12 half-pint or pint jars with new lids and sound rings
  • 1 jar lifter, 1 wide funnel, 1 bubble tool
  • 1 copy of the current USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning, or your OSU Extension office on the phone

How

  1. Before anything else, get the tested figures. Look up the current process for fish in the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning, or call your county Extension office, which in this state is OSU Extension. Processing time and pressure change with jar size and with your altitude, and this page is deliberately not printing a number for you to misapply.

  2. Altitude matters more than people expect. Siletz sits close to sea level, so the sea level figures apply here. If you are canning at elevation anywhere inland, your pressure has to go up, and the tested tables tell you by how much. Look up your own altitude and use the line that matches it.

  3. Start with fish that has been handled properly. Bled at the water, gutted quickly, on ice since. Canning does not rescue fish that sat warm in a tote all afternoon; it seals in whatever was already happening.

  4. Rinse the fillets, remove the fins and the tail, and cut crosswise into lengths that stand upright in your jars. Leave the skin and the bones in. Both soften completely under pressure and the bones are where a lot of the calcium is.

  5. Pack the pieces in vertically, skin against the glass, filling firmly without crushing. Fish gives up plenty of its own liquid, so add no water. Leave the headspace your tested recipe specifies, which for fish is generally more than you would guess.

  6. Add salt if you want it, run the bubble tool around the inside, wipe every rim with a clean damp cloth until it squeaks, and fit lids and rings finger tight. A speck of fish oil on a rim is a seal that fails in the cupboard three weeks later.

  7. Load the canner with the water your manufacturer specifies, lock it down, and vent steam in a steady column for the full ten minutes before you close the vent. Skipping the vent traps air, and trapped air means the inside of that canner is cooler than the gauge claims.

  8. Bring it to the pressure your tested recipe gives and start timing only when it is there and holding. Adjust the burner constantly to keep it steady. If the pressure drops below target at any point, you bring it back up and start the timing over from the beginning. That is the rule and it is not negotiable.

  9. When time is up, kill the heat and let the canner depressurise on its own. Do not run it under water, do not lift the weight to hurry it. Then wait the extra ten minutes, remove the lid away from your face, and lift jars straight up onto a towel.

  10. Leave them alone overnight. Check every seal the next day by pressing the centre of the lid; a sealed lid does not move or click. Anything unsealed goes in the fridge and gets eaten within a couple of days, not re-processed.

  11. Never taste-test a suspect jar. If a lid is bulging, if the contents spurt on opening, if it smells wrong, or if you have any doubt about whether the process was followed, dispose of it without tasting and follow the disposal instructions in the USDA guide. Botulinum toxin gives you no warning you can detect.

Notes

  • A dial gauge canner should be tested for accuracy once a season. Extension offices do this, often free. A gauge reading two pounds light is a whole batch of unsafe jars.
  • Half-pints are the useful size. A pint of canned salmon is more than most households get through before it wants using up.

Keeping it

Rings off, jars wiped, labelled without a date on the glass if you like, but write something on the lid so you know which batch is which. Cool dark cupboard, best quality within a year. Once open, fridge, and use in three or four days.
