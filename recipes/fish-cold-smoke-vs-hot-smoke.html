Fish & Shellfish

Cold Smoke and Hot Smoke, Explained

Two entirely different processes that share a word, and only one of them cooks anything.

One clear head about which one you want
Makes
30 minutes of reading and setting up
Hands on
Varies by method, from half a day to three days
Start to finish

People use the word smoked to mean two processes that have almost nothing in common. Hot smoking cooks the fish in smoke, somewhere above 150°F (66°C), and produces flaky, opaque, fully cooked fish that keeps in a fridge for about a week. Cold smoking flavours fish with smoke at a temperature deliberately too low to cook it, which means the salt cure is doing all the preservation work and the smoke is doing none of it.

This piece is the explanation, not a recipe. Read it before you build anything, because the failure mode of the second one is not a bad dinner.

What you need

For understanding hot smoking

  • 1 smoker capable of holding 175 to 225°F (80 to 107°C)
  • 1 reliable probe thermometer
  • 2 lb (900 g) fish, brined first

For understanding cold smoking

  • 1 smoke generator separated from the fish chamber
  • 1 way to keep the chamber under about 80 degrees Fahrenheit (27°C)
  • 1 tested cure recipe you did not invent
  • 1 thermometer for the chamber, not the fish

How

  1. Understand what smoke does. Smoke deposits phenols and acids on the surface of the fish. Those slow surface spoilage and taste good. They do not sterilise anything and they do not reach the middle of a fillet in any meaningful quantity.

  2. Hot smoking is cooking. You hold a chamber at 175 to 225°F (80 to 107°C) and take the fish to an internal temperature of 145 degrees Fahrenheit (63°C), at which point it is opaque, firm, and flakes apart under a fork. It is a cooked food and it is handled like one.

  3. Cold smoking is not cooking. The chamber is kept cool enough that the fish never sets, which puts the whole product in the temperature range bacteria like most, for hours. The only thing standing between you and a problem is a salt cure of the right strength, held for the right time, at refrigeration temperature.

  4. Because of that, do not improvise a cold smoke. Use a tested cure with a stated salt concentration and a stated time, and follow it exactly. The bacterium people worry about here is Clostridium botulinum, which is happy without oxygen, and it does not announce itself by taste or smell.

  5. Know what freezing does and does not do. Freezing at commercial parameters is used to address parasites in fish intended to be eaten raw or lightly cured. It does not kill bacteria, and it does not make an unsafe cure safe. If you want the current guidance, read what the FDA publishes on parasite destruction rather than taking anybody's word for it, including ours.

  6. Pick by what you actually want. If you want flaky, warm, smoky fish for a chowder or a sandwich, you want hot smoke, and it is forgiving. If you want silky translucent slices for a bagel, you want a cold smoke or a cure like gravlax, and it is not.

  7. Build the right equipment for the job. Hot smoking needs a firebox, a chamber and a probe. Cold smoking needs the smoke source physically separated from the fish, usually a tube or a pellet generator feeding a cool box, and it works badly on a warm day for reasons that should now be obvious.

  8. Decide who is eating it. Cold-smoked fish is a ready-to-eat product that gets no further heat step, and public health guidance has long steered pregnant, immunocompromised, elderly and very young people away from it. Hot-smoked fish taken to temperature carries no such caveat.

Notes

  • Local smokehouses exist for exactly this reason. The half pound of smoked salmon in our cooler was made by people with a licensed process and a temperature log.
  • If a recipe you find online for cold smoke does not state a salt percentage and a cure time, it is not a recipe. It is a photograph with words under it.

Keeping it

Hot-smoked fish: fridge about a week, freezer three months. Cold-smoked fish: follow the cure's own guidance, keep it cold, and treat it as a short-life product, not a shelf-stable one.
