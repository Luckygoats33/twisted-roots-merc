Fish & Shellfish

Gravlax, Cured Not Cooked

Salt, sugar, dill, weight and three days of doing nothing.

One cured side, about 2 lb (900 g)
Makes
20 minutes
Hands on
3 days
Start to finish

Gravlax is a salt and sugar cure that draws water out of a salmon fillet and firms it into something you can slice thin enough to read through. There is no heat at any point. The fish never reaches a cooked internal temperature of 145 degrees Fahrenheit (63°C), and it is not supposed to; the salt is doing the work that the oven usually does.

Which means the safety thinking is different. This is a raw ready-to-eat product. Start with fish you trust from a source you trust, keep it cold the whole way through, and read the note at the bottom before you decide who at the table is eating it.

What you need

Cure

  • 150 g coarse sea salt
  • 150 g granulated sugar
  • 2 tbsp coarsely cracked black peppercorns
  • 1 tbsp crushed coriander seed

Fish

  • 1 salmon fillet, skin on, about 2 lb (900 g), centre cut, pin bones out
  • 2 large bunches dill, stems and all, roughly chopped
  • 3 tbsp aquavit or gin, optional

Mustard sauce

  • 3 tbsp coarse mustard
  • 1 tbsp Dijon mustard, smooth
  • 2 tbsp sugar
  • 2 tbsp white wine vinegar
  • 6 tbsp neutral oil
  • 3 tbsp chopped dill

How

  1. Run your fingers along the fillet against the grain and pull every pin bone with pliers. Slicing thin later is miserable if you are working around bones you meant to remove and did not.

  2. Mix salt, sugar, pepper and coriander. Note the ratio is equal weights of salt and sugar, which is the classic setting; more salt firms it faster and makes it saltier, more sugar softens both.

  3. Lay a long sheet of plastic wrap in a dish. Spread a third of the cure on it, then a third of the dill, then the fillet skin down. Sprinkle the spirit over the flesh if you are using it, then pack the rest of the cure and dill over the top, thicker at the thick end.

  4. Wrap tightly, set the dish in the fridge, and put something heavy on top: a board with two cans on it, or a foil-wrapped brick. The weight presses liquid out and keeps the cure in contact with the flesh.

  5. Turn the parcel once a day. Liquid will pool in the dish; that is water leaving the fish and it means the cure is working. Do not pour it back.

  6. After three days the fillet should feel firm all the way to the middle when pressed with a finger, with no soft give at the thick end, and the colour should have gone deeper and more translucent. Thin fillets can be ready in two days, a thick king side may want four.

  7. Unwrap, scrape the cure off, and rinse briefly under cold water only if it tastes too salty at the edge. Pat dry and refrigerate uncovered an hour to firm the surface for slicing.

  8. Slice on a long shallow angle, almost parallel to the board, starting at the tail. Stop at the skin. A thin flexible knife and slow hands beat a sharp knife and hurry.

  9. For the sauce, whisk both mustards with sugar and vinegar, then dribble the oil in while whisking until it thickens like a loose mayonnaise, and stir in the dill.

  10. Safety, plainly: this is raw cured fish. Anyone pregnant, immunocompromised, elderly or very young should not eat it. If you want the current guidance on freezing fish intended to be eaten raw or lightly cured, read what the FDA publishes on the subject rather than relying on any recipe, this one included. Freezing addresses parasites; it does nothing about bacteria.

Notes

  • Skin on, always. It gives you something to slice against and it holds the fillet together under weight.
  • The cure is not reusable. Throw it out.

Keeping it

Wrapped tight in the fridge, about a week. Slices dry out fast, so cut only what you are putting on the plate. It freezes for a month but the texture goes slightly grainy on thawing.
