Fish & Shellfish

Salmon on the Grill Without Losing Half of It

Hot grate, clean grate, oiled grate, and the patience not to move it.

Serves 4 to 6
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
1 hour 30 minutes with the plank soak
Start to finish

Two ways to grill salmon, and the argument between them is about how much you trust your grate. Straight on the bars gives you smoke, char and grill marks, and it will tear a fillet apart if the grate is dirty or the fish is moved too soon. A soaked cedar plank gives up the char and gains a guarantee, because the fish never touches metal at all.

Both are here. Pick by how much you care about the fish surviving intact in front of company, which is a legitimate way to make a cooking decision.

What you need

Fish

  • 1 salmon side, about 2.5 lb (1.1 kg), skin on, pin bones out
  • 2 tsp fine sea salt
  • 1 tbsp brown sugar
  • 1 tsp smoked paprika
  • 2 tbsp neutral oil

For the plank version

  • 1 untreated cedar grilling plank
  • 4 cups cold water for soaking
  • 1 lemon, sliced thin

Glaze, optional

  • 3 tbsp local honey
  • 1 tbsp soy sauce
  • 1 tsp grated ginger

How

  1. If you are planking, submerge the cedar in cold water for at least an hour, weighted down with a can. A dry plank is kindling, and you will find that out at the worst moment.

  2. Mix salt, brown sugar and paprika and rub it over the flesh side. Let the side sit at room temperature 20 minutes while the grill comes up. The sugar helps colour; the salt firms the surface so it is less inclined to shred.

  3. Build a two-zone fire: coals banked on one side, nothing on the other, or half the burners lit. You want the hot side around 450°F (230°C) with the lid down and a genuinely cool side to retreat to.

  4. Scrape the grate clean while it is hot, then wipe it with a folded towel dipped in oil, held in tongs. Repeat twice. A clean, hot, oiled grate is the entire difference between grilled salmon and salmon scraped off with a putty knife.

  5. Straight on the grate: oil the fish, lay it skin down over the hot side, and close the lid. Do not touch it. The skin will release on its own when it has set, which takes several minutes. If it resists a gentle lift with a thin spatula, it is not ready.

  6. Slide the fillet to the cool side once the skin has crisped and darkened, then close the lid and let it coast. Brush with the honey glaze in the last few minutes only, because honey over direct heat turns to carbon quickly.

  7. On a plank: lay lemon slices on the wet plank, fish on top, plank over the hot side until the edges of the board smoke and darken, then move the whole thing to the cool side and close the lid. Keep a spray bottle handy in case a corner catches.

  8. Either way, go by the probe. Fully cooked is an internal temperature of 145 degrees Fahrenheit (63°C), with the flesh opaque and flaking cleanly. If you prefer a rosy middle, cooks commonly pull at 125 to 130 degrees Fahrenheit (52 to 54°C), which is below the FDA figure and should be skipped by anyone pregnant, immunocompromised, elderly or very young.

  9. Slide two spatulas under the fillet to move it, or leave the skin behind on purpose and lift the flesh off in pieces. Both are respectable. Rest 5 minutes before serving.

  10. What goes wrong: flipping. A side of salmon does not need turning over. Skin down the whole way, lid closed, is the method with the fewest ways to fail.

Notes

  • Planks are single use if they char through, and good for a second run if they only scorch. They are not worth cleaning and re-soaking more than once.
  • Skin-off fillets on a grate are for people who enjoy suffering. Buy skin on.

Keeping it

Fridge, covered, three to four days. Cold grilled salmon on toast with something sharp is arguably better than the first serving.
