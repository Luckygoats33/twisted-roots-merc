Fish & Shellfish

Hot-Smoked Salmon

A brine you can measure, a chamber you can hold, and a number you stop at.

About 2 lb (900 g) finished
Makes
40 minutes
Hands on
About 18 hours including brine and drying
Start to finish

Hot smoking is a cooking method with a brine in front of it, and both halves matter. The brine seasons through the fillet, firms the proteins so the fish does not fall apart, and sets up the pellicle that smoke sticks to. The smoke chamber then cooks it slowly enough that the outside does not seize before the middle arrives.

The measurements below are given as a percentage of the weight of water, because scaling a brine by cups of salt across different salt crystal sizes is how people end up with fish that is either bland or inedible. Weigh it.

What you need

Brine, 6 percent by weight

  • 2 litres cold water, weighed
  • 120 g fine sea salt, which is 6 percent of the water weight
  • 80 g brown sugar
  • 2 tbsp cracked black pepper
  • 4 bay leaves

Fish

  • 2.5 lb (1.1 kg) salmon fillet, skin on, cut into portions of even thickness

Smoke

  • 3 cups alder chips or pellets
  • 1 tbsp neutral oil for the rack

How

  1. Dissolve the salt and sugar completely in a litre of the water, whisking until the liquid runs clear rather than cloudy, then add the rest of the water cold along with the pepper and bay. Undissolved salt at the bottom of a bucket is salt that is not in your fish.

  2. Submerge the fish, weighted down with a plate, and refrigerate. Thin belly pieces need about 4 hours; portions an inch thick want 8 to 12. Over-brining is real, and it shows up as fish too salty to eat with anything.

  3. Rinse the fillets under cold water, pat dry, and set them skin down on an oiled rack. Refrigerate uncovered 4 to 12 hours, or sit them in front of a fan in a cool room for 2 to 3 hours.

  4. Wait for the pellicle. The surface should go from wet to dull and slightly tacky, like the back of an envelope. Smoke does not adhere to a wet fillet; it beads up and runs off, and you get pale fish that tastes of nothing much.

  5. Bring the smoker to 175°F (80°C) with alder going. Load the fish, leaving room around each piece for air to move, and close it up.

  6. Hold the chamber between 175 and 225°F (80 and 107°C). Lower and slower is better for texture, hotter is faster and drier. Expect two to four hours depending on thickness and how well your box holds heat on a wet coastal afternoon.

  7. Cook to an internal temperature of 145 degrees Fahrenheit (63°C) at the thickest point. At that number the flesh is opaque through, firm to a fingertip, and flakes into clean sheets. Do not run a hot smoke to a lower temperature; the whole point of hot smoke is that it is a cooked product.

  8. Watch for white albumin beading along the surface. A little is normal. A lot means the chamber is running hot and the fish is squeezing out water, and the finished fillet will be dry no matter what you do next.

  9. Cool the fish on the rack at room temperature for half an hour, then refrigerate uncovered until cold before wrapping. Wrapping warm fish traps steam and softens everything you just built.

Notes

  • Alder is the local wood and the traditional one for salmon here. Fruit woods are gentler, hickory and mesquite will bury the fish.
  • A 6 percent brine is a middle setting. Drop to 4 percent for a mild result you can eat in slabs, go to 8 percent for something closer to a preserved product that you use in small amounts.

Keeping it

Fridge, wrapped, about a week. Vacuum sealed and frozen, three months without much loss. It thaws well, which is more than can be said for most cooked fish.
