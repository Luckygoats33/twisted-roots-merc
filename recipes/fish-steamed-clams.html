Fish & Shellfish

Steamed Clams in Butter and Wine

Ten minutes, one pan, and the broth is the point.

Serves 4 as a starter, 2 as dinner
Makes
15 minutes
Hands on
1 hour with the purge
Start to finish

Steamed clams are a fifteen minute dinner with one hour of waiting in front of them, and the waiting is not optional. Clams from a bay or a beach carry sand, and a bowl of gritty clams is a bowl nobody finishes. An hour in cold salted water gives them time to pump the sand out on their own.

After that it is a hot pan, some aromatics, a splash of wine, a lid, and enough restraint to pull the clams out as they open rather than waiting for the last stubborn one. The broth left behind is better than the clams and there should be bread ready for it.

What you need

Clams

  • 3 lb (1.4 kg) manila or littleneck clams
  • 8 cups cold water for purging
  • 3 tbsp salt for the purge water

Pan

  • 3 tbsp butter
  • 2 tbsp olive oil
  • 4 cloves garlic, sliced thin
  • 1 large shallot, minced
  • 1 pinch chile flakes
  • 1 cup dry white wine
  • 3 tbsp cold butter, cubed, for finishing
  • 1/3 cup chopped parsley
  • 1 lemon, halved

To serve

  • 1 loaf crusty bread, thickly sliced and toasted

How

  1. Dissolve the salt in the cold water and let the clams sit in it, in a bowl in the fridge, for an hour. Do not use fresh water alone; it kills them and they stop purging. Lift the clams out of the water rather than pouring, so the sand stays at the bottom.

  2. Scrub the shells and sort them. Any clam with a cracked shell goes in the bin. Any clam gaping open should close when you tap it firmly; if it does not, it is dead and it goes too.

  3. Heat the butter and oil in a wide pan with a lid over medium. Cook the garlic, shallot and chile until the garlic is fragrant and just turning pale gold at the edges, which takes about a minute and turns bitter in two.

  4. Pour in the wine, raise the heat, and let it bubble hard for a minute so the raw alcohol cooks off and the flavour concentrates.

  5. Add the clams, put the lid on, and shake the pan once. Steam over high heat, lifting the lid every minute or so, and remove clams to a bowl with tongs as they gape wide open. They do not open at the same rate and the early ones turn to erasers waiting for the late ones.

  6. When the great majority have opened, take the pan off the heat. Discard any clam that stayed shut after everything else opened.

  7. Let the broth settle for a moment, then pour it off carefully, leaving the last spoonful with any grit behind. Return it to the pan, bring it to a simmer, and swirl in the cold butter a cube at a time until the broth turns glossy and slightly thickened.

  8. Return the clams, add parsley and a squeeze of lemon, and serve straight from the pan with toast for the broth.

  9. What goes wrong: overcooking. A clam that has been in the pan two minutes past opening is chewy and tastes of nothing. Pull them as they go.

Notes

  • Anyone harvesting their own should check the current shellfish biotoxin closures published by the Oregon Department of Agriculture before digging. Closures for domoic acid and paralytic shellfish poisoning are not visible in the clam and not affected by cooking.
  • A licence is required to harvest shellfish in this state, and the rules are ODFW's. Look them up rather than guessing.

Keeping it

Live clams keep a day or two in the fridge in an open bowl under a damp towel, never sealed in a bag and never in water. Cooked clams keep a day and are best picked out of the shell and dropped into a chowder.
