Fish & Shellfish

A Whole Salmon, Roasted

One fish, one pan, one hot oven, and the only real skill is knowing when to stop.

Serves 8 to 10
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
1 hour 20 minutes
Start to finish

A whole fish looks like a project and cooks like a roast chicken with fewer opinions. The bones and the skin hold water in the meat while the oven pulls it out, so the window between right and chalky is several minutes wider than it is on a bare fillet. The trade is that you cannot see the middle, which is why a thermometer goes into the thickest part of the shoulder, alongside the backbone, and not into the belly flap.

Buy or catch a fish that fits your longest pan on the diagonal. Measure the oven before you commit to anything, because a fish that does not fit is a problem you cannot solve at the counter.

What you need

The fish

  • 1 whole salmon, 8 to 10 lb (3.6 to 4.5 kg), gutted, gills removed, scaled
  • 2 tbsp coarse sea salt
  • 1 tsp coarsely ground black pepper
  • 2 tbsp neutral oil

The cavity

  • 1 lemon, sliced thin
  • 8 sprigs thyme
  • 4 cloves garlic, crushed flat
  • 1 small fennel bulb, sliced thin, optional

To finish

  • 3 tbsp butter, melted
  • 2 tbsp flat-leaf parsley, chopped
  • 1 lemon, cut in wedges

How

  1. Set a rack in the middle of the oven and heat it to 400°F (205°C). Line a sheet pan with foil, then lay parchment over the foil. The foil is for the cleanup. The parchment is so the skin releases instead of welding itself to the pan.

  2. Rinse the cavity, run a thumbnail down the backbone to clear the dark blood line, and dry the whole fish hard with paper towels. Wet skin steams. Dry skin browns.

  3. Slash the thickest part of each side three or four times, straight down to the bone, at an angle. The cuts let heat reach the shoulder while the tail is still catching up, which is the whole problem with cooking a wedge-shaped animal evenly.

  4. Salt the cavity and the outside, working salt into the slashes. Pack the cavity with lemon, thyme, garlic and fennel. Do not tie it shut; you want steam to move through.

  5. Rub the outside with oil, lay the fish diagonally on the pan, and slide it in. Figure on roughly 10 minutes per inch measured at the shoulder, but treat that as a reason to start checking rather than a schedule.

  6. Start testing early. Push the probe into the slash at the shoulder until the tip sits near the backbone. Fully cooked, by the FDA figure, is 145 degrees Fahrenheit (63°C), at which point the flesh is opaque all the way through and separates into firm flakes under a fork.

  7. Plenty of people pull salmon at 125 to 130 degrees Fahrenheit (52 to 54°C) for a translucent middle. That is below the FDA number, and it is a real risk, not a technicality. Anyone pregnant, immunocompromised, very young or elderly should get fish taken to the full internal temperature instead.

  8. Rest the fish on the pan for 10 minutes. It coasts upward a few degrees, and the flesh firms enough to lift cleanly. Pour the melted butter over it and scatter parsley.

  9. To serve, run a knife along the backbone and lift the top fillet off in sections. When that side is gone, grip the tail, lift the whole spine away in one piece, and carry on. Nobody has to flip a whole salmon at the table.

  10. What goes wrong: taking it to the point where white albumin is weeping out of every slash. That is protein squeezing water out, and it means the middle is already past. Pull earlier next time and let the rest finish the job.

Notes

  • A fish over about 12 lb (5.4 kg) is better split into two sides and roasted flat. The shoulder on a big one will not catch up to the tail no matter how you slash it.
  • Save the frame. It makes better stock than any fillet trim, and there is a chowder in this collection built on it.

Keeping it

Cooked salmon keeps in the fridge, covered, three to four days. Pick the leftovers off the bone the same night while it is easy, and the flakes go straight into cakes, chowder or a sandwich.
