Game & The Freezer

Aging and Trimming an Animal

Most of what people call gamey was decided before the meat ever saw a kitchen.

One animal, broken down properly
Makes
Several hours across a few days
Hands on
3 to 10 days depending on temperature
Start to finish

Ask ten people why venison tastes strong and you will get ten answers about the animal. Almost always it was the handling. Heat left in the carcass, hair and dirt worked into the meat, fat left on, and silverskin left in place all contribute far more than what the deer was eating. An elk taken care of properly tastes like very good beef with a darker note. One that sat warm in the back of a truck tastes like a mistake.

Aging is a slow, cold, controlled version of the same enzymes that spoil meat when it is warm. Get the temperature right and it tenderises. Get it wrong and you are just growing things.

What you need

What you need

  • 1 hanging space that holds a steady cold temperature, ideally 34 to 38 degrees Fahrenheit (1 to 3°C)
  • 1 thermometer for that space, checked daily
  • 4 game bags, breathable, not plastic
  • 1 boning knife, thin and flexible
  • 1 sharpening steel or stone
  • 6 to 10 rolls of butcher paper or a vacuum sealer
  • 2 boxes nitrile gloves

Optional but useful

  • 1 spare refrigerator, shelves removed
  • 1 fan for air circulation
  • 2 clean towels for wiping the surface

How

  1. Get the heat out first. Everything else is secondary. A carcass that cools quickly is a carcass you can age; one that does not is one you should be cutting and freezing tonight. Open it up, get air moving through it, and quarter it if that is what it takes.

  2. Hang or hold the meat between 34 and 38 degrees Fahrenheit (1 to 3°C). Above about 40 degrees Fahrenheit (4°C) you are no longer aging, you are spoiling slowly. Below freezing, nothing happens at all. This is a narrow window and a thermometer is not optional.

  3. Judge the time by the animal and the temperature. In that range, a young deer wants three to five days and a mature elk five to ten. There is no gain past about two weeks on a home setup, and there is real risk.

  4. Expect the outside to dry into a dark, firm crust. That is normal and it gets trimmed away. What is not normal is anything slimy, anything green, or any sour smell that persists after you have wiped the surface. Trust your nose. It is a better instrument than any rule of thumb.

  5. If you cannot hold that temperature, do not age. Quarter the animal, bone it out and get it cold or frozen. Coolers with ice, drained daily so the meat is not sitting in water, will hold quarters for several days and are how most people here manage it.

  6. When you break it down, remove every scrap of fat. Deer and elk fat is waxy, it does not render pleasantly, it coats the roof of your mouth, and it goes rancid in the freezer faster than anything else on the animal. This is the single biggest flavour decision you will make.

  7. Take out any blood-shot meat, any hair that has worked into the surface, and anything that smells of gut. Cut wide around it. The instinct to save every ounce is exactly how one bad spot flavours twenty pounds of burger.

  8. Separate the muscles at their seams rather than sawing across them. Push a thumb into the natural line between two muscles and they come apart cleanly, which gives you whole pieces to cook rather than a pile of odd chunks.

  9. Sort as you go into three piles: steaks and roasts, braising cuts, and grind. Shanks, neck and anything laced with connective tissue go to braise or grind, never to a hot pan.

  10. Wrap tightly and label. Air is the enemy in a freezer, and a package you cannot identify in the dark is a package that gets used last, if ever.

  11. On disease and parasites: freezing does not make everything safe and it does not substitute for testing. Rules and recommendations around chronic wasting disease, sampling, and what to do with an animal that looked wrong are Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife's to publish, and they change. Check with ODFW and your state's current hunter guide rather than taking a recipe page's word for any of it.

Notes

  • A dedicated old refrigerator with the shelves pulled out and a small fan inside is the most practical aging chamber most households will ever have.
  • Bone-in aging protects the meat from drying but takes more space. Boned-out muscles age faster and need watching.

Keeping it

Wrapped tight and held at 0 degrees Fahrenheit (-18°C), whole-muscle cuts keep their quality about a year and ground meat about four months. Both are safe indefinitely at that temperature; it is quality that degrades, not safety.
