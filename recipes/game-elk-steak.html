Elk Steak in a Cast Iron Pan

Beefier than beef, half the fat, and the pan needs to be hotter than you think.

Serves 4
Makes
20 minutes
Hands on
1 hour with the dry brine
Start to finish

Elk is the most beef-like of the wild meats and the easiest sell to somebody who claims not to like game. It is also leaner than any beef you can buy, which means the fat that normally lubricates a steak and buys you thirty seconds of margin is simply not present.

So the fat comes from the pan. A hard sear in tallow, a butter baste at the end, and a resting plate with more butter on it. The other adjustment is thickness: cut elk steaks thicker than you would cut beef, because a thin lean steak is overcooked before it is browned.

What you need

Steaks

  • 4 elk steaks from the round or sirloin, 1.25 inches thick, about 8 oz (225 g) each
  • 2 tsp coarse salt
  • 1 tsp cracked black pepper

Pan

  • 2 tbsp beef tallow or clarified butter
  • 4 tbsp butter
  • 4 sprigs rosemary
  • 4 cloves garlic, crushed

Finishing

  • 1 tsp flaky salt
  • 2 tbsp softened butter mashed with 1 tsp chopped rosemary
  • 1 tsp lemon zest

How

  1. Salt the steaks on both sides and leave them on a rack in the fridge, uncovered, for 45 minutes. This dries the surface and seasons the inside, and both matter more on lean meat than on marbled beef.

  2. Take them out and let them lose the worst of the chill while the pan heats. A cold steak in a hot pan cooks its outside twice as far before the middle wakes up.

  3. Heat a cast iron skillet over high heat, dry, until a drop of water bounces and skitters instead of sitting and boiling. That takes several minutes and there is no shortcut.

  4. Add the tallow, swirl once, and lay the steaks in without crowding. Two at a time in a ten inch pan. You should hear a hard continuous sizzle immediately; if you hear a polite one, the pan was not ready.

  5. Leave them alone until a proper crust has formed and the steak releases from the pan without tearing, about two to three minutes. Then flip once.

  6. Drop in butter, rosemary and garlic, tilt the pan and baste continuously with a spoon. The butter should be foaming and smell nutty, not black and acrid; pull the pan off the heat for a moment if it is getting away from you.

  7. Pull the steaks at an internal temperature of 125 to 130 degrees Fahrenheit (52 to 54°C), because they will climb five degrees while resting to land at medium rare. Whole-muscle elk gets the same treatment as a whole-muscle beef steak; the 160°F (71°C) rule is for ground meat only.

  8. Rest on a warm plate with the rosemary butter melting on top, at least 8 minutes. Skipping the rest costs you the juice on the board instead of in the meat.

  9. Slice across the grain and finish with flaky salt and lemon zest.

  10. What goes wrong: medium well. Elk past about 145 degrees Fahrenheit (63°C) goes dry, grainy and slightly liver-ish, and it does it fast. If your household insists on well done, cook that portion as a braise instead and everyone will be happier.

Notes

  • Round steaks from an older animal benefit from a light jaccard or a fork stab all over before salting. Sirloin does not need it.
  • Cast iron holds heat; thin stainless does not. If a lightweight pan is all you have, do two steaks at a time and let it recover between batches.

Keeping it

Raw steaks keep three days in the fridge, a year vacuum sealed in the freezer at 0 degrees Fahrenheit (-18°C). Cooked, three days, and sliced cold into a sandwich they are better than reheated.
