Game & The Freezer

Freezer Burn, and How to Avoid It

It is not burning and it is not spoilage. It is your meat evaporating in the dark.

A freezer full of meat that is still worth eating
Makes
30 minutes of doing it properly
Hands on
The life of the package
Start to finish

Freezer burn is sublimation. Ice on the surface of the meat turns straight to vapour without melting, moves through whatever gap exists in the packaging, and refreezes as frost on the inside of the bag. What it leaves behind is a patch of dehydrated, oxidised meat that is grey-brown, leathery, and tastes stale and slightly of cardboard.

It is not dangerous. Meat held at a proper freezer temperature is safe indefinitely. It is purely a quality problem, and it is entirely preventable with air removal, tight wrapping and a freezer that is not being opened forty times a day.

What you need

What actually prevents it

  • 1 vacuum sealer, or 2 rolls of freezer paper and good tape
  • 1 box of quality freezer bags, not sandwich bags
  • 1 freezer thermometer
  • 2 rolls plastic wrap for the inner layer
  • 1 permanent marker that writes on plastic

Useful extras

  • 1 chest freezer, which holds temperature far better than an upright
  • 6 plastic bins for organising by cut
  • 1 inventory list taped to the lid

How

  1. Get the air out. Air is what carries the moisture away, so every technique below is really the same technique. A vacuum sealer does it best; the water displacement method with a zip bag lowered slowly into a sink does it nearly as well for free.

  2. Double wrap if you are not vacuum sealing. Plastic wrap pressed hard against the surface of the meat first, then freezer paper with the coated side in, then tape every seam. Butcher paper alone breathes and is not enough on its own.

  3. Pack flat and thin. A flat package freezes through in a fraction of the time of a lump, which means smaller ice crystals, less cell damage and less liquid lost on thawing.

  4. Keep the freezer at 0 degrees Fahrenheit (-18°C) or below and check it with a thermometer rather than trusting the dial. Every degree warmer speeds up sublimation, and a freezer that cycles wide swings is worse than one that runs slightly warm and steady.

  5. Freeze fast and in small loads. Dumping forty pounds of warm packages into a freezer raises the temperature of everything already in there. Spread new packages out in a single layer, and stack them only once they are solid.

  6. Fill the empty space. A full freezer holds temperature far better than a half-empty one; jugs of water fill the gaps and act as thermal ballast, which also buys you hours during a power cut.

  7. Stop opening it. Every time the door opens, humid room air rushes in, condenses and frosts. A chest freezer in a garage that gets opened once a week will keep meat looking fresh far longer than a kitchen upright that gets browsed.

  8. Rotate. Put new packages at the bottom or the back and pull from the front. An inventory list taped to the lid, updated with a pencil, is worth more than any gadget.

  9. If you find a burned package, do not throw the whole thing out. Trim the grey leathery patches away with a knife while the meat is still partly frozen, which is when they cut most cleanly, and use the rest in a braise or a chili where the loss is least noticeable.

  10. Judge severity honestly. A few small patches is a trim job. A package that is grey throughout, dry to the touch and smells stale when it thaws is not going to taste good in anything, and pretending otherwise wastes the other ingredients.

Notes

  • Ground meat burns faster than whole muscle because there is vastly more surface area. Package it flatter and use it sooner.
  • Vacuum sealing roughly doubles the useful life of everything. If you shoot an animal most years, it pays for itself in one season.

Keeping it

At 0 degrees Fahrenheit (-18°C): whole-muscle cuts about a year, ground about four months, sausage about three, and roughly double all of those vacuum sealed. Safety is not the limit here; quality is.
