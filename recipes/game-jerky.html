Game & The Freezer

Venison Jerky With the Safety Step

Drying alone does not make meat safe, which is the part most jerky recipes skip.

About 1 lb (450 g) finished jerky
Makes
45 minutes
Hands on
About 12 hours
Start to finish

Jerky is dried meat, and the mistake in most home recipes is assuming drying is the same as cooking. It is not. A dehydrator running at a typical setting removes water slowly enough that bacteria can adapt and survive the process, and dried survivors are still there when you eat it three weeks later.

The fix is one extra step, recommended by USDA and Extension services: bring the meat to an internal temperature of 160°F (71°C) either before drying or right after. That is the whole difference between jerky and a gamble, and it costs you fifteen minutes.

What you need

Meat

  • 3 lb (1.4 kg) venison from the round or another lean roast, all fat and silverskin removed

Marinade

  • 3/4 cup soy sauce
  • 1/4 cup Worcestershire sauce
  • 3 tbsp brown sugar
  • 2 tbsp cider vinegar
  • 1 tbsp coarsely ground black pepper
  • 2 tsp onion powder
  • 2 tsp garlic powder
  • 1 tsp smoked paprika
  • 1 tsp red pepper flakes
  • 1 tsp liquid smoke, optional

Equipment

  • 1 dehydrator or an oven that will hold 175°F (80°C)
  • 1 probe thermometer
  • 4 wire racks and sheet pans if using the oven

How

  1. Freeze the roast for about an hour until it is firm at the edges. Partly frozen meat slices evenly, and even slices are the difference between a batch that finishes together and one that has burnt strips and damp ones in the same tray.

  2. Trim every trace of fat. Fat does not dry, it goes rancid, and it is the reason a batch of jerky tastes wrong after two weeks in a jar.

  3. Slice a quarter inch thick. With the grain gives chewy jerky, across the grain gives tender jerky that breaks apart more easily. Pick one and be consistent within the batch.

  4. Whisk the marinade and submerge the strips, pressing out the air. Refrigerate 6 to 24 hours. Longer is saltier, not better, and past a day the texture starts to go mushy.

  5. Now the safety step. The straightforward version: lay the drained strips on racks over sheet pans and put them in an oven at 275°F (135°C) until a probe pushed lengthwise into a thick strip reads an internal temperature of 160°F (71°C). That usually takes about ten to fifteen minutes and the meat will look cooked and grey rather than dried.

  6. Alternatively, dry first and heat afterwards, which some people prefer for texture: dry to finished, then hold the strips in a 275°F (135°C) oven for ten minutes to reach the same internal temperature. Either order works; skipping it entirely does not.

  7. Move the strips to the dehydrator at 160 to 165°F (71 to 74°C), or to an oven at its lowest setting with the door propped open a crack and a fan nearby. Leave space between strips so air can move.

  8. Dry until a strip bends and cracks along the bend but does not snap in half, and shows no wet or shiny spots when you tear one open. That is typically four to six hours, and thickness matters more than the clock.

  9. Cool the strips completely on the racks before packing. Warm jerky in a sealed bag sweats, and that condensation is exactly what you spent six hours removing.

  10. Condition it: pack loosely in a jar for a day and shake it occasionally. If you see any condensation on the glass, it needs more drying time. If you see mould at any point, the whole batch goes out.

  11. A note on wild game specifically. Freezing does not reliably kill everything, and this style of drying is not a substitute for cooking. For anything to do with parasites, chronic wasting disease sampling or testing an animal that looked unwell, go to the Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife and your current state hunter guide rather than a recipe.

Notes

  • Ground meat jerky pressed through a jerky gun follows the same rule and is less forgiving, because there is no intact interior at all. The heat step matters more there, not less.
  • A dehydrator that will not hold at least 160°F (71°C) is fine, provided you are doing the oven heat step. Do not use one as your only heat source.

Keeping it

In a sealed jar in a cool cupboard, two to three weeks. In the fridge, two months. In the freezer, a year. Oxygen absorbers help; a scrap of moisture does not.
