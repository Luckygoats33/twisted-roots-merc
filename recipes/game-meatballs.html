Game & The Freezer

Venison Meatballs

A panade, added fat, and a light hand, which is three ways of saying do not make them dense.

About 30 meatballs, serves 6
Makes
40 minutes
Hands on
1 hour 30 minutes
Start to finish

Lean ground meat makes dense, dry meatballs unless you build in something that holds water. That something is a panade, which is bread soaked in milk mashed to a paste. The starch traps moisture and physically gets between the meat proteins so they cannot tighten into a ball bearing.

Add fat as well, because venison has none, and handle the mix like you are folding rather than kneading. A meatball should give when you press it, not bounce.

What you need

Panade

  • 3 slices white bread, crusts off, torn
  • 2/3 cup whole milk

Meat

  • 1.5 lb (680 g) ground venison
  • 8 oz (225 g) ground pork
  • 2 large eggs
  • 3/4 cup grated Parmesan
  • 4 cloves garlic, minced fine
  • 1/3 cup finely chopped parsley
  • 1.5 tsp fine sea salt
  • 1 tsp black pepper
  • 1/2 tsp fennel seed, cracked
  • 1/4 tsp ground nutmeg

Sauce

  • 3 tbsp olive oil
  • 1 onion, finely diced
  • 4 cloves garlic, sliced
  • 2 cans crushed tomatoes, 28 oz (800 g) each
  • 1 tsp dried oregano
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • 6 basil leaves
  • 1 tsp fine sea salt

How

  1. Soak the torn bread in the milk for 10 minutes, then mash it with a fork into a smooth paste. Lumps of soggy bread in a meatball are unpleasant in a way that is hard to describe and easy to notice.

  2. Combine the panade with the venison, pork, eggs, Parmesan, garlic, parsley and seasonings. Mix with your fingertips, lifting and folding, until it is just uniform. Squeezing and kneading builds a springy texture.

  3. Fry a teaspoon of the mix in a small pan until browned through and taste it for salt. Adjust before you roll thirty of them.

  4. Chill the mix 30 minutes so it firms up, then roll balls about an inch and a half across with wet hands. Wet hands are the difference between round meatballs and a mess.

  5. Start the sauce: warm the oil, cook the onion until soft and translucent, add garlic for a minute, then the tomatoes, oregano, sugar and salt. Simmer at a lazy bubble while you brown the meatballs.

  6. Brown the meatballs in a wide skillet in batches, turning them, until they have a dark crust on several sides. They will not be cooked through and should not be; they are going into the sauce.

  7. Slide the browned meatballs into the simmering sauce and cook, covered, at a bare simmer for 25 minutes. The sauce goes into the meatballs and the meat flavours the sauce, which is the entire reason not to finish them in the pan.

  8. Check one. Ground meat, venison and pork alike, is done at an internal temperature of 160°F (71°C), and a probe into the middle of the largest meatball should confirm it. Grinding distributes surface bacteria through the whole mix, so this number applies regardless of species.

  9. Tear the basil in at the end and let the pot sit off the heat 10 minutes before serving.

  10. What goes wrong: too much filler. A panade at this ratio is invisible in the finished meatball. Doubled, it makes bread dumplings with a meat flavour, and people can tell.

Notes

  • Ground pork here is doing the fat job. Ground beef chuck works too; straight venison does not.
  • These freeze beautifully cooked in their sauce, and they are the reason to make a double batch while the grinder is dirty anyway.

Keeping it

Fridge four days in sauce, freezer three months. Reheat covered, gently, until the sauce bubbles at the edge and the centres are hot.
