Game & The Freezer

Shanks, Osso Buco Style

Cross-cut shanks, a long slow oven, and the marrow is the reason.

Serves 6
Makes
45 minutes
Hands on
4 hours 30 minutes
Start to finish

Most people grind shanks, which is a waste of the most interesting cut on a deer or an elk. A shank is dense muscle wrapped around a bone full of marrow, and it is nearly all connective tissue, which under a long braise becomes something no other cut can produce. Ask a butcher to cross-cut them into thick rounds, or do it yourself with a bone saw.

The gremolata at the end is not a garnish. Raw lemon zest, garlic and parsley scattered over a heavy braise is the thing that keeps four hours of rich cooking from being monotonous by the third bite.

What you need

Shanks

  • 6 cross-cut venison or elk shanks, about 2 inches thick
  • 2 tsp fine sea salt
  • 1 tsp black pepper
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 3 tbsp olive oil
  • 6 lengths kitchen twine

Braise

  • 2 onions, finely diced
  • 2 carrots, finely diced
  • 2 stalks celery, finely diced
  • 5 cloves garlic, minced
  • 2 tbsp tomato paste
  • 1.5 cups dry white wine
  • 1 can whole tomatoes, 28 oz (800 g), crushed by hand
  • 3 cups beef or game stock
  • 3 sprigs thyme
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 1 strip lemon peel

Gremolata

  • 1 cup finely chopped parsley
  • 2 cloves garlic, minced very fine
  • 2 tbsp lemon zest
  • 1 pinch flaky salt

How

  1. Tie a length of twine around the circumference of each shank. They will fall apart in the pot otherwise, and half the pleasure is putting a whole round on a plate with the bone standing up in the middle.

  2. Season the shanks, dredge them in flour, and knock off the excess. Heat the oil in a wide Dutch oven over medium-high.

  3. Brown them thoroughly on both flat faces, in batches, until a hard mahogany crust has formed and they lift away from the pan without sticking. This takes longer than you want it to; do not rush it and do not crowd the pan.

  4. Remove the shanks. Lower the heat and cook the onion, carrot and celery in the same fat until soft, sweet-smelling and just starting to catch at the edges of the pot.

  5. Add garlic and tomato paste, stirring until the paste turns brick red and smells cooked rather than sharp. Deglaze with the white wine, scraping the bottom clean, and let it reduce by half.

  6. Add tomatoes, stock, thyme, bay and lemon peel. Settle the shanks back in so the liquid comes about two thirds of the way up but does not cover them; the exposed tops brown while the bottoms braise.

  7. Bring to a bare simmer on the stove, cover, and put it in a 300°F (150°C) oven. Peek after half an hour to confirm the liquid is barely moving. Anything more vigorous will string the meat out.

  8. Braise until the meat has pulled back from the bone, a fork twists in it with no resistance, and the marrow in the centre has gone soft and glossy. Figure three and a half to four hours, and check rather than trusting the clock.

  9. There is no useful internal temperature target for a shank. It goes well past 160°F (71°C) within the first hour and then spends three more hours converting connective tissue, so you judge it with a fork and not a thermometer.

  10. Lift the shanks out carefully and snip off the twine. Skim the fat from the sauce and, if it is thin, reduce it on the stovetop until it coats a spoon.

  11. Chop the gremolata ingredients together on a board so the oils mix, and scatter over each shank at the table. Serve with polenta, risotto or bread.

  12. What goes wrong: shanks submerged completely and cooked hot. They come out grey and shredded. Half-submerged and barely simmering is the whole method.

Notes

  • A marrow spoon or the handle of a teaspoon is the correct tool for the middle of the bone. Whoever cooked it gets first go.
  • This is a good candidate for making a day ahead. Cool it in the pot, refrigerate, lift the fat off, and reheat gently in the oven.

Keeping it

Fridge four days in the sauce, freezer three months. Reheat covered at 300°F (150°C) until the sauce is bubbling gently at the edges and the meat is hot through.
