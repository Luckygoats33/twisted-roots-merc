Game & The Freezer

Getting the Silverskin Off

The livery taste people blame on the deer is usually still attached to the meat.

One backstrap or roast, cleaned
Makes
20 minutes
Hands on
20 minutes
Start to finish

Silverskin is the pearly sheet of connective tissue that wraps whole muscles. It is not fat, it does not render, and it does not soften with cooking. Under heat it shrinks harder than the meat does, which curls a steak into a bowl and squeezes it tight, and it carries a strong metallic flavour that people confuse with the animal itself.

Removing it properly takes a thin flexible knife and about twenty minutes of patience. It is the highest-return knife skill in this whole category, and it is worth doing badly a few times to learn.

What you need

Tools

  • 1 boning or fillet knife, thin, flexible, genuinely sharp
  • 1 large cutting board with a damp towel under it
  • 1 clean tray for trimmed meat
  • 1 bowl for scraps

The meat

  • 1 venison or elk backstrap, roast or whole muscle, chilled
  • 2 paper towels for grip

How

  1. Work with cold meat. Cold muscle is firm and holds its shape under a knife; room temperature muscle is soft and slippery and you will take meat off with the skin.

  2. Find an edge of the silverskin at the thick end and slide the knife tip underneath to lift a flap about an inch wide. Get enough of a tab that you can hold it firmly with a paper towel between your fingers and the meat.

  3. Angle the blade slightly upward, into the silverskin rather than into the meat. This is the whole trick, and it feels wrong the first three times you do it.

  4. Pull the tab taut with your free hand and push the knife forward in a shallow sawing motion, keeping tension the entire time. The skin peels off in a strip and the blade rides along underneath it.

  5. Do not try to take it all in one piece. Two or three strips down the length of a backstrap is normal and faster than fighting for a single sheet.

  6. Check the result by running a palm over the muscle. It should feel like bare meat, faintly tacky, with no smooth slick patches. Any silver you can still see will still be there when it comes off the grill.

  7. Turn the muscle over and repeat on any seams and on the strip of tough tissue that runs down the underside of a backstrap.

  8. Trim any remaining fat while you are here. On deer and elk it is waxy, it does not improve anything, and it is where rancid freezer flavours start.

  9. A cleaned muscle also cooks more predictably, which matters because whole-muscle venison is pulled at an internal temperature of 130 to 135 degrees Fahrenheit (54 to 57°C) and a curled, unevenly thick piece will not read honestly on a probe.

  10. Keep the trimmings separate. Meat scraped off the silverskin goes in the grind pile; the skin itself goes in the bin, or into a stockpot if you want gelatin, where long wet heat will finally break it down.

  11. What goes wrong: cutting downward. If your strips are coming off with a layer of red meat attached, raise the back of the knife and let the blade climb toward the surface. You want a translucent ribbon, not a steak.

Notes

  • A dull knife makes this nearly impossible. Sharpen before you start, not halfway through when you are annoyed.
  • Nobody gets a clean backstrap on the first animal. It is a physical skill and it arrives after about the fourth one.

Keeping it

Cleaned whole muscles wrap tighter and freeze better than untrimmed ones, because there is less surface irregularity to trap air. Vacuum seal if you can, double wrap if you cannot.
