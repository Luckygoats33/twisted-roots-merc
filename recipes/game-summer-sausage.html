Summer Sausage, and a Word About Nitrite

A cured, smoked, shelf-friendly sausage where the curing salt is a safety ingredient, not a flavour one.

About 5 lb (2.3 kg), four chubs
Makes
1 hour 30 minutes
Hands on
2 days including the cure and the smoke
Start to finish

Summer sausage is a cured sausage held for hours at low temperatures in a smoker, which is precisely the temperature range where Clostridium botulinum is happiest. Curing salt containing sodium nitrite is what makes that process safe, and it is not an optional flavouring you can leave out because you would rather not use it. Leave it out and you are making something else, and something riskier.

It is also toxic in excess, which is why it is dyed pink so nobody confuses it with table salt, and why the ratio below comes from tested guidance rather than from anybody's judgement. Weigh it on a scale that reads grams. Do not scoop it.

What you need

Meat

  • 3.5 lb (1.6 kg) venison, trimmed clean, in cubes
  • 1.5 lb (680 g) pork shoulder or pork fat, in cubes

Cure and seasoning, for 5 lb of meat

  • 1 level tsp Prague Powder No. 1, which is 6.25 percent sodium nitrite, weighed at about 5.7 g
  • 3 tbsp kosher salt
  • 2 tbsp coarsely ground black pepper
  • 1 tbsp mustard seed
  • 2 tsp garlic powder
  • 2 tsp sugar
  • 1 tbsp whole coriander seed, cracked
  • 1/2 cup ice water
  • 1 cup high-temperature cheddar, optional

Casings and equipment

  • 4 fibrous casings, 2.5 inch diameter, soaked
  • 1 gram scale that reads to a tenth of a gram
  • 1 probe thermometer with a long lead
  • 3 cups hickory or cherry chips

How

  1. Read a tested recipe before you start, and use its cure ratio rather than this page's if they differ. The standard guidance for Prague Powder No. 1 is one level teaspoon per five pounds of meat, and both halves of that sentence matter: the right product, and the right weight for the batch you actually have.

  2. Understand what the nitrite is for. It inhibits Clostridium botulinum during the long warm smoke, it keeps the meat pink, and it contributes the tang people associate with the style. It is a preservative doing a safety job in a process specifically designed around it.

  3. Chill everything: meat, fat, grinder parts, bowls. Grind the venison and pork together once through a coarse plate, then run half through a fine plate for a tighter texture.

  4. Weigh the cure on the gram scale and dissolve it in the ice water along with the salt, so it disperses evenly instead of sitting in pockets. Uneven cure is both a safety problem and a taste problem.

  5. Add the cure water and the rest of the seasoning to the meat and mix hard for two to three minutes, until the mix is sticky enough that a handful sticks to an upturned palm. That bind is what gives summer sausage its sliceable texture.

  6. Cover and refrigerate 24 hours. The cure needs that time to distribute all the way through, and you cannot shorten it by mixing longer.

  7. Stuff into soaked fibrous casings firmly, without air pockets, and tie the ends. Prick any bubbles you can see. Air pockets are where things go wrong.

  8. Bring the smoker up slowly. Start dry around 130 degrees Fahrenheit (54°C) for an hour with no smoke to dry the casing, then add smoke and step the chamber up by roughly 10 degrees Fahrenheit every hour to a maximum of 175°F (80°C).

  9. Cook until the internal temperature of the chubs reaches 155 to 160°F (68 to 71°C). Use a probe in the centre of the thickest chub and leave it there. Pushing the chamber hot to hurry this renders the fat out and leaves greasy pockets under the casing.

  10. Plunge the finished chubs into an ice water bath until the internal temperature drops below about 110 degrees Fahrenheit (43°C). This stops the cooking, sets the texture, and gets the sausage down through the warm range quickly.

  11. Bloom them at room temperature for an hour, then refrigerate overnight before slicing. Cutting into a warm chub gives you a crumbly face and a disappointment.

  12. If any of this feels like guesswork, stop and find a tested recipe with stated weights from a source that publishes them properly. Cured sausage is not a place for improvisation, and nobody at this store has cooked this particular batch for you.

Notes

  • Prague Powder No. 1 is for anything cooked. Prague Powder No. 2 contains nitrate as well and is for long dry-cured products that are never cooked. They are not interchangeable.
  • Keep curing salt labelled, in its own container, well away from the salt cellar. It is dyed pink for exactly this reason.

Keeping it

Refrigerated, wrapped, about three weeks. Vacuum sealed and frozen, six months. Despite the name, treat it as a refrigerated product rather than something to leave in a cupboard.
