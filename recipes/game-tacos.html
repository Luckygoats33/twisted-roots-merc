Game & The Freezer

Venison Tacos, Two Ways

Fast with ground, slow with a shoulder, and both are better than the beef version.

Serves 6
Makes
30 minutes for the fast version
Hands on
40 minutes fast, 3 hours 30 minutes slow
Start to finish

Venison takes to chile and lime better than beef does, because the meat has a slightly darker, more mineral flavour that acid and heat lift rather than fight. This page gives the weeknight version, with ground meat in a skillet, and the weekend version, with a shoulder braised until it shreds.

The ground one obeys ground-meat rules and gets cooked to a number. The braised one goes so far past any number that the question stops being relevant, which is worth understanding rather than memorising.

What you need

Fast version

  • 2 lb (900 g) ground venison
  • 4 oz (110 g) bacon or pork fat, finely diced
  • 1 large onion, diced
  • 4 cloves garlic, minced
  • 2 tbsp chili powder
  • 1 tbsp ground cumin
  • 2 tsp smoked paprika
  • 1 tsp dried oregano
  • 1.5 tsp fine sea salt
  • 2 tbsp tomato paste
  • 1 cup beef or game stock
  • 1 tbsp lime juice

Slow version

  • 3 lb (1.4 kg) venison shoulder, in large chunks
  • 3 dried guajillo chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 dried ancho chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 1 onion, quartered
  • 5 cloves garlic
  • 1 tbsp cumin seed, toasted
  • 2 cups stock
  • 2 tbsp cider vinegar
  • 2 tsp fine sea salt

To serve

  • 18 corn tortillas
  • 1 cup diced white onion
  • 1 cup chopped cilantro
  • 3 limes, in wedges
  • 1 cup crumbled cotija
  • 2 avocados, sliced

How

  1. Fast version: render the bacon in a heavy skillet over medium heat until the fat runs. Ground venison has essentially no fat and without this it browns into dry crumbs.

  2. Raise the heat, add the venison, and press it into a flat layer. Leave it undisturbed until the underside has browned properly before you start breaking it up. Constant stirring steams it grey.

  3. Add onion and cook until soft, then garlic and all the dry spices, stirring until they smell toasted and begin to stick to the pan.

  4. Stir in tomato paste, cook it until it darkens, then add the stock and simmer until most of the liquid has gone and the meat is glossy and just moist rather than wet. Confirm with a probe that the meat has reached an internal temperature of 160°F (71°C), which is the standard for any ground meat and not optional here. Finish with lime juice.

  5. Slow version: toast the dried chiles in a dry pan, pressing them flat, until pliable and fragrant but nowhere near blackened. Soak them in hot water 20 minutes.

  6. Blend the soaked chiles with the onion, garlic, cumin, a cup of the stock, the vinegar and salt into a smooth sauce.

  7. Brown the venison chunks hard in a Dutch oven, pour the chile sauce and remaining stock over, and braise covered at 300°F (150°C) until the meat pulls apart under two forks with no resistance at all, about three hours.

  8. Shred the meat, return it to the sauce, and reduce uncovered until the sauce clings to the meat instead of pooling under it.

  9. For the crisp version, spread the shredded meat on a sheet pan and run it under a broiler until the edges char and crisp, then toss it back through the sauce. This is worth the extra five minutes.

  10. Warm the tortillas directly over a flame or in a dry pan until they puff and spot, and hold them in a folded towel.

  11. Build with onion, cilantro, cotija, avocado and lime. Two tortillas per taco if the filling is wet, which it will be.

Notes

  • Elk works identically. Bear and other omnivores do not; they carry a trichinella risk and must be cooked to a much higher internal temperature. Look up the current guidance for the species before you cook anything unfamiliar.
  • Double the spice mix for the fast version and keep it in a jar. It turns this into a twenty minute dinner.

Keeping it

Both fillings keep four days in the fridge. The braised one freezes for three months in its sauce; the ground one freezes for two and dries out slightly on reheating.
