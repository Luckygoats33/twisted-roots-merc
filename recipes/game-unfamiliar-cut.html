Game & The Freezer

Cooking a Cut You Have Never Seen Before

There are only two questions, and the animal answers both of them.

One unfamiliar package, cooked correctly
Makes
20 minutes
Hands on
Anywhere from 15 minutes to 5 hours
Start to finish

Sooner or later somebody hands you a package labelled in handwriting you cannot read, or you get a share of an animal you did not butcher, and there is a muscle on the board that does not match anything in a cookbook. The good news is that meat is more predictable than recipes suggest. Everything an animal has is either a muscle that worked hard or one that did not, and that single fact tells you almost everything about how to cook it.

What follows is the decision tree, and it applies to elk, deer, bear, a goat somebody gave you, and cuts of beef you have never bought.

What you need

What you need to decide

  • 1 unfamiliar piece of meat, thawed
  • 1 sharp knife to look at the grain
  • 1 probe thermometer
  • 2 minutes of thinking before the pan goes on

For the fast route

  • 2 tbsp beef tallow or high smoke point oil
  • 2 tsp coarse salt
  • 3 tbsp butter for basting

For the slow route

  • 2 cups stock
  • 1 cup wine
  • 2 onions, sliced
  • 3 cloves garlic, crushed
  • 2 bay leaves

How

  1. Question one: where was it on the animal? Anything from the back, along the spine, or from the inside of the hind leg did comparatively little work and is tender. Anything from the shoulder, neck, shank, flank or the outside of the hind leg worked constantly and is not.

  2. Question two: what does it look like? Hold it up to the light. Fine, close grain with little visible connective tissue means fast and hot. Coarse grain shot through with white sheets and seams means slow and wet. This test works even when you have no idea what the muscle is called.

  3. Press it. A tender muscle feels uniform and yielding. A working muscle feels dense and resistant, and often has a rubbery sheet you can feel through the surface.

  4. Fast route, for tender cuts: trim the silverskin, salt it an hour ahead, get a pan very hot, sear hard on all sides, baste with butter, and pull it at an internal temperature of 130 to 135 degrees Fahrenheit (54 to 57°C) for medium rare. Whole-muscle game gets the same latitude as beef. Rest it as long as you cooked it.

  5. Slow route, for working cuts: brown it hard first, then cook it covered in liquid at 300°F (150°C) until a fork twists in it with no resistance and it starts to come apart under its own weight. That is three to five hours and it is a texture, not a clock.

  6. If you genuinely cannot tell, cut a small piece off and cook it in a pan. Ninety seconds and a taste settles the question and costs you two ounces of meat.

  7. Test the grain direction before you carve anything. Find the lines of muscle fibre and cut across them, not along them. Slicing with the grain makes a tender cut chewy and a chewy cut inedible.

  8. Never split the difference. The worst outcome for both categories is medium heat for a medium time, which is too long for a tender cut and nowhere near long enough for a tough one. Commit to fast or slow.

  9. Know your exceptions. Flank and skirt style muscles are coarse-grained but thin, and they work fast and hot provided you slice them very thin across the grain afterwards. Anything from an omnivore, bear especially, has a parasite risk that changes the rules entirely and needs a much higher internal temperature; look up the current guidance for that species rather than assuming.

  10. When the animal itself is the unknown, not just the cut, go to the Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife and your state hunter guide for anything about disease, testing or handling. Freezing does not make everything safe and no cooking method substitutes for knowing what you have.

Notes

  • Ground anything, from any species, goes to an internal temperature of 160°F (71°C). That rule never has an exception and it does not depend on which muscle it came from.
  • Write on the package next time. Two words in marker save an argument every winter.

Keeping it

Whatever it turns out to be, the storage rules do not change: thawed raw whole muscle three to five days in the fridge, cooked meat four days, and everything wrapped tight if it goes back in the freezer.
