Game & The Freezer

What to Do With the Trim

The bag of scraps in the bottom of the fridge is stock, a confit and a pot of ragu.

Varies, roughly 3 quarts of stock plus a pot of ragu
Makes
1 hour
Hands on
8 hours across two days
Start to finish

After an animal is broken down there is a pile of things that are not steaks, not roasts and not clean enough for the grinder: neck scraps, rib meat, the ends of the flank, bones with meat still on them. Most of it gets thrown away, which is a shame, because pound for pound it has more flavour than the backstrap everyone fought over.

Three uses, in the order they make sense. Roast the bones for stock. Braise the ragged meat into a ragu. Render nothing, because game fat is not worth saving no matter how thrifty you feel.

What you need

Stock

  • 8 lb (3.6 kg) bones, sawn into lengths, with meat scraps attached
  • 2 tbsp neutral oil
  • 2 onions, halved
  • 3 carrots, in chunks
  • 3 stalks celery, in chunks
  • 1 head garlic, halved crosswise
  • 3 tbsp tomato paste
  • 2 cups red wine
  • 6 quarts cold water
  • 10 peppercorns
  • 3 bay leaves
  • 6 parsley stems

Ragu

  • 3 lb (1.4 kg) ragged trim meat, silverskin removed, chopped
  • 6 oz (170 g) pancetta or bacon, diced
  • 2 onions, finely diced
  • 2 carrots, finely diced
  • 2 stalks celery, finely diced
  • 1 cup dry red wine
  • 1 can crushed tomatoes, 28 oz (800 g)
  • 3 cups of the stock above
  • 1 cup whole milk
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 1 tsp fine sea salt

How

  1. Sort the trim into three piles: bones, meat clean enough to eat, and anything blood-shot, hairy or smelling of gut. The third pile goes out. One bad handful will flavour the whole pot and there is no rescuing it.

  2. Roast the bones with the oil at 450°F (230°C), turning once, until they are deeply browned and smell roasted rather than raw. Pale bones make pale stock that tastes like dishwater.

  3. Add the vegetables to the pan for the last twenty minutes, then smear the tomato paste over everything and roast until it darkens.

  4. Move it all to a stockpot, deglaze the roasting pan with the wine and scrape every brown speck in, then cover with cold water by two inches and add the aromatics.

  5. Bring it up slowly and hold it just below a simmer, with the surface trembling and a bubble every few seconds, for six to eight hours. Skim the grey foam for the first half hour. Never boil it, or the stock emulsifies its own fat and comes out cloudy and greasy.

  6. Strain, cool quickly in a shallow pan, and refrigerate. The fat sets on top as a hard disc; lift it off and throw it out, because game fat is waxy and it will taste of the freezer within weeks.

  7. For the ragu, render the pancetta, then brown the chopped trim meat hard in batches until it has real colour. Set it aside.

  8. Cook the diced vegetables in the fat until completely soft and sweet, about 15 minutes, then return the meat, add the wine, and reduce until the pan is nearly dry.

  9. Add tomatoes, stock, bay and salt and simmer, barely covered, for three hours, until the meat has collapsed into threads and the sauce has gone dark and thick enough to hold a line behind a spoon.

  10. Stir in the milk in the last half hour. It sounds strange and it is traditional; it softens the acidity and gives the sauce a rounder texture.

  11. A braise like this passes any internal temperature target within the first hour and keeps going for reasons that have nothing to do with safety, so judge it by whether the meat shreds under a fork.

  12. What goes wrong: keeping trim you should have thrown away. Thrift is a virtue right up to the point where it costs you six hours of cooking and a pot of stock.

Notes

  • Bones freeze indefinitely for practical purposes. Collect them across a season and do one big stock day rather than several small bad ones.
  • Stock reduced to a syrup and frozen in an ice cube tray takes up almost no room and turns a pan sauce into a good pan sauce.

Keeping it

Stock keeps five days in the fridge and six months frozen. Ragu keeps five days and freezes four months, and it is the single most useful thing in a freezer on a weeknight.
