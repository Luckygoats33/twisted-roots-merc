Game & The Freezer

Venison Backstrap, Reverse Seared

The best cut on the animal, and the easiest one to ruin in ninety seconds.

Serves 4
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
1 hour 15 minutes
Start to finish

Backstrap is the loin that runs along the outside of the spine, and it is lean enough that the usual hot-pan-then-rest routine leaves a grey band an eighth of an inch thick around a raw middle. Reverse searing fixes that: a low oven brings the whole piece up gently and evenly, and then a very short violent sear builds the crust at the end, when there is no time left to overcook anything.

Whole-muscle venison and elk take the same latitude as a beef steak. Ground is a different matter entirely, but an intact steak from a healthy animal is treated exactly like beef, medium rare and all.

What you need

Meat

  • 1 venison backstrap section, about 1.5 lb (680 g), silverskin removed
  • 2 tsp coarse salt
  • 1 tsp coarsely ground black pepper

Sear and baste

  • 1 tbsp beef tallow or neutral oil
  • 4 tbsp butter
  • 4 sprigs thyme
  • 3 cloves garlic, crushed
  • 6 juniper berries, crushed, optional

Pan sauce

  • 1 large shallot, minced
  • 1/2 cup red wine
  • 1/2 cup beef or game stock
  • 1 tbsp cold butter
  • 1 tsp red wine vinegar

How

  1. Salt the backstrap on all sides and let it sit on a rack, uncovered, in the fridge for at least an hour and up to overnight. Salt needs time to move inward, and the dry surface it leaves behind is what lets you build a crust in one minute later.

  2. Heat the oven to 250°F (120°C) and set the meat on a rack over a sheet pan, still uncovered. Pepper it now.

  3. Roast slowly until a probe in the centre reads about 115 to 118 degrees Fahrenheit (46 to 48°C). The outside will look pale and unappetising and the meat will feel springy rather than soft. Expect 35 to 50 minutes depending on thickness, and go by the probe.

  4. Rest it on the counter while you get a heavy skillet ripping hot with the tallow, to the point where it shimmers and the first smoke lifts. Anything less and the sear takes too long.

  5. Sear the backstrap on every side, turning every 30 seconds or so, until the entire surface is deeply browned and crusted. This should take about two minutes total. Use tongs and stand it on its edges as well.

  6. Add butter, thyme, garlic and juniper in the last thirty seconds, tilt the pan, and spoon the foaming butter over the meat. Then get it out.

  7. Pull at an internal temperature of 130 to 135 degrees Fahrenheit (54 to 57°C) for medium rare, which is the same target you would use for a whole-muscle beef steak and the point at which the meat is deep pink through with a thin brown band at the edge. Rest 10 minutes.

  8. For the sauce, pour off most of the fat, cook the shallot until translucent, deglaze with the wine and reduce it until the pan is nearly dry and the smell has turned from sharp to sweet. Add stock, reduce by half, then swirl in cold butter and the vinegar off the heat.

  9. Slice across the grain, about half an inch thick, and pour the sauce over.

  10. What goes wrong: cooking it to well done out of nerves about wild game. That turns the leanest cut on the animal into sawdust. If you want reassurance, the internal temperature guidance for whole-muscle game is the same as for whole-muscle beef; the higher figure of 160°F (71°C) applies to ground meat, where surface bacteria get mixed throughout.

Notes

  • Tenderloins, which sit inside the body cavity, are smaller and even leaner. Same method, half the oven time, watch them closely.
  • If the animal was not handled well in the field, no cooking method rescues the backstrap. Cook that one as stew and take better care of the next one.

Keeping it

Cooked backstrap keeps three days and is best eaten cold and sliced thin rather than reheated. Raw, vacuum sealed, it holds a year at 0 degrees Fahrenheit (-18°C) without meaningful loss.
