Game & The Freezer

Venison Burgers, With the Fat Put Back

A wild animal has no fat worth eating, so you supply it or you eat a hockey puck.

6 burgers
Makes
30 minutes
Hands on
1 hour
Start to finish

Straight ground venison makes a burger that is dry, crumbly and slightly liver-ish, and no amount of egg or breadcrumb fixes it. The problem is arithmetic: a good beef burger runs somewhere around twenty percent fat, and a deer is closer to two. What you add back matters as much as how much.

Beef fat is the safe answer and pork fat is the better-tasting one. Deer and elk fat is the wrong answer, because it is waxy, it does not render, and it coats your mouth. Trim it off and buy something else to put in.

What you need

The grind

  • 2 lb (900 g) venison, trimmed of all fat and silverskin, in cubes
  • 8 oz (225 g) beef fat trim or pork back fat, in cubes
  • 2 tsp fine sea salt
  • 1 tsp coarsely ground black pepper
  • 1 tsp onion powder
  • 1/2 tsp garlic powder

To cook

  • 2 tbsp beef tallow or neutral oil
  • 6 slices sharp cheddar or Swiss
  • 6 burger buns, split
  • 3 tbsp butter for the buns

On top

  • 1 large onion, sliced thin
  • 6 leaves crisp lettuce
  • 2 tomatoes, sliced
  • 6 tbsp mayonnaise, full fat
  • 3 tbsp yellow mustard

How

  1. Put the meat, the fat, the grinder parts and a bowl in the freezer for 30 minutes. Everything should be firm and near freezing but not solid. Warm fat smears instead of cutting, and smeared fat renders straight out in the pan and leaves you back where you started.

  2. Grind the venison and fat together once through a coarse plate, mixed roughly so the ratio stays even. Twenty percent fat by weight is the target, which is where the eight ounces to two pounds comes from.

  3. Season the ground meat with salt, pepper and the powders and mix with two forks, lightly and briefly. Working ground meat hard develops the proteins into a springy, sausage-like texture that eats wrong in a burger.

  4. Form six loose patties slightly wider than the buns and press a shallow dimple into the centre of each with your thumb. Patties swell in the middle as they cook; the dimple is what stops them turning into domes.

  5. Chill the patties 20 minutes. Cold patties hold their shape and let you build a crust before the middle catches up.

  6. Heat a cast iron skillet or a griddle over medium-high with the tallow until it shimmers and a drop of water skitters. Butter and toast the buns first, until the cut faces are golden, and set them aside.

  7. Cook the patties without moving them until the underside has a solid brown crust and the edges look cooked a third of the way up, then flip once. Add cheese after the flip and cover the pan for a minute so it melts.

  8. Cook ground venison to an internal temperature of 160°F (71°C), checked with a probe pushed in from the side of the patty. This is not the place for medium rare. Grinding spreads whatever was on the outside of the meat all through the batch, which is the whole reason ground meat has a higher target than a steak does.

  9. Rest the patties two minutes on a warm plate while you dress the buns. Building straight out of the pan means the juice runs into the bottom bun and it collapses.

  10. What goes wrong: overworking the mix and pressing the patties in the pan. Pressing squeezes out the fat you deliberately added, which is an expensive way to make a dry burger.

Notes

  • Bacon ends work as the added fat and bring salt and smoke with them. Reduce the added salt if you go that way.
  • If you have the grinder out, do a double batch and freeze the patties between squares of parchment. They cook from frozen over lower heat with a lid on.

Keeping it

Raw patties keep two days in the fridge and three months in the freezer, wrapped tight. Ground game generally holds its quality about four months at 0 degrees Fahrenheit (-18°C), less than whole muscle, because there is far more cut surface exposed.
