Game & The Freezer

Ground Venison Chili

Ground game gets cooked to a number, and this is where that number matters.

Serves 8
Makes
35 minutes
Hands on
2 hours 30 minutes
Start to finish

Ground meat is a different food safety proposition from a steak. Grinding takes whatever was on the outside surfaces and distributes it through the whole batch, so there is no intact interior to rely on. That is why ground venison, elk, beef and everything else gets cooked to 160°F (71°C) all the way through, and why the medium-rare latitude a backstrap enjoys does not transfer.

Chili takes that requirement in stride, since it simmers for hours anyway. What it needs from you is a real browning at the start and toasted whole spices, which is the difference between chili and meat in red sauce.

What you need

Meat

  • 2.5 lb (1.1 kg) ground venison
  • 8 oz (225 g) bacon or pork fat, diced
  • 2 tsp fine sea salt
  • 1 tsp black pepper

Chili base

  • 4 dried ancho chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 dried guajillo chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 cups boiling water
  • 2 onions, diced
  • 5 cloves garlic, minced
  • 1 tbsp ground cumin, toasted
  • 1 tbsp sweet paprika
  • 2 tsp dried oregano
  • 1 tsp ground coriander
  • 3 tbsp tomato paste

Liquid and beans

  • 1 bottle dark beer, 12 oz (355 ml)
  • 1 can crushed tomatoes, 28 oz (800 g)
  • 3 cups beef or game stock
  • 2 cans kidney or pinto beans, 15 oz (425 g) each, drained
  • 2 tbsp masa harina mixed with 1/4 cup water
  • 1 tbsp cider vinegar

How

  1. Toast the dried chiles in a dry skillet over medium heat, pressing them flat, until they smell nutty and turn pliable, about 30 seconds a side. Do not let them blacken; burnt chile is bitter and there is no fixing it.

  2. Cover them with the boiling water and soak until soft, then blend with a cup of the soaking liquid into a smooth paste. This is the actual chili flavour, and it is why the powdered jar version tastes flat.

  3. Render the bacon or pork fat in a heavy pot until the fat has run out. Ground venison has almost no fat of its own, and without added fat it browns into dry pellets.

  4. Brown the venison in batches over high heat, pressing it flat and leaving it to crust before breaking it up. Cooked properly it goes deep brown with crisp edges; stirred constantly it just turns grey and weeps water.

  5. Confirm the meat has reached an internal temperature of 160°F (71°C) with a probe pushed into the thickest part of the layer. Ground meat of any species gets that number and there is no medium-rare version of this dish.

  6. Add onions to the pot and cook until soft, then garlic, cumin, paprika, oregano and coriander, stirring until the spices smell toasted and start to stick.

  7. Stir in the tomato paste and cook it until it darkens, then add the chile paste, the beer, tomatoes and stock. Scrape the bottom clean.

  8. Simmer uncovered, at a lazy bubble, until the chili has darkened, thickened, and a spoon dragged across the bottom leaves a trail that fills in slowly. That is roughly an hour and a half.

  9. Add the beans and cook 20 minutes more so they take on flavour. Stir in the masa slurry and simmer until it thickens and the raw corn flavour has cooked out, about 10 minutes.

  10. Finish with the vinegar off the heat, taste for salt, and let it sit half an hour before serving. Chili served the minute it stops cooking always tastes unfinished.

Notes

  • Ground venison at the butcher is often already mixed with pork or beef fat. If yours is straight venison, the added fat here is doing necessary work, not enriching for the sake of it.
  • Chunked venison instead of ground makes a coarser and arguably better chili. It also then follows braising rules, not ground-meat rules.

Keeping it

Fridge five days, freezer four months. It thickens on standing, so loosen with stock rather than water when reheating.
