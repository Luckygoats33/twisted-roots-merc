Game & The Freezer

Venison Stew for a Wet Week

Dark, thick, and better on the third day than it was on the first.

Serves 8
Makes
45 minutes
Hands on
3 hours 30 minutes
Start to finish

Stew is where the awkward parts of the animal go: the front quarter, the neck, the bits that came off the bone in shapes nobody wants to look at on a plate. Cut into cubes and braised for hours, all of it becomes the same good thing.

The two decisions that separate a great pot from a grey one are browning the meat properly and keeping the pot below a simmer once the lid is on. Everything else is a matter of what is in the vegetable drawer, and stew has never been a recipe so much as a method with suggestions attached.

What you need

Meat

  • 3 lb (1.4 kg) venison stew meat, in 1.5 inch cubes, silverskin removed
  • 2 tsp fine sea salt
  • 1 tsp black pepper
  • 1/3 cup all-purpose flour
  • 4 tbsp beef tallow or neutral oil

Pot

  • 8 oz (225 g) bacon, diced
  • 2 onions, diced
  • 4 cloves garlic, minced
  • 3 tbsp tomato paste
  • 1.5 cups red wine or dark beer
  • 6 cups beef or game stock
  • 2 tbsp Worcestershire sauce
  • 3 sprigs thyme
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 1 tsp smoked paprika

Vegetables, added later

  • 1.5 lb (680 g) waxy potatoes, in chunks
  • 4 carrots, in thick coins
  • 2 parsnips, in chunks
  • 8 oz (225 g) mushrooms, halved
  • 1 cup frozen peas
  • 3 tbsp chopped parsley

How

  1. Render the bacon in a heavy pot until crisp, lift it out, and leave the fat. Season the venison cubes and toss them in flour, shaking off what does not cling.

  2. Brown the meat in batches in the bacon fat and tallow, giving each batch room and leaving it undisturbed until a dark crust forms and it releases on its own. A crowded pot steams, and steamed meat makes grey stew.

  3. Set the meat aside. Cook the onions in the fat until they are soft and picking up colour, then add garlic and tomato paste and stir until the paste darkens and smells sweet.

  4. Deglaze with the wine, scraping every brown speck off the bottom, and reduce it by half.

  5. Return the meat and bacon, add stock, Worcestershire, thyme, bay and paprika. Bring it to a bare simmer, cover, and hold it there over the lowest heat that keeps a bubble breaking every couple of seconds.

  6. After about two hours, test a cube. It should be tender enough to crush against the roof of your mouth but still holding together. If it squeaks or resists, it needs longer, and no amount of stirring will hurry it.

  7. Add potatoes, carrots and parsnips and cook until they are tender to a knife tip with no chalky centre, roughly 30 minutes. Adding them at the start turns them to paste.

  8. Brown the mushrooms separately in a dry hot pan until they have given up their water and squeaked, then browned, and stir them in. Mushrooms added raw to a stew just release water and dilute it.

  9. Stir in the peas at the very end and let them heat through for two minutes so they stay green. Taste for salt, add the parsley, and let the pot sit off the heat for 15 minutes before serving.

  10. What goes wrong: thin stew. If it is watery, lift the meat and vegetables out and reduce the liquid hard until it coats a spoon, then put everything back. Do not add flour at the end; it tastes raw and never quite disappears.

Notes

  • Any whole-muscle game works. The internal temperature rules that matter for ground meat do not apply here, because a long braise takes everything far past any of them.
  • A square of dark chocolate or a spoon of instant coffee stirred in at the end deepens the colour and the flavour without tasting of either.

Keeping it

Fridge five days, and it improves for the first three. Freezes three months, though the potatoes go slightly grainy; leave them out if you are freezing it deliberately.
