All-Butter Pie Crust

Cold butter, cold water, and the discipline to stop mixing while it still looks wrong.

Two 9 inch crusts, enough for one double-crust pie
Makes
20 minutes
Hands on
1 hour 20 minutes with the chill
Start to finish

All-butter crust tastes better than anything else and it is the least forgiving of the fats, because butter is about fifteen percent water and it melts at a temperature your hands comfortably exceed. Everything below is aimed at keeping distinct sheets of solid butter inside the dough right up until they hit the oven, where the water in them flashes to steam and pushes the layers apart.

The dough should look shaggy and underhydrated when you stop working it. If it looks like a smooth ball, you have gone too far, and the crust will come out of the oven tough rather than flaky. Cold and undermixed beats warm and tidy every time.

What you need

Dough

  • 320 g all-purpose flour, about 2.5 cups
  • 1 tbsp granulated sugar
  • 1.5 tsp fine sea salt
  • 255 g unsalted butter, very cold, in 1/2 inch cubes
  • 6 tbsp ice water, plus up to 2 tbsp more
  • 1 tbsp cider vinegar, cold

For finishing a pie

  • 1 large egg beaten with 1 tbsp cream, for the wash
  • 2 tbsp coarse sugar

How

  1. Cube the butter and put it back in the freezer for 15 minutes. Mix the ice water and vinegar and keep them in the fridge. The vinegar slightly inhibits gluten and makes the dough more forgiving of handling.

  2. Whisk flour, sugar and salt in a wide bowl. Add the butter and toss it so every cube is coated.

  3. Flatten each cube between your thumb and forefinger into a rough sheet, working quickly. You want flat shards, not peas and not crumbs. Those sheets become the layers.

  4. Sprinkle in the ice water and vinegar a tablespoon at a time, tossing with a fork after each. Stop as soon as the mixture holds together when you squeeze a handful, even though the bowl still looks like rubble.

  5. Tip it onto the counter and push the pile away from you a few times with the heel of your hand, folding it back over itself. Six or seven pushes, no more. This is what makes the layers long rather than random.

  6. Divide, press into two flat discs rather than balls, wrap and refrigerate at least an hour. A disc chills evenly and rolls out without cracking; a ball does neither.

  7. Roll on a lightly floured counter from the centre outward, turning the disc a quarter turn after every few passes so it does not stick and stays round. If the butter starts to smear or the dough goes slack, stop and put it back in the fridge for ten minutes.

  8. Ease the dough into the pan rather than stretching it. Stretched dough shrinks back in the oven and you get a crust that has slumped halfway down the sides.

  9. Chill the shaped shell for at least 30 minutes before baking, and freeze it if you have time. Cold dough into a hot oven is the whole game.

  10. Bake in a hot oven, generally 400 to 425°F (205 to 220°C) to start, until the crust is set and pale gold at the edges before you drop the temperature. If you are checking a filled pie for doneness, an instant-read thermometer pushed into the centre of the filling should read close to 200°F (93°C), which is where fruit starches finish thickening.

  11. What goes wrong: warm butter. If your kitchen is above about 70 degrees Fahrenheit (21°C), work faster, chill between every step, and consider running the food processor for the first stage rather than your hands.

Notes

  • Weigh the flour. A cup of flour can vary by thirty grams depending on how it was scooped, and that is the difference between a supple dough and a dry one.
  • The dough freezes for three months as discs. Thaw overnight in the fridge, never on the counter.

Keeping it

Wrapped discs keep three days in the fridge. A rolled and fitted shell keeps two days covered in the fridge and freezes well in the pan, wrapped twice.
