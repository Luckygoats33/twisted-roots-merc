Berry Syrup

For pancakes, soda water and the bottom of a glass of something stronger.

About 4 cups
Makes
30 minutes
Hands on
4 hours including the drip
Start to finish

Syrup is the useful thing to make from berries that are too soft for pie and too far gone for jam. It is fruit juice and sugar cooked briefly, which means the flavour stays bright rather than going jammy, and it is the only preserve on this list that improves a glass of soda water.

Written here as a refrigerator and freezer preserve, deliberately. Tested water bath processes for fruit syrups do exist and they work, but they belong to a tested recipe with its own ratios and times, not to this one.

What you need

Juice

  • 10 cups berries, about 1.4 kg, any combination
  • 1 cup water
  • 2 tbsp bottled lemon juice

Syrup

  • 4 cups extracted berry juice, roughly
  • 3 cups granulated sugar, adjusted to taste
  • 2 tbsp bottled lemon juice
  • 1 pinch fine sea salt
  • 1 vanilla bean or 1 tsp vanilla extract, optional

Equipment

  • 1 jelly bag or 4 layers of cheesecloth in a sieve
  • 4 clean bottles or jars with tight lids
  • 1 candy thermometer
  • 2 freezer-safe containers if freezing

How

  1. Put the berries and water in a wide pot and heat gently, crushing with a masher as they go, until the fruit has completely collapsed and the juice runs freely, about 10 minutes. Do not boil it hard; hard boiling extracts bitterness from seeds.

  2. Add the first measure of lemon juice, take it off the heat, and let it stand 10 minutes.

  3. Pour everything into a jelly bag or a cheesecloth-lined sieve set over a bowl and let it drip for at least 2 hours, or overnight in the fridge.

  4. Do not squeeze the bag. Squeezing forces fine pulp through and gives you a cloudy syrup. If you want cloudy syrup with more body, squeeze on purpose and own the decision.

  5. Measure the juice you got and combine it with the sugar in a clean pot, in roughly a four to three ratio by volume, along with the second lemon measure and the salt.

  6. Heat gently, stirring, until every grain of sugar has dissolved and the liquid runs clear off a spoon rather than looking grainy.

  7. Bring it to a gentle boil and cook 5 minutes, until it has thickened just enough to coat the back of a spoon and leave a brief track when you drag a finger through it. It should stay pourable; syrup thickens noticeably as it cools.

  8. Do not take this to the jam gel stage. A thermometer reading 220°F (105°C) means you have made jelly, not syrup, and there is no way back short of adding water and starting over.

  9. Skim any foam, add the vanilla off the heat, and pour into clean bottles while hot, leaving headspace if any are going to the freezer.

  10. Cool, lid, and refrigerate. This is a refrigerator preserve; it has not been processed and it is not shelf stable, so it does not belong in a cupboard.

  11. If you want shelf-stable syrup, use a tested water bath recipe from the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning or from OSU Extension, with its stated ratios, jar size and processing time. Times change with altitude, and while Siletz is near sea level, plenty of readers are not.

Notes

  • The drained pulp is not waste. Push it through a food mill and it becomes a rough fruit butter, or spread it thin and dry it into fruit leather.
  • Marionberry, blackberry, raspberry and huckleberry all work. Blue-toned berries make the best colour and strawberry makes the worst.

Keeping it

Refrigerated in a sealed bottle, about a month. Frozen in containers with headspace, a year. It has not been canned and it is not safe at room temperature.
