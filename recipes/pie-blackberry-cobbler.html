Pie, Cobbler & Preserves

Blackberry Cobbler

For the berries growing over your fence, which are free and endless and slightly aggressive.

One 9 by 13 inch pan, serves 10
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
1 hour 15 minutes
Start to finish

Himalayan blackberry is a noxious weed in this state and a genuinely excellent pie fruit, which is an awkward combination if you own land. Cobbler is what you make when you have picked far more than any pie could hold and your forearms are scratched to pieces.

Biscuit topping rather than batter, dropped in rough spoonfuls, because that gives you crisp browned tops, soft undersides where they meet the fruit, and gaps for steam to escape. A batter poured over the whole surface seals the pan and steams the fruit into soup.

What you need

Fruit

  • 8 cups blackberries, about 1.1 kg
  • 3/4 cup granulated sugar
  • 3 tbsp cornstarch
  • 2 tbsp lemon juice
  • 1/4 tsp fine sea salt
  • 1/2 tsp ground cinnamon, optional

Biscuit topping

  • 280 g all-purpose flour, about 2.25 cups
  • 1/4 cup granulated sugar
  • 1 tbsp baking powder
  • 1/2 tsp baking soda
  • 3/4 tsp fine sea salt
  • 150 g cold butter, in cubes
  • 1 cup cold buttermilk
  • 2 tbsp heavy cream for brushing
  • 3 tbsp coarse sugar

How

  1. Heat the oven to 375°F (190°C) and butter a 9 by 13 inch dish. Set it on a rimmed sheet pan, because cobbler bubbles over and cleaning an oven floor is a poor use of an evening.

  2. Toss the berries with sugar, cornstarch, lemon and salt and spread them in the dish. Let it sit while you make the topping so the starch hydrates.

  3. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, soda and salt. Cut in the cold butter with your fingertips until the largest pieces are the size of a small bean and the rest is coarse and sandy.

  4. Pour in the buttermilk and stir with a fork just until a shaggy dough forms with dry patches still visible. Overmixed biscuit dough goes tough and dense, and this one has nowhere to hide.

  5. Drop the dough over the fruit in rough spoonfuls, leaving real gaps between them. The gaps are functional. They let steam out and let the fruit reduce instead of boil.

  6. Brush the tops with cream and scatter the coarse sugar heavily. Do not brush the fruit.

  7. Bake until the biscuits are deep golden and firm, the fruit is bubbling thickly around the edges and up through the gaps, and a skewer pushed into the middle of the largest biscuit comes out clean with no wet dough on it.

  8. If the tops are browning before the biscuits are cooked through, tent with foil and keep going. An underbaked biscuit bottom sitting in hot fruit turns to paste, and that is the usual failure here.

  9. An instant-read thermometer in the fruit should be near 200°F (93°C), which is where the cornstarch has fully thickened. Below that the filling stays runny even after it cools.

  10. Cool at least 30 minutes before serving. It will still be warm and it will have gone from soup to something that holds its shape in a bowl.

Notes

  • Wild blackberries are seedier and sharper than cultivated ones. Add a little more sugar and expect a more assertive pie.
  • This is the correct place for vanilla ice cream, and it is not a garnish.

Keeping it

Covered at room temperature two days, or in the fridge four. Reheat portions in a 325°F (165°C) oven until the fruit bubbles again and the biscuit crisps.
