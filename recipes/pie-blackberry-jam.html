Blackberry Jam, Seedless or Not

The weed in your fence line, in jars, and the seeds are optional.

About 7 half-pint jars
Makes
1 hour 15 minutes
Hands on
About 3 hours
Start to finish

Wild blackberry makes a sharper, more assertive jam than marionberry and it has a lot more seed in it. Whether you strain those seeds out is a matter of household politics; straining costs you about a third of the yield and all of the texture, and produces something closer to a jelly.

Blackberry is high-acid fruit, which is why a boiling water bath is the correct process for it. Low-acid foods such as plain vegetables, meat, fish and soup are not water bath safe under any circumstances and need a pressure canner. That line never moves.

What you need

Jam

  • 10 cups blackberries, about 1.4 kg
  • 6 cups granulated sugar
  • 1/4 cup bottled lemon juice
  • 1 tbsp butter, optional, for foam

If going seedless

  • 1 food mill fitted with a fine screen, or 1 fine sieve
  • 1 sturdy spoon or ladle for pressing
  • 2 cups extra berries to make up the lost volume

Equipment

  • 7 half-pint jars with new lids
  • 1 boiling water canner with a rack
  • 1 jam thermometer
  • 3 plates in the freezer
  • 1 copy of the current USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning, or OSU Extension on the phone

How

  1. Look up the tested processing time for berry jam in your jar size before you start, from the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning or your Extension office. The figure depends on jar size and altitude and is not printed here on purpose.

  2. Adjust for where you are. Near sea level, which includes Siletz, the base figures apply. Higher up, water boils cooler and the tested altitude table tells you how much time to add. Look up your own elevation.

  3. If going seedless, cook the berries gently with a splash of water until they collapse and the juice runs freely, then pass everything through a food mill or press it through a sieve. Expect to lose roughly a third of the volume to seed and pulp.

  4. Measure what you have after straining and adjust the sugar to roughly three parts fruit to two parts sugar by volume. Adding the full sugar to a reduced quantity of fruit gives you candy.

  5. Combine fruit, sugar and bottled lemon juice in a wide heavy pot over low heat and stir until the sugar is completely dissolved and the mixture feels smooth rather than gritty on the back of a spoon.

  6. Bring it to a full rolling boil that cannot be stirred down, stirring constantly and scraping the bottom corners of the pot where jam catches first.

  7. Boil until it reaches the gel stage, which reads 220°F (105°C) on a thermometer at sea level or about 8°C above whatever the boiling point is where you are.

  8. Test on a frozen plate. A spoonful left for 30 seconds should wrinkle when you push it and hold the track your finger makes. If it floods back, keep boiling and test again in two minutes. Pull the pot off the heat while testing.

  9. Fill hot jars to a quarter inch of headspace, remove bubbles, wipe the rims until they squeak, and fit lids and rings finger tight.

  10. Process in the boiling water canner for the tested time you looked up, with the jars covered by at least an inch of water, timed from the return to a full boil. Rest five minutes off the heat, then lift the jars out onto a towel.

  11. Leave them alone overnight, then check every lid. A sealed lid is dished and rigid. Refrigerate anything that did not seal.

  12. What goes wrong: overcooking. Blackberry jam pushed well past the gel stage turns dark, stiff and slightly bitter, and there is no undoing it. Start testing the moment the thermometer approaches the target.

Notes

  • Half seedless and half whole, combined at the end, gives you jam with body and without the dental experience. It is the version most people actually want.
  • Never call a home canning recipe safe because it worked once. Follow tested processes, and dispose of any jar that is bulging, spurting or smells wrong without tasting it.

Keeping it

Sealed jars, rings off, a year or more in a cool dark place. Opened jars live in the fridge and are good for about a month.
