Pie, Cobbler & Preserves

Blind Baking, Properly

The fix for a soggy bottom, and the reason your custard pie had a raw seam.

One 9 inch prebaked shell
Makes
15 minutes
Hands on
1 hour 15 minutes
Start to finish

Blind baking is baking a crust before, or entirely without, its filling. You do it whenever the filling will not be in the oven long enough to cook the pastry underneath it, which covers every cream pie, every custard, and any fruit pie with a very wet filling.

The two problems it solves are slumping walls and a raw base. The two problems it creates, if done carelessly, are a crust that shrinks down the sides and a base that puffs into a dome. Weight and cold dough solve both, and there is a specific order of operations that matters more than any single trick.

What you need

What you need

  • 1 batch pie dough, one disc, chilled
  • 2 lb (900 g) pie weights, dried beans or rice
  • 1 large sheet of parchment or heavy foil
  • 1 egg white, beaten, for sealing
  • 2 tbsp flour for the counter

How

  1. Roll the dough a good two inches wider than the pan so there is enough to come up the sides and over the rim without stretching. Stretched dough is dough that will shrink.

  2. Ease it into the pan and press it firmly into the corner where the base meets the wall. Air trapped in that corner is exactly where a shell collapses.

  3. Trim, leaving an overhang, then fold it under and crimp. Leaving the rim slightly thicker gives you insurance against shrinkage.

  4. Freeze the shell solid, at least 30 minutes and ideally an hour. This matters more than the weights. Frozen fat does not start moving until the structure of the crust has already set.

  5. Line the frozen shell with parchment, pushing it right into the corner, and fill it to the very top with weights. Half-filling the shell leaves the upper walls unsupported and they will slump inward.

  6. Bake at 400°F (205°C) until the edges are set and beginning to colour and the surface under the parchment looks dry and matte rather than damp. Lift a corner to check.

  7. Remove the parchment and weights and return the shell to the oven. Bake until the base is dry, firm and a light gold, with no translucent patches left. This is the step people cut short, and it is why the bottom is pale.

  8. For a fully baked shell, keep going to a deep even gold. For a partially baked shell that will go back in with a filling, stop at pale gold.

  9. Brush the hot base with beaten egg white and return it for two minutes so it sets into a thin waterproof seal. This is the single best defence against a wet filling soaking in, and it costs nothing.

  10. If the base has puffed anyway, press it flat with the back of a spoon while it is still hot and soft. It will stay where you put it.

  11. For a custard filling going into this shell, remember it is an egg product with its own doneness point. Bake a custard until the edges are set and the centre wobbles as one mass rather than rippling, which lands around an internal temperature of 175 to 180°F (79 to 82°C). Past that and it curdles and weeps.

Notes

  • Rice and dried beans work as weights forever, and they can go back in the jar and be reused indefinitely. They will no longer cook, so keep them labelled for the purpose.
  • A second pie pan nested inside the shell also works, though it browns the walls less evenly than loose weights do.

Keeping it

A baked shell keeps two days at room temperature loosely covered, and freezes for a month baked. Do not refrigerate a baked shell uncovered; it goes soft from the humidity.
