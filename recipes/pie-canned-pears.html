Pie, Cobbler & Preserves

Canned Pears in Light Syrup

High-acid fruit, a boiling water bath, and a processing time you go and look up.

About 7 quart jars from a half bushel
Makes
1 hour 30 minutes
Hands on
About 4 hours
Start to finish

Pears are the one fruit worth canning even if you never can anything else, because a good canned pear is closer to a fresh ripe one than any other preserved fruit gets. They are also high-acid, which is why a boiling water bath is the right and safe process for them. Low-acid foods, meaning plain vegetables, meats, fish and soups, are never water bath safe and require a pressure canner.

The trick with pears is ripeness. They ripen off the tree, from the inside out, and a pear canned rock hard stays woody in the jar forever. Let them come to the point where the flesh at the neck gives to a thumb.

What you need

Fruit

  • 20 lb (9 kg) firm-ripe pears, Bartlett or similar
  • 1 gallon water with 3 tbsp bottled lemon juice, as a holding solution
  • 1 tsp ascorbic acid powder, optional, for colour

Light syrup

  • 9 cups water
  • 2.25 cups granulated sugar
  • 1 stick cinnamon per jar, optional
  • 2 whole cloves per jar, optional

Equipment

  • 7 quart jars with new lids and sound rings
  • 1 boiling water canner with a rack, deep enough to cover quarts by an inch
  • 1 jar lifter, 1 wide funnel, 1 bubble tool
  • 1 melon baller for coring
  • 1 copy of the current USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning, or OSU Extension

How

  1. Look up the tested processing time for pears in your jar size, hot packed, in the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning or with OSU Extension. Quarts and pints differ, and altitude changes it again. This page does not print a number for you to apply to the wrong jar.

  2. Find your altitude and use the line that matches. Siletz is near sea level, so the sea level figures apply here. Anyone inland or up a hill needs more time, and the tested table says exactly how much.

  3. Ripen the pears at room temperature until the flesh just below the stem yields to gentle thumb pressure. Underripe pears go woody and stay that way; overripe ones fall apart into the syrup.

  4. Peel, halve and core them with a melon baller, and drop each half straight into the lemon water. Cut pear browns within a minute of meeting air.

  5. Make the syrup by heating the water and sugar together until fully dissolved and clear, then keep it hot. A light syrup lets the pear taste like a pear; heavier syrups hold shape and colour slightly better and taste like dessert.

  6. Hot pack the fruit, which is what the tested times above assume. Simmer the drained pear halves in the syrup for 5 minutes, until they are heated through and look translucent at the edges but still hold their shape on a spoon.

  7. Hot packing matters. Fruit going into the jar already hot drives out air and shortens the time the centre of the jar needs to reach process temperature. You are not probing for an internal temperature here; the tested process time is what does the work.

  8. Pack the hot halves into hot jars, cavity side down and overlapping like roof tiles, and ladle hot syrup over to leave half an inch of headspace.

  9. Run the bubble tool down the inside of each jar and adjust the syrup level after the air comes out. Wipe every rim until it squeaks and fit lids and rings finger tight.

  10. Lower the jars onto the rack in the canner with water already hot, add water to cover them by at least an inch, and bring it to a full rolling boil.

  11. Start timing only when it is at a full boil, and process for the tested time you looked up. Keep the water boiling the entire time; if it drops off the boil, bring it back and start the timing again from the beginning.

  12. Turn off the heat, wait 5 minutes with the lid off, then lift jars straight up onto a towel with space between them and leave them undisturbed overnight.

  13. Check the seals the next day. A sealed lid is concave and does not move when pressed. Refrigerate anything unsealed and eat it within a few days.

  14. Never taste from a jar that is bulging, that spurts when opened, that smells fermented, or whose contents look wrong. Dispose of it following the instructions in the USDA guide.

Notes

  • Nobody at this store has canned your particular batch. Follow tested processes from a current source, because home canning is the one place in cooking where improvising has consequences you cannot taste coming.
  • Very ripe pears are better made into pear butter or sauce than canned as halves. They will not hold their shape.

Keeping it

Sealed jars with the rings removed keep well over a year in a cool dark cupboard, with best quality in the first year. Once opened, refrigerate and use within about a week.
