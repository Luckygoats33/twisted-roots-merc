Freezer Jam

No canner, no boiling, and it tastes more like the fruit than anything you cook.

About 6 cups
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
24 hours mostly waiting
Start to finish

Freezer jam is uncooked or barely cooked fruit set with pectin and stored cold. Because the fruit never boils, the colour stays bright and the flavour stays raw and immediate in a way that no cooked jam manages. It is the closest thing to eating the berry in the middle of winter.

To be completely clear about what this is: it is not canned and it is not shelf stable. It lives in the freezer, and once opened it lives in the fridge, and it is not safe to store at room temperature. That is a feature rather than a limitation, since it means no canner, no processing and no altitude tables.

What you need

Jam

  • 4 cups crushed berries, about 560 g
  • 4 cups granulated sugar, or the amount your pectin specifies
  • 1 packet powdered pectin labelled for freezer jam, or 3 tbsp instant pectin
  • 2 tbsp bottled lemon juice
  • 3/4 cup water, if your pectin calls for it

Containers

  • 6 freezer-safe jars or plastic containers with tight lids
  • 1 permanent marker for labelling
  • 2 clean towels

How

  1. Read your pectin package first. Freezer jam pectins differ: some are stirred straight into sugar and fruit cold, some need a brief boil with water. Follow the one you bought rather than a generic ratio.

  2. Crush the berries with a potato masher rather than blending them. A blender aerates the jam and turns it pink and foamy, and it pulverises the seeds.

  3. Stir the sugar into the crushed fruit and let it stand 10 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the sugar has completely dissolved and the mixture looks glossy instead of grainy.

  4. If your pectin needs cooking, bring it to a boil with the water and boil for one minute exactly, stirring, then pour it into the fruit. Boiling longer breaks the pectin down and it will not set at all.

  5. Stir the fruit and pectin together for three full minutes by the clock. This is the step people cut short and it is what distributes the pectin. Under-stirred freezer jam sets in patches.

  6. Add the bottled lemon juice and stir it through. It brightens the flavour and helps the set.

  7. Ladle into containers, leaving a good half inch of headspace, because the jam expands as it freezes and a full jar will push its lid off or crack.

  8. There is no gel stage here and no internal temperature to hit. A cooked jam is judged at 220°F (105°C) on a thermometer; this one sets chemically at room temperature and a thermometer tells you nothing useful about it.

  9. Leave the containers on the counter, lids on, for 24 hours to set. It will look far too loose at the start; freezer pectin takes most of a day to firm up and there is no way to hurry it.

  10. Label every container and move them to the freezer, keeping one in the fridge to start on.

  11. If it has not set after a day, it will not. Use it as a syrup on pancakes or ice cream, which is a perfectly good outcome, or reprocess it with a fresh batch of pectin per the package instructions.

Notes

  • This is a refrigerator and freezer preserve. It has not been processed, it is not shelf stable, and it must not be stored in a cupboard.
  • Any fruit works, but very low-acid fruit sets poorly. Berries, stone fruit and rhubarb are all reliable.

Keeping it

In the freezer, up to a year with good colour and flavour. In the fridge, about three weeks once thawed or opened. Never at room temperature.
