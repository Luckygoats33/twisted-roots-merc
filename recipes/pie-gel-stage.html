The Gel Stage, and How to Test For It

Jam does not set because it never got there. That is nearly always the answer.

One reliable test you can trust
Makes
10 minutes of reading
Hands on
Saves you a batch
Start to finish

A jam sets because three things line up: pectin, acid and sugar concentration. The first two you supply at the start, and the third only arrives once enough water has boiled away. The gel stage is simply the moment the sugar concentration is high enough for the pectin to build a network, and it happens at a specific temperature because boiling point rises as syrup concentrates.

So when jam does not set, the usual reason is not bad pectin or bad luck. It is that the pot came off the heat too early. Here are three tests, and the reasons to use more than one.

What you need

What you need to test

  • 1 candy or jam thermometer that you have checked in boiling water
  • 3 small plates in the freezer
  • 1 cold metal spoon
  • 1 pot of jam at a full rolling boil
  • 1 timer for the intervals between tests

How

  1. Calibrate first. Put your thermometer in plain boiling water and read it. At sea level, which includes Siletz, water boils at 212 degrees Fahrenheit (100°C), and the gel stage sits about 8°C above that, which is 220°F (105°C).

  2. Adjust for altitude by measuring, not by arithmetic. Whatever your water boiled at, the gel stage is roughly 8°C above that number. This is why a fixed target temperature copied from a recipe fails for people up a hill.

  3. Test one, the thermometer. Keep the bulb in the jam and off the bottom of the pot. It is the fastest test and it is only as good as your calibration and your patience, since jam sits near the target for a while before crossing it.

  4. Test two, the frozen plate, which is the one to trust. Take the pot off the heat, put a small spoonful on a plate from the freezer, and wait 30 seconds. Push it with a fingertip. Set jam wrinkles ahead of your finger and holds the track. Unset jam floods back into the gap.

  5. Take the pot off the heat while you test. Thirty seconds of hard boiling while you are looking at a plate is enough to overshoot, and overshot jam is stiff and dull.

  6. Test three, the sheet test. Dip a cold metal spoon and hold it sideways over the pot. Loose syrup runs off in drops. Jam approaching the gel stage runs off in two streams that merge and fall together as a sheet off the edge of the spoon.

  7. Use at least two of the three. The thermometer tells you when to start testing; the plate tells you when to stop cooking.

  8. Know what else affects it. Underripe fruit has more pectin than ripe fruit. Too little acid and the network cannot form, which is why lemon juice is in nearly every jam recipe. Too much sugar and it sets hard and candied; too little and it may never set at all without a low-sugar pectin.

  9. Understand that added pectin changes the rules. Liquid and powdered pectins set on a timed boil rather than at a temperature, and pushing them to 220°F (105°C) breaks them down and guarantees the opposite of what you want. Follow the packet.

  10. If it did not set, you can fix it. Pour it back into a pot, bring it to a rolling boil, and cook it to the gel stage properly. If you are canning it, that means clean jars, new lids and processing again for the tested time from a current source such as the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning or OSU Extension.

  11. And if it still will not set, stop. Call it syrup, put it on pancakes and ice cream, and move on. Every person who preserves has a shelf of jars quietly relabelled in their head.

Notes

  • Jam continues to firm for up to two days in the jar. Do not judge a batch on the first night and re-boil something that would have been fine.
  • A digital probe thermometer is more accurate than a clip-on candy thermometer and far easier to keep off the bottom of the pot.

Keeping it

Keep the frozen plates in the freezer permanently through berry season. It is the cheapest piece of equipment in the kitchen and the one that saves the most batches.
