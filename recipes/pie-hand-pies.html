Pie, Cobbler & Preserves

Hand Pies

Pie you can eat in a truck, which is the entire design brief.

12 hand pies
Makes
50 minutes
Hands on
2 hours 30 minutes
Start to finish

A hand pie has one job that a whole pie does not: it has to survive being held. That changes the filling entirely. Anything loose enough to be good on a plate will run out of a hand pie down your wrist, so the filling gets cooked down first until it is more like a thick jam than a pie filling.

The other adjustment is sealing. A hand pie has a long seam relative to its size and every inch of it is a chance to leak. Cold dough, a dry rim, a firm crimp, and a vent in the top so the steam has somewhere to go other than out the side.

What you need

Filling

  • 4 cups berries, about 550 g, or the same weight of diced apple
  • 2/3 cup granulated sugar
  • 3 tbsp cornstarch mixed with 3 tbsp water
  • 1 tbsp lemon juice
  • 1/4 tsp fine sea salt
  • 1 tbsp butter

Pastry

  • 1 double batch pie dough, four discs, chilled
  • 2 tbsp flour for the counter
  • 1 large egg beaten with 1 tbsp cream
  • 3 tbsp coarse sugar

Optional glaze

  • 1 cup powdered sugar
  • 2 tbsp milk
  • 1/2 tsp vanilla extract

How

  1. Cook the fruit with the sugar, lemon and salt over medium heat until it collapses and releases its juice, then keep going until the volume has dropped by about a third and it smells cooked rather than fresh.

  2. Stir in the cornstarch slurry and cook until the mixture turns glossy and thick enough that a spoon dragged through the pan leaves a track that stays open for a second. Stir in the butter off the heat.

  3. Cool the filling completely, ideally overnight in the fridge. Warm filling melts pastry and a warm hand pie assembled in a hurry will leak.

  4. Roll the dough about an eighth of an inch thick and cut twelve pairs of shapes: rounds about five inches across, or rectangles. Chill the cut shapes on a lined tray for 15 minutes.

  5. Put a heaped tablespoon of filling on the centre of each base, leaving a clear border of at least three quarters of an inch. Overfilling is the leading cause of blowouts and everyone does it the first time.

  6. Brush the border with water or egg wash, lay the top on, and press the air out from the filling toward the edge before you seal. Trapped air expands and splits the seam.

  7. Crimp the edge firmly with a fork, pressing right through both layers, and cut two short vents in the top of each.

  8. Freeze the assembled pies on the tray for 20 minutes. This firms the fat and sets the shape so they do not slump into ovals.

  9. Brush with egg wash and scatter coarse sugar. Bake at 400°F (205°C) until the pastry is deep golden brown all over, the bottoms are firm and browned when you lift one, and any escaping filling has bubbled and caramelised rather than run thin.

  10. Cool on a rack at least 20 minutes. An instant-read thermometer pushed into the filling of one straight from the oven will read near 200°F (93°C), which is to say the filling inside is far hotter than the outside suggests and it has burned more than one impatient person.

  11. If glazing, whisk the powdered sugar, milk and vanilla and drizzle it over the fully cooled pies. Glazing warm pies just makes a transparent sticky film.

  12. What goes wrong: a loose filling. If your first batch leaked, cook the next filling down further. It should mound on a spoon and hold its shape.

Notes

  • Marionberry jam straight from the jar is a legitimate shortcut filling and needs no cooking down at all.
  • These freeze unbaked and bake from frozen, adding about ten minutes, which makes them the most useful thing in this section.

Keeping it

Two days at room temperature in a tin, five in the fridge, three months frozen. Reheat in a 350°F (175°C) oven for a few minutes to bring the pastry back.
