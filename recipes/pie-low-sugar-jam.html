Low-Sugar Jam, and Why It Behaves Differently

Sugar is not only sweetness. Take it out and you have to replace what it was doing.

About 5 half-pint jars
Makes
45 minutes
Hands on
2 hours 30 minutes
Start to finish

Standard jam is roughly equal parts fruit and sugar by weight, and that sugar is doing three jobs. It binds water so microbes cannot use it, it is a structural partner to pectin in forming a gel, and it tastes sweet. Cut it in half and the gel gets weaker, the keeping quality drops, and the jam tastes much more strongly of the fruit, which is the reason people want it.

The replacement is a different pectin. Low-methoxyl pectin sets with calcium rather than with sugar and acid, which is why it works with almost no sugar at all. It is a different product with different instructions, and this is one of the places where following the insert in the box matters more than following any recipe page.

What you need

Jam

  • 8 cups prepared fruit, about 1.1 kg, crushed
  • 1 to 1.5 cups granulated sugar, or honey, to taste
  • 3 tbsp low-methoxyl pectin, the kind sold for low or no sugar recipes
  • 1 packet calcium powder as supplied with that pectin, mixed per its instructions
  • 1/4 cup bottled lemon juice

Equipment

  • 5 half-pint jars with new lids
  • 1 boiling water canner with a rack
  • 1 jam thermometer
  • 2 plates in the freezer
  • 1 copy of the pectin manufacturer's current instructions
  • 1 copy of the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning, or OSU Extension

How

  1. Start with the tested recipe that came with your pectin. Low-sugar jams are the one category where the manufacturer's insert outranks a general recipe, because the ratios are specific to that product and the safety of the finished jam depends on them.

  2. Then look up the processing time separately in the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning or with OSU Extension. It varies by jar size and by altitude, and this page prints no number for you to copy.

  3. Remember what changes. Less sugar means less water is bound up, which means a shorter shelf life once opened and a softer set. Fruit is still high acid, so a boiling water bath is still the right process; the sugar is not what made it water bath safe in the first place.

  4. Prepare the calcium water as the packet directs and set it aside. This is the ingredient that actually causes the set in a low-methoxyl pectin, and skipping it gives you fruit sauce.

  5. Crush the fruit in a wide pot, add the lemon juice and the calcium water, and heat until it is steaming and the fruit has slumped.

  6. Mix the pectin powder thoroughly into the sugar before it goes anywhere near the pot. Pectin dropped into hot liquid on its own clumps instantly into rubbery lumps that never dissolve.

  7. Stir the sugar and pectin mixture in and bring it to a full rolling boil that cannot be stirred down. Boil hard for one minute, or for whatever your pectin's instructions say.

  8. Do not judge this by the standard gel stage. A conventional jam is done at 220°F (105°C) because that temperature corresponds to the right sugar concentration; a low-sugar jam will never reach it and does not need to. The set comes from calcium, not from boiling down.

  9. Test on a frozen plate anyway. A spoonful should firm up within a minute and hold a shape when pushed. Low-sugar jams often look alarmingly loose in the pot and set properly in the jar overnight.

  10. Fill hot jars to a quarter inch headspace, debubble, wipe the rims until they squeak, fit lids finger tight, and process for the tested time you looked up, timed from a full boil.

  11. Cool overnight undisturbed and check the seals. Any jar that did not seal goes in the fridge.

  12. If you would rather not can at all, this recipe is a perfectly good refrigerator jam. Skip the processing entirely, cool it, and keep the jars in the fridge for about three weeks, or in the freezer for a year.

Notes

  • Regular pectin and low-methoxyl pectin are not interchangeable. Using regular pectin with half the sugar produces syrup, every time.
  • Do not reduce the sugar in a recipe written for regular pectin and expect it to be safe or to set. Use a recipe designed for the amount of sugar you actually want to use.

Keeping it

Sealed and processed, a year in a cool cupboard, though the colour fades faster than a full-sugar jam. Once opened, about two to three weeks in the fridge rather than a month, because there is less sugar holding the water.
