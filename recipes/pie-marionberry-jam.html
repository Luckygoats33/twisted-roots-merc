Pie, Cobbler & Preserves

Marionberry Jam

High-acid fruit, so a boiling water bath is the right process, and the times come from a tested source.

About 6 half-pint jars
Makes
1 hour
Hands on
About 3 hours including the process and cooling
Start to finish

Marionberries make the best jam of any fruit in this valley, and they are also naturally high in acid, which is what makes them safe to process in a boiling water bath. That is the key distinction in all home preserving. High-acid foods, meaning fruits, jams, jellies and anything pickled with added vinegar, can be safely processed in boiling water. Low-acid foods, meaning plain vegetables, meat, fish and soups, cannot, and need a pressure canner.

This page gives the jam. It deliberately does not print a processing time, because that figure depends on your jar size and your altitude, and printing a number for someone to misapply is worse than sending them to look it up.

What you need

Jam

  • 8 cups marionberries, about 1.1 kg, crushed
  • 6 cups granulated sugar
  • 1/4 cup bottled lemon juice
  • 1 tbsp butter, optional, to reduce foaming

Equipment

  • 1 wide heavy-bottomed pot, at least 8 quarts
  • 6 half-pint jars with new lids and sound rings
  • 1 boiling water canner with a rack, or a deep stockpot with a rack
  • 1 candy or jam thermometer
  • 3 small plates in the freezer for testing
  • 1 copy of the current USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning, or your OSU Extension office

How

  1. Get the tested processing time first. Look up the current figure for berry jam in your jar size in the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning, or call OSU Extension. Time varies by jar size and by altitude, and this recipe will not guess it for you.

  2. Note where you are. Siletz sits close to sea level, so the sea level figures apply here. Water boils cooler as you go up, so anyone canning at elevation adds time according to the tested altitude table. Look up your own altitude rather than assuming somebody else's applies.

  3. Use bottled lemon juice, not fresh. Bottled juice has a standardised acidity; fresh lemons vary, and the acid here is doing a safety job as well as a setting one.

  4. Wash the jars and keep them hot. Bring the canner water up with the rack in it. Have the lids clean and at hand.

  5. Crush the berries in the pot with a potato masher, leaving some texture. Add the sugar and lemon juice and stir over low heat until every grain of sugar has dissolved and the mixture no longer feels gritty on a spoon. Undissolved sugar crystallises in the jar.

  6. Raise the heat and bring it to a full rolling boil, meaning a boil that does not stop when you stir it. Stir constantly and scrape the bottom, because jam scorches the moment you turn away.

  7. Boil hard until it reaches the gel stage. On a thermometer that is 220°F (105°C) at sea level, or roughly 8°C above the boiling point of water where you are. Skim the foam if you did not use the butter.

  8. Confirm with a cold plate. Put a spoonful on a frozen plate, wait 30 seconds, and push it with a fingertip. Set jam wrinkles ahead of your finger and holds a line. Loose jam floods back. Take the pot off the heat while you test or you will overshoot.

  9. Ladle the hot jam into hot jars through a wide funnel, leaving a quarter inch of headspace. Slide a bubble tool around the inside, wipe every rim until it squeaks, and fit lids and rings finger tight.

  10. Lower the jars into the canner on the rack, with water covering them by at least an inch, and process for the tested time you looked up, timed from the moment the water returns to a full boil.

  11. Turn off the heat, wait five minutes, then lift the jars straight up and set them on a towel with space between them. Leave them undisturbed overnight.

  12. Check the seals the next day. A sealed lid is concave and does not flex when pressed. Anything unsealed goes in the fridge and gets eaten within a month, not reprocessed on a whim.

  13. What goes wrong: jam that never sets, which almost always means it never reached the gel stage. It is not a canning safety failure, and you can pour it back into a pot and re-boil it, but you would have to process again in clean jars with new lids.

Notes

  • Nobody at this store has cooked this particular batch. Follow a tested process, and if anything about a jar looks or smells wrong when you open it, dispose of it without tasting.
  • Local jam is on the shelf here for the eleven months a year when there is no fruit. Rock Creek Preserves does the marionberry one.

Keeping it

Sealed jars keep a year or more in a cool dark cupboard, with the rings removed so a failed seal is visible. Once opened, refrigerate and use within about a month.
