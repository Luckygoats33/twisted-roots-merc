Pie, Cobbler & Preserves

Marionberry Pie

The state's best berry, and it releases enough juice to flood a badly built pie.

One 9 inch double-crust pie
Makes
35 minutes
Hands on
5 hours including cooling
Start to finish

The marionberry is a blackberry cross bred at Oregon State and named for the county, and it is darker, more acidic and more aromatic than the blackberries growing over every fence in this valley. It is also very wet, which is the whole engineering problem of a marionberry pie.

Two things make it work. Enough starch for the amount of juice the fruit will actually release, which is more than you think, and a genuinely long cooling period. A pie cut warm is a pie that runs across the plate, and the cooling is when the starch sets, not an afterthought.

What you need

Filling

  • 6 cups marionberries, fresh or frozen, about 850 g
  • 3/4 cup granulated sugar, more if the fruit is sharp
  • 5 tbsp instant tapioca or 1/3 cup cornstarch
  • 2 tbsp lemon juice
  • 1/2 tsp lemon zest
  • 1/4 tsp fine sea salt
  • 1 tbsp butter, in small pieces

Pastry

  • 1 batch all-butter pie dough, two discs
  • 1 large egg beaten with 1 tbsp cream
  • 2 tbsp coarse sugar

How

  1. If the berries are frozen, do not thaw them. Frozen fruit tossed with starch and baked from frozen gives up its liquid gradually into a filling that is already thickening, where thawed fruit dumps it all at once.

  2. Toss the berries with sugar, starch, lemon juice, zest and salt. Let the bowl sit 15 minutes so the starch hydrates and the sugar starts drawing juice.

  3. Taste a berry with the sugar on it. Marionberries vary enormously; early ones are sharp and late ones are almost sweet enough on their own. Adjust now rather than regretting it later.

  4. Line a 9 inch pan with the bottom crust, letting it overhang, and chill it while you roll the top.

  5. Fill the shell, mounding the fruit slightly in the centre because it will settle, and dot the butter over the top.

  6. Add the top crust or a lattice, seal and crimp the rim firmly, and cut generous vents if you have gone with a solid top. Steam has to leave or the filling boils and the crust steams.

  7. Freeze the assembled pie 20 minutes, then brush with egg wash and scatter coarse sugar.

  8. Bake at 425°F (220°C) on a preheated sheet pan on a low rack for 20 minutes. The hot pan under the pie is what cooks the bottom crust, and it is the difference between crisp and pale.

  9. Drop the oven to 375°F (190°C) and continue until the crust is deep golden brown all over and the filling is bubbling thickly and slowly in the centre, not just around the edge. Thick slow bubbles mean the starch has activated; thin fast ones mean it has not.

  10. An instant-read thermometer in the middle of the filling should read close to 200°F (93°C). Cornstarch and tapioca both need to get to roughly that temperature to set, and a pie pulled at the point where the edges look right is usually short in the centre.

  11. Cool the pie completely on a rack, at least four hours and preferably overnight. This is not optional and it is not conservative advice. Warm fruit pie filling is liquid.

  12. What goes wrong: not enough thickener for very wet fruit, and cutting it early. If your pie ran, one of those two things happened, and it was probably the second.

Notes

  • Rock Creek Preserves marionberry jam sits on the local shelf here for the eleven months a year when the fresh berries are not around. A pie built on jam is a different thing, but a good one.
  • Frozen marionberries are as good as fresh for pie and often better, because the freezing ruptures cells and gets the juice moving early.

Keeping it

Room temperature under a cloth for two days, then in the fridge for three more. Fruit pie does not like being refrigerated from the start; the crust goes soft.
