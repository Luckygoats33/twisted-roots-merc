Pie, Cobbler & Preserves

Refrigerator Pickles

Crisp, sour, and never processed, which is stated here on purpose.

3 quart jars
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
48 hours mostly waiting
Start to finish

Refrigerator pickles skip the canner entirely, which means they skip the heat that turns a canned cucumber soft. They stay genuinely crisp, they take twenty minutes, and they are ready in two days. The trade is that they live in the fridge and they do not keep for a year.

This is worth saying plainly because the difference between a refrigerator pickle and a canned one is a safety difference, not a convenience one. An unprocessed jar has not been heated enough to be shelf stable no matter how much vinegar is in it, and it belongs in the cold.

What you need

Cucumbers

  • 3 lb (1.4 kg) small pickling cucumbers, blossom ends trimmed off
  • 3 tbsp pickling or kosher salt for the pre-salt
  • 4 cups ice

Brine

  • 3 cups vinegar at 5 percent acidity, white or cider
  • 3 cups water
  • 3 tbsp granulated sugar
  • 2 tbsp pickling salt

Per jar

  • 2 heads fresh dill, or 1 tbsp dill seed
  • 4 cloves garlic, halved
  • 1 tsp black peppercorns
  • 1 tsp mustard seed
  • 1/2 tsp coriander seed
  • 1 grape leaf or 1/4 tsp black tea leaves, for crispness
  • 1 dried chile, optional

How

  1. Trim the blossom end off every cucumber, taking a good sixteenth of an inch. That end carries an enzyme that softens pickles, and it is the most common reason a jar goes limp.

  2. Layer the cucumbers with the salt and ice in a bowl and leave them 2 hours. This draws water out and firms them up, which is the second half of the crispness question.

  3. Rinse them well and dry them. Skipping the rinse leaves them far too salty once the brine goes in as well.

  4. Pack the cucumbers upright into clean quart jars, tightly, with the aromatics tucked in around them. Add the grape leaf or the tea, which contribute tannins that help keep them firm.

  5. Heat the vinegar, water, sugar and salt together, stirring, until everything is fully dissolved and the brine is steaming and clear. It does not need to boil hard.

  6. No internal temperature applies to a refrigerator pickle. The brine is heated only to dissolve the salt and sugar, which is exactly why these stay crisp and exactly why they are not shelf stable.

  7. Pour the hot brine over to cover the cucumbers completely, leaving about half an inch of space at the top. Anything above the liquid line will spoil first.

  8. Tap the jars on the counter to release air bubbles, lid them, and let them cool on the counter before refrigerating.

  9. Wait 48 hours before opening. They are edible sooner and they taste raw and disjointed; the brine needs two days to get to the middle of a whole cucumber.

  10. If you want shelf-stable pickles instead, that is a different project. Use a tested recipe with a stated brine ratio and processing time from the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning or OSU Extension, and expect a softer pickle as the price of the process. Do not simply water bath this recipe.

  11. Processing time for any canned pickle depends on jar size and on altitude. Siletz is close to sea level, so base figures apply here; readers higher up need the line in the tested table that matches their own elevation.

  12. What goes wrong: soft pickles. Blossom end left on, cucumbers too large, brine too weak, or a jar that sat warm too long before it went in the fridge. In that order of likelihood.

Notes

  • These are refrigerator pickles. They have not been processed, they are not shelf stable, and they are not safe stored at room temperature.
  • Sliced spears take the brine much faster than whole cucumbers and are ready overnight. They also go soft sooner.

Keeping it

In the fridge, about two months, staying crisp for the first three or four weeks. Throw out any jar with cloudy or ropey brine, a bad smell, or mould of any kind.
