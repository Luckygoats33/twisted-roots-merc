Shortcake

A biscuit, not a sponge, and the berries need half an hour to make their own syrup.

8 shortcakes
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
1 hour
Start to finish

Shortcake in this country is a sweetened biscuit split and filled, not the yellow sponge cups sold in plastic trays. The difference matters, because a biscuit has structure and salt and butter and can stand up to a pile of wet fruit, and a sponge cup dissolves into pink mush within four minutes.

The other half of the recipe is macerating. Sugar on cut berries pulls out juice by osmosis and makes a syrup that is better than anything you could cook, and it takes half an hour of doing nothing. That syrup is really the point of the dish.

What you need

Berries

  • 8 cups strawberries or mixed berries, hulled and sliced
  • 1/2 cup granulated sugar
  • 1 tbsp lemon juice
  • 1 pinch fine sea salt

Biscuits

  • 390 g all-purpose flour, about 3 cups
  • 1/3 cup granulated sugar
  • 1 tbsp baking powder
  • 1/2 tsp baking soda
  • 1 tsp fine sea salt
  • 170 g cold butter, in cubes
  • 1.25 cups cold buttermilk
  • 2 tbsp cream for brushing
  • 3 tbsp coarse sugar

Cream

  • 2 cups heavy cream, very cold
  • 3 tbsp powdered sugar
  • 1 tsp vanilla extract

How

  1. Toss the berries with sugar, lemon and salt and leave them at room temperature at least 30 minutes, stirring once or twice, until a pool of dark syrup has collected in the bottom of the bowl.

  2. Heat the oven to 425°F (220°C) and line a sheet pan with parchment.

  3. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, soda and salt. Cut in the cold butter with your fingertips until the largest pieces are pea-sized and the rest is coarse.

  4. Pour in the buttermilk and stir with a fork until it just comes together, with dry patches still showing. Stop early. A shortcake dough that looks finished in the bowl is overmixed.

  5. Tip it onto the counter, pat it into a rectangle, and fold it in thirds like a letter. Do that twice more. Those folds are what make the biscuit pull apart in layers instead of crumbling.

  6. Pat to about an inch thick and cut eight rounds with a sharp cutter, pressing straight down without twisting. Twisting seals the edges and the biscuit cannot rise there.

  7. Brush the tops with cream, scatter coarse sugar, and chill the tray for 10 minutes.

  8. Bake until the biscuits have risen tall, the tops are deep golden, and the sides look dry and set rather than damp. A skewer into the middle of one should come out clean, and if you want the number, an instant-read thermometer in the centre reads about 200°F (93°C) when a biscuit is baked through.

  9. Cool on a rack 10 minutes. Split them with your fingers rather than a knife, which follows the natural layer and gives you two rough faces that hold syrup.

  10. Whip the cream with the powdered sugar and vanilla to soft peaks that just hold their shape and flop over at the tip. Stiff whipped cream is grainy and it slides off.

  11. Build: bottom half, cream, berries with plenty of syrup, top half at an angle, more berries. Assemble at the table, because a built shortcake starts collapsing immediately.

  12. What goes wrong: warm butter and heavy hands. Both give you a dense scone rather than a light biscuit, and the fruit will drown it.

Notes

  • Local honey whipped into the cream instead of powdered sugar is very good and slightly less sweet.
  • The biscuits freeze unbaked on a tray and bake from frozen, adding a few minutes. That makes this a fifteen minute dessert in season.

Keeping it

Biscuits are best the day they are made and acceptable the next, refreshed in a hot oven for a few minutes. Macerated berries keep two days in the fridge and get better on the first one.
