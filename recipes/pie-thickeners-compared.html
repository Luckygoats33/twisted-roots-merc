Pie, Cobbler & Preserves

Thickeners, Compared

Why your pie leaked, in terms of which starch you used and how much water the fruit had.

One clear idea about how much starch your fruit needs
Makes
15 minutes of reading
Hands on
Saves you an afternoon of disappointment
Start to finish

A pie leaks for exactly one reason: there was more free water in the fruit than the thickener could hold. Everything else, the crust, the oven, the pan, is downstream of that. So the useful skill is not following a number in a recipe, it is looking at the fruit in front of you and deciding how wet it is.

Below are the four thickeners worth knowing, what each one does badly, and rough amounts per quart of fruit. Treat them as starting points and adjust upward for very juicy fruit and downward for anything dense and dry.

What you need

Per quart of prepared fruit, roughly

  • 2 to 3 tbsp cornstarch for medium-wet fruit
  • 3 to 4 tbsp instant tapioca for very wet berries
  • 4 to 5 tbsp all-purpose flour for apples and dense fruit
  • 1.5 to 2 tbsp tapioca starch or arrowroot for a glossy finish
  • 1 tbsp lemon juice, which affects how starches set

Wetness guide

  • 1 quart of blackberries or marionberries, the wettest category
  • 1 quart of peaches or cherries, wet but less so
  • 1 quart of apples or pears, comparatively dry
  • 1 quart of frozen fruit, which behaves wetter than the same fruit fresh

How

  1. Cornstarch is the default. It sets firm and clear-ish, it needs to reach roughly 200°F (93°C) to thicken fully, and it thins out again if you boil it hard or too long. It also loses power in the presence of a lot of acid, which matters with rhubarb and sharp berries.

  2. Instant tapioca is the berry specialist. It holds more water than cornstarch, it stays glossy, and it tolerates freezing and reheating without weeping. Its drawback is texture: the granules need at least 15 minutes to hydrate before baking or you get small firm beads in the finished pie.

  3. Flour is the old country answer. It is forgiving, it never breaks, and it produces a cloudy, slightly gluey set that some people find comforting and others find dull. It also mutes the flavour of the fruit more than any other option.

  4. Arrowroot and tapioca starch give the glossiest, cleanest set and the most vivid fruit colour. They are the most expensive, they break down under long baking, and they turn slimy if you use too much. Good for a shallow pie, poor for a deep one.

  5. Judge your fruit before you measure anything. Squeeze a handful. If juice runs freely between your fingers, go to the top of the range. If it barely moistens your palm, go to the bottom.

  6. Understand that frozen fruit is wetter. Ice crystals rupture cell walls, so thawed fruit gives up far more liquid than the same fruit fresh. Bake from frozen, and add roughly a quarter more starch.

  7. Let the mixed filling stand 15 minutes before it goes in the shell, whichever starch you used. Dry starch dropped straight into a hot oven clumps and leaves both watery patches and chalky ones.

  8. Bake until the filling bubbles in the middle of the pie, thickly and slowly, and not just around the rim. Fast thin bubbles at the edge mean the centre has not reached temperature yet and the starch there has not activated.

  9. Cool completely. Every starch on this list continues to set as it cools, and a pie cut at an hour will run even if it was baked perfectly. This is the single most common reason people blame the thickener.

  10. If you have leaked anyway, diagnose honestly. A pie that ran but tasted right was underthickened or undercooled. A pie that was firm but pasty was overthickened. A pie that went watery after a day in the fridge used cornstarch where tapioca would have held.

Notes

  • Never substitute these one for one by volume. Tapioca and cornstarch are roughly comparable, flour has about half the thickening power, and arrowroot has slightly more than cornstarch.
  • A tablespoon of ground instant tapioca, buzzed in a spice grinder, dissolves invisibly and is the best of both worlds for a berry pie.

Keeping it

Keep starches in sealed jars away from steam. Cornstarch lasts years; instant tapioca goes slowly stale and loses some power after about a year in a humid cupboard.
