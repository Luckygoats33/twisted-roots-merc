Biscuits, Rolls & Buns

Biscuits Built For Gravy

A leaner biscuit at 1:4 fat to flour that soaks without dissolving, and the sausage gravy to put on it.

Eight biscuits and enough gravy for all of them
Makes
40 minutes
Hands on
1 hour
Start to finish

A rich flaky biscuit is the wrong tool under gravy. All that butter makes fine layers and those layers slide apart and turn to paste the moment something wet lands on them. What you want here is leaner and tighter: fat to flour at one to four, which builds a sturdier crumb with a closer grain that absorbs gravy and still holds a fork. It is a worse biscuit on its own and a considerably better one under a ladle, which is the whole argument. The gravy is a plain roux gravy and the pepper is not optional.

What you need

Biscuits

  • 480 g all-purpose flour (about 4 cups)
  • 18 g baking powder (about 4 tsp)
  • 3 g baking soda (about 1/2 tsp)
  • 10 g fine salt (about 1 3/4 tsp)
  • 120 g cold unsalted butter, cubed (about 8 1/2 tbsp)
  • 350 g cold buttermilk (about 1 1/2 cups)

Sausage gravy

  • 450 g breakfast sausage, casings removed (about 1 lb)
  • 45 g all-purpose flour (about 6 tbsp)
  • 700 g whole milk (about 3 cups)
  • 1 tsp cracked black pepper, and more after
  • Salt, to taste
  • A pinch of grated nutmeg

How

  1. Heat the oven to 450°F (230°C). Whisk the flour, baking powder, soda and salt.

  2. Cut in the cold butter until it is mostly pea-sized with nothing larger than a hazelnut. Less butter than usual means it disperses faster, so check earlier than you would otherwise.

  3. Add the buttermilk and stir with a fork until just together. At 73% hydration this dough is wetter than the house biscuit, which is deliberate: less fat, more water, a tighter and stronger crumb.

  4. Pat out 2 cm thick, fold in thirds once only. One fold gives some structure without building the fragile layers you are trying to avoid here.

  5. Pat to 2.5 cm, cut straight down with a floured cutter, and set them on a tray with sides touching.

  6. Bake 15 to 18 minutes, until the tops are gold and the sides look dry and set rather than damp, and an instant-read reads 205°F (96°C).

  7. While they bake, start the gravy. Break the sausage into a cold heavy skillet and put it over medium heat. Cook, breaking it up, until it is well browned in patches, no pink remains, and an instant-read in the thickest crumble reads 160°F (71°C).

  8. Look at the fat in the pan. You want about 45 g of it. Pour off the excess or add butter to make up the difference, because the flour needs a matching weight of fat to make a roux that will not go greasy or grainy.

  9. Scatter the flour over the meat and stir constantly for two minutes, until the raw flour smell is gone and the mixture looks like wet sand clinging to the sausage.

  10. Add the milk in three lots, stirring hard after each and letting it thicken before the next. Adding it all at once is how you get lumps.

  11. Simmer gently 6 to 10 minutes, stirring, until the gravy coats the back of a spoon and holds a line when you draw a finger through it. It will thicken further off the heat, so stop while it is still a shade looser than you want.

  12. Season with black pepper, nutmeg and salt to taste. Split the biscuits, ladle over, and add more pepper than seems polite.

  13. If the gravy goes stodgy waiting, loosen it with a splash of milk over low heat until it flows again. If it broke and looks greasy, there was too much fat for the flour.

Notes

  • Sage-heavy sausage is the traditional choice and the one that tastes like breakfast. Hot sausage works and needs less pepper.
  • The gravy is better with a splash of the water the potatoes were cooked in, if there happen to be potatoes.

Keeping it

Gravy keeps three days in the fridge and reheats with milk. The biscuits are a same-day proposition.
