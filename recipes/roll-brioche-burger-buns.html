Biscuits, Rolls & Buns

Brioche Burger Buns

Twenty-five percent butter, an overnight chill, and a bun that holds together under a wet patty.

Eight buns, about 110 g each baked
Makes
40 minutes
Hands on
About 16 hours, mostly overnight in the fridge
Start to finish

These are brioche in ratio if not in ambition: butter at twenty-five percent of the flour weight, which is rich enough to give the close, tender, faintly yellow crumb people want and lean enough that the bun does not collapse under something hot and wet. The overnight chill is doing two jobs. It firms the butter so a very soft dough becomes shapeable, and it gives the flavour somewhere to develop, because a dough with this much sugar and fat ferments fast and tastes of very little if you rush it.

What you need

Dough

  • 500 g bread flour (about 4 cups)
  • 200 g whole milk, cold (about 3/4 cup plus 1 tbsp)
  • 2 large eggs, about 100 g
  • 50 g caster sugar (about 1/4 cup)
  • 10 g fine salt (about 1 3/4 tsp)
  • 8 g instant dried yeast (about 2 1/2 tsp)
  • 125 g unsalted butter, soft (about 9 tbsp)

To finish

  • 1 egg beaten with 1 tbsp milk, for the wash
  • 2 tbsp sesame seeds

How

  1. Mix the flour, sugar, salt and yeast, add the cold milk and eggs, and bring to a dough. Knead 10 minutes, until it is smooth and strong — build the gluten thoroughly before any butter goes near it, because fat coats the proteins and stops them linking.

  2. Add the butter 25 g at a time, working each piece in completely before the next. The dough will break apart into a greasy mess after each addition and come back together within a minute. If it stays greasy, it is too warm; chill it for 15 minutes and carry on.

  3. Knead until glossy, elastic and pulling cleanly from the bowl. It should stretch into a thin, even, translucent sheet.

  4. Bulk one hour at room temperature, then cover and refrigerate 8 to 14 hours. Cold brioche dough handles like modelling clay; warm brioche dough handles like something you want to throw away.

  5. Divide the cold dough into eight pieces of about 120 g and shape each into a tight ball on an unfloured counter.

  6. Set them well apart on a parchment-lined tray and flatten each gently with your palm to about 9 cm across. Flattening now gives you a bun rather than a ball; brioche puffs up more than you think it will.

  7. Prove 2 to 3 hours from cold, until nearly doubled, visibly domed, and a light poke leaves a dent that fills back slowly. Cold dough proves slowly and this stage always takes longer than people allow for.

  8. Heat the oven to 375°F (190°C). Brush twice with egg wash, gently, and scatter sesame seeds.

  9. Bake 16 to 20 minutes, until the tops are deep glossy brown and an instant-read in the centre of a bun reads 190°F (88°C). Enriched dough browns early because of the sugar and egg, so trust the thermometer over the colour.

  10. Cool completely on a rack before splitting, at least an hour. Warm brioche compresses to nothing under a knife.

  11. If the buns spread flat instead of doming, the shaping was slack or the dough was over-proofed. If the crumb is dense and the butter leaked out, it went in too fast or too warm.

Notes

  • Toast the cut sides in a dry pan with a little butter before building anything on them. It is a seal against the juices as much as it is flavour.
  • The same dough at 60 g a piece makes slider buns, baked for 12 to 14 minutes to the same internal temperature.

Keeping it

Three days in a bag. They freeze well split, which means you can toast them from frozen.
