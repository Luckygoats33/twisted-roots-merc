Biscuits, Rolls & Buns

House Buttermilk Biscuits

The counter biscuit: fat to flour at 1:2.8, cut tall, baked hot, no twisting the cutter.

Eight to nine biscuits
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
50 minutes
Start to finish

Everything about a biscuit is decided in the first five minutes, before it goes anywhere near heat. Cold fat left in visible pieces makes steam pockets and steam pockets make layers; fat that has melted into the flour makes a scone-ish crumb that is fine but is not this. The ratio here is one part butter to two point eight parts flour by weight. At 1:2 you get something closer to pastry, rich and short and inclined to go greasy at the base. At 1:4 you get a bread roll. This sits where a biscuit should: tall, flaky, and structurally sound enough to hold an egg.

What you need

Dough

  • 480 g all-purpose flour, or soft winter wheat flour (about 4 cups)
  • 18 g baking powder (about 4 tsp)
  • 3 g baking soda (about 1/2 tsp)
  • 9 g fine salt (about 1 1/2 tsp)
  • 10 g sugar (about 2 tsp)
  • 170 g cold unsalted butter, cut in 1 cm cubes (about 12 tbsp)
  • 340 g cold buttermilk (about 1 1/3 cups)

To finish

  • 20 g melted butter for the tops (about 1 1/2 tbsp)
  • A pinch of flaky salt

How

  1. Put the cubed butter in the freezer for 10 minutes and heat the oven to 450°F (230°C). A hot oven is half of the lift: the steam has to fire before the fat has time to melt out.

  2. Whisk the flour, baking powder, soda, salt and sugar together thoroughly. Under-mixed leavening gives you one bitter biscuit and eight flat ones.

  3. Cut in the butter with your fingertips or a pastry blender until the largest pieces are the size of a hazelnut and the rest is coarse and irregular. Stop while you can still see butter. If it looks like even sand, it has gone too far.

  4. Pour in the buttermilk all at once and stir with a fork until it just comes together in a shaggy mass with a few dry patches. Twelve or fifteen strokes, not thirty. Hydration here is 71% on the flour, which feels wetter than most people expect.

  5. Tip it onto a floured counter, pat into a rectangle about 2 cm thick, and fold it in thirds like a letter. Turn it 90 degrees and repeat twice more. That is three folds and it is where the layers come from.

  6. Pat out to a final 2.5 cm thick. Flour a 2 3/4 inch cutter and press straight down without twisting — twisting seals the cut edge and the biscuit rises lopsided or not at all. Push straight down, lift straight up.

  7. Set them on a tray so the sides touch. Biscuits climbing on each other rise higher than biscuits standing alone; the touching edges stay soft and give the dough something to hold onto.

  8. Brush the tops with melted butter, avoiding the cut sides, and scatter a little flaky salt.

  9. Bake 14 to 18 minutes, until the tops are deep gold, the sides show dry flaky seams rather than a wet-looking band, and the biscuits feel light when lifted. An instant-read through the side reads 205°F (96°C).

  10. Eat them within the hour. If they came out short and dense, the likely culprits in order are: warm butter, over-mixing, a twisted cutter, or leavening past its date of usefulness — test it by dropping a teaspoon in hot water and watching whether it fizzes hard.

Notes

  • Soft winter wheat flour, low protein, makes a more tender biscuit. All-purpose works; bread flour makes them chewy.
  • Grating frozen butter on the coarse side of a box grater is faster than cutting it in and keeps the pieces colder.
  • Scraps re-patted make a fine biscuit and an ugly one. Do not re-roll more than once.

Keeping it

Same day. Day two they are for splitting, toasting hard under a grill, and covering in something.
