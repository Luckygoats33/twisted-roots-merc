Biscuits, Rolls & Buns

When There Is No Buttermilk

Three substitutions that actually work, with the liquid adjusted for each, plus the biscuit to test them on.

Eight biscuits
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
45 minutes
Start to finish

Buttermilk is doing two separate jobs. It is acidic, which reacts with baking soda for lift and tenderises gluten so the crumb stays short. It is also thick, because culturing thickens milk, and that viscosity is why a buttermilk dough hydrates without going slack. Most substitutes replace the acid and forget the body, which is why milk soured with vinegar makes a wetter, flatter biscuit if you use it gram for gram. Here are the three worth knowing, corrected for that, and a plain biscuit to try them in.

What you need

Pick one substitute for 280 g buttermilk

  • Soured milk: 260 g whole milk plus 15 g lemon juice or white vinegar, stood 10 minutes
  • Thinned yogurt: 200 g plain whole-milk yogurt plus 80 g milk, whisked smooth
  • Powdered: 25 g cultured buttermilk powder whisked into the dry flour, plus 280 g water

Biscuit

  • 400 g all-purpose flour (about 3 1/4 cups)
  • 14 g baking powder (about 3 tsp)
  • 2 g baking soda (about 1/3 tsp)
  • 8 g fine salt (about 1 1/3 tsp)
  • 140 g cold unsalted butter, cubed (about 10 tbsp)
  • 280 g of your chosen substitute, cold

How

  1. If you are souring milk, stir the acid in and leave it 10 minutes. It will look curdled and slightly thickened. That is the acid doing its part; it will never reach the body of real buttermilk, which is why the milk here is measured at 260 g rather than 280 and the acid makes up the rest. Swap it gram for gram and the dough runs wet.

  2. If you are thinning yogurt, whisk until there are no lumps at all. Lumps of yogurt do not disperse in a barely-mixed dough and each one bakes into a pale wet spot.

  3. Heat the oven to 450°F (230°C) and whisk the flour, baking powder, soda and salt together — plus the buttermilk powder if you are using that route.

  4. Cut in the cold butter until the biggest pieces are hazelnut sized and the mixture is coarse and uneven. This is a 1:2.9 fat to flour dough, near enough the same as the house biscuit, so the substitutes are being tested rather than the ratio.

  5. Add the liquid all at once and stir with a fork until it just holds together. If it is drier than you expect, add another 15 g rather than working it harder. Yogurt doughs usually want a splash more; soured milk almost never does.

  6. Pat out 2 cm thick, fold in thirds twice, then pat to 2.5 cm.

  7. Cut with a floured 2 3/4 inch cutter, pressing straight down, and set them on a tray with the sides touching.

  8. Bake 14 to 17 minutes, until risen and deep gold on top with dry seams down the sides, and an instant-read reads 205°F (96°C).

  9. Judge them honestly. Yogurt gives the closest result and the softest crumb. Soured milk gives good lift and a slightly thinner flavour. Powder is the best of the three for keeping in a cupboard and the one worth having when the road is closed and the shop is shut.

  10. What does not work: sour cream straight, which is too fat and too thick and bakes greasy; and lemon juice in cream, which curdles into something you cannot mix in evenly.

Notes

  • Plain kefir is an excellent swap at a straight one-to-one and needs no adjustment at all.
  • If you are out of both buttermilk and acid, drop the baking soda and use 18 g of baking powder with plain milk. It will be milder and a shade less tender.

Keeping it

Cultured buttermilk powder keeps for a year in a cool cupboard, which is why it is on the shelf here.
