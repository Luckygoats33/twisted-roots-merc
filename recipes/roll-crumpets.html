Biscuits, Rolls & Buns

Crumpets

A 160% batter, bicarbonate at the end, and rings on a low griddle until the holes open and set.

Ten crumpets
Makes
35 minutes
Hands on
2 hours
Start to finish

A crumpet is a batter, not a dough, and the holes are not incidental — they are the entire product. Yeast makes the bubbles during the rest, and a slug of bicarbonate stirred in at the very end makes a second wave of gas that pushes them up through a batter loose enough to let them travel. Cook them low. The top has to set while the bubbles are still opening, and every crumpet that comes out solid and heavy was cooked too fast, too thick, or without the bicarbonate.

What you need

Batter

  • 250 g bread flour (about 2 cups)
  • 5 g instant dried yeast (about 1 1/2 tsp)
  • 5 g caster sugar (about 1 tsp)
  • 300 g warm water (about 1 1/4 cups)
  • 100 g warm whole milk (about 1/3 cup plus 1 tbsp)

Added at the end

  • 4 g fine salt (about 3/4 tsp)
  • 3 g bicarbonate of soda (about 1/2 tsp)
  • 30 g warm water (about 2 tbsp)

For cooking

  • 20 g butter or oil for the rings and pan (about 1 1/2 tbsp)

How

  1. Whisk the flour, yeast and sugar with the warm water and milk until completely smooth. It will be a loose batter, around 160% hydration, roughly the consistency of thick double cream.

  2. Cover and leave in a warm place for 60 to 90 minutes, until the surface is a mass of bubbles, the batter has risen and started to fall back, and it smells strongly of yeast. That collapse is the signal, not a problem.

  3. Dissolve the bicarbonate in the 30 g of warm water with the salt and stir it through the batter. It will loosen further and foam. Leave it 20 minutes.

  4. Heat a heavy skillet over low heat and butter it along with the inside of the rings well. An instant-read on the pan surface should read about 150°C. This is lower than feels right and it is the difference between crumpets and pancakes.

  5. Set the rings in the pan and pour in enough batter to come about 1.5 cm up the side — roughly 60 g each. Too deep and the middle never sets; too shallow and there is no depth for the holes to form in.

  6. Cook 8 to 12 minutes without touching them. Holes should rise and open across the surface from the edges inward, and the top should go from wet and glossy to matte and dry. If no holes appear in the first four minutes, the batter is too thick — whisk another 20 g of water into the rest.

  7. When the top is set and dry to a fingertip, lift the rings off with a cloth and turn the crumpets. Cook the second side only 2 to 3 minutes, until it is lightly coloured. That side is meant to be pale.

  8. Check one: an instant-read through the side should read 200°F (93°C), and a crumpet torn in half should be full of open channels rather than damp and cakey.

  9. Cool on a rack. Crumpets are always toasted before eating, even fresh ones, which is why the first cook is so gentle.

  10. If they came out smooth on top with no holes, either the batter was too thick, the bicarbonate was old, or the pan was too hot and the surface sealed before the gas got up through it.

Notes

  • Batter that has sat too long tastes sharply of alcohol. Use it within about three hours of the bicarbonate going in.
  • Butter the rings every time. Crumpet batter welds to bare metal.

Keeping it

Four days in a bag, or freeze them. They are designed for toasting, so age matters less here than anywhere else in this section.
