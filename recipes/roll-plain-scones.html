Biscuits, Rolls & Buns

Plain Scones

Low fat, high leavening, cut deep and straight, out of the oven in twelve minutes.

Ten to twelve scones
Makes
20 minutes
Hands on
35 minutes
Start to finish

A scone is not a sweet biscuit, whatever the shape suggests. The fat is far lower — about one part to five and a half here, against one to two point eight in the house biscuit — and the leavening is much higher, so the lift comes chemically rather than from steam in butter layers. That makes a lighter, cakier, more open crumb that is built to carry jam and cream. They also go stale within hours, which is not a flaw so much as a schedule: make them when people are already in the house.

What you need

Dough

  • 450 g plain flour (about 3 3/4 cups)
  • 20 g baking powder (about 4 1/2 tsp)
  • 60 g caster sugar (about 1/4 cup)
  • 6 g fine salt (about 1 tsp)
  • 80 g cold unsalted butter, cubed (about 5 1/2 tbsp)
  • 250 g whole milk, cold (about 1 cup)
  • 1 large egg, about 50 g

To finish

  • 1 egg beaten with 1 tbsp milk, for the tops

How

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F (220°C) and line a tray. Scones want a hot oven and a short bake; a slow one dries them out before they have finished rising.

  2. Whisk the flour, baking powder, sugar and salt together properly. Twenty grams of baking powder is a lot and it has to be evenly distributed or you get bitter patches.

  3. Rub in the cold butter with your fingertips until it looks like fine breadcrumbs. Unlike a biscuit, you want it evenly dispersed here — no visible pieces.

  4. Beat the egg into the milk and add it all at once. Bring it together with a knife blade held flat, cutting through rather than stirring, until it just forms a soft dough. It should be softer and stickier than a biscuit dough.

  5. Turn out onto a floured counter and pat, do not roll, to 3 cm thick. Thin scones do not rise; the height you cut is close to the height you get plus half again.

  6. Dip a 2 inch cutter in flour and press straight down through the dough without twisting. Twisting seals and pinches the edge, and pinched scones come out lopsided every time.

  7. Set them on the tray close together, about 2 cm apart, and brush the tops only with egg wash. Egg down the sides glues the rise shut.

  8. Bake 10 to 14 minutes, until well risen, golden on top, and the sides show a pale band and a clear break line. Tapped underneath they should sound light and hollow.

  9. Cool on a rack five minutes and eat them warm. If they came out flat, the likely reasons are dough patted too thin, a twisted cutter, or baking powder that has lost its fizz.

Notes

  • For fruit scones, add 100 g sultanas soaked briefly in hot water and drained.
  • Buttermilk in place of the milk, with 4 g of the baking powder swapped for baking soda, gives a slightly tangier and more tender scone.

Keeping it

Same day, and really the same hour. Day-old scones split and toasted are still worth eating; day-old scones as they are, are not.
