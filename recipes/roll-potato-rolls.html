Biscuits, Rolls & Buns

Potato Rolls

Riced russet and its cooking water in the dough, for rolls that stay soft for four days.

Twelve rolls
Makes
40 minutes
Hands on
3 hours 30 minutes, plus cooling the potato
Start to finish

Potato does three useful things in a dough. Its starch gelatinises during cooking and then holds water in a way raw flour starch cannot, so the crumb stays moist. It brings no gluten, which softens the structure. And the cooking water carries dissolved starch and a little potassium, which the yeast likes. The result keeps noticeably longer than a plain roll of the same enrichment, which is why potato rolls turn up wherever bread has to survive a day. Use a floury russet, not a waxy potato, and rice it rather than mashing it.

What you need

Potato

  • 1 large russet potato, about 300 g
  • 200 g riced cooked potato, cooled (about 1 cup, lightly packed)
  • 150 g of the potato cooking water, cooled to lukewarm (about 2/3 cup)

Dough

  • 500 g bread flour (about 4 cups)
  • 1 large egg, about 50 g
  • 30 g caster sugar (about 2 tbsp)
  • 10 g fine salt (about 1 3/4 tsp)
  • 7 g instant dried yeast (about 2 1/4 tsp)
  • 60 g unsalted butter, soft (about 4 tbsp)

To finish

  • 1 egg beaten with 1 tbsp water, for the wash
  • 20 g melted butter (about 1 1/2 tbsp)

How

  1. Peel and chunk the potato and simmer in unsalted water until a knife goes through with no resistance and the edges are starting to break down, 15 to 20 minutes. Save 150 g of the cooking water.

  2. Rice or press the potato through a sieve while hot, then spread it out and cool it to lukewarm. Riced potato is fluffy; mashed potato is gluey, and glue in a dough makes a dense roll.

  3. Mix the flour, sugar, salt and yeast. Add the potato, the cooking water and the egg, and bring to a dough. It will feel tacky and soft. Resist adding flour.

  4. Knead 8 to 10 minutes, then work in the butter. Potato dough never gets as smooth or as elastic as a straight wheat dough — a slightly slack, silky feel is the target rather than a tight windowpane.

  5. Bulk covered until doubled, 75 to 90 minutes. It rises well because of the extra starch, and it can run past its peak faster than you expect.

  6. Divide into twelve pieces of about 85 g and shape each into a taut ball. Set them on a parchment-lined tray, spaced for pull-apart rolls or well apart for individual buns.

  7. Prove 45 to 60 minutes, until puffy and holding a slow-filling dent.

  8. Heat the oven to 375°F (190°C), brush with egg wash, and bake 18 to 22 minutes, until deep gold and glossy and an instant-read reads 190°F (88°C).

  9. Brush with melted butter as they come out and cool on a rack.

  10. If the crumb is heavy and damp, there was too much potato or it was mashed rather than riced. Two hundred grams to five hundred of flour is about the ceiling.

Notes

  • Left-over mashed potato works if it has no butter, milk or salt in it. If it does, reduce the dough butter and salt accordingly.
  • Flattened to 10 cm across before proving, these are the best hamburger buns in this collection, though a brioche bun holds a fattier patty better.

Keeping it

Four days in a bag on the counter, which is a day or two longer than a plain enriched roll would last.
