Biscuits, Rolls & Buns

Sweet Potato Biscuits

Roasted, not boiled, with the liquid held back because purée moisture is never the same twice.

Ten biscuits
Makes
25 minutes, plus roasting
Hands on
2 hours including roasting and cooling
Start to finish

Roast the sweet potatoes; do not boil them. Boiling waterlogs the flesh and you spend the rest of the recipe compensating for liquid you added on purpose. Roasted purée is denser, sweeter and drier, and it still varies enough between one potato and the next that the buttermilk has to be held back and added by feel. These are soft rather than flaky, faintly sweet, and orange in a way that surprises people. They are the right thing under a slice of ham and they make an unreasonable amount of sense with chowder.

What you need

Purée

  • 2 medium sweet potatoes, about 700 g whole
  • 300 g roasted flesh, cooled and mashed smooth (about 1 1/4 cups)

Dough

  • 420 g all-purpose flour (about 3 1/2 cups)
  • 16 g baking powder (about 3 1/2 tsp)
  • 2 g baking soda (about 1/3 tsp)
  • 9 g fine salt (about 1 1/2 tsp)
  • 20 g light brown sugar (about 1 1/2 tbsp)
  • 1/2 tsp ground cinnamon
  • A pinch of grated nutmeg
  • 130 g cold unsalted butter, cubed (about 9 tbsp)
  • 60 to 90 g cold buttermilk (about 1/4 to 1/3 cup)

How

  1. Roast the sweet potatoes whole at 400°F (205°C) for 50 to 70 minutes, until a knife slides through with no resistance and the skins have wrinkled and started weeping caramel onto the tray. Cool completely, then scoop and mash. Warm purée melts the butter later.

  2. Heat the oven to 425°F (220°C). Whisk the flour, baking powder, soda, salt, sugar and spices together.

  3. Cut in the butter to hazelnut-sized pieces.

  4. Stir 60 g of the buttermilk into the cold purée to loosen it, then add that to the flour and stir with a fork. Add more buttermilk 15 g at a time only if there is dry flour left in the bowl. A dry-roasted potato might want the full 90 g and a wet one none at all.

  5. Bring it together on a floured counter, pat to 2 cm, fold in thirds twice. This dough is softer and stickier than a plain biscuit dough and needs a lighter hand and more flour on the counter.

  6. Pat to 2.5 cm and cut with a floured 2 3/4 inch cutter, straight down.

  7. Set on parchment with sides touching and bake 18 to 22 minutes, until risen, dry to the touch, and browned at the edges — the tops stay orange and will not go golden the way a plain biscuit does, so use the edges as your guide. An instant-read reads 205°F (96°C).

  8. Cool five minutes. They are fragile hot and firm up quickly.

  9. If they are gummy in the middle, the purée was too wet. Next time spread it on a tray and dry it in a low oven for 15 minutes before using it.

Notes

  • Tinned pumpkin works in place of the purée at the same weight and is more consistent, if less interesting.
  • A tablespoon of honey brushed on the hot tops with the butter turns these into something to eat on their own.

Keeping it

Three days in a tin — the purée keeps them moist longer than a plain biscuit. They freeze well baked.
