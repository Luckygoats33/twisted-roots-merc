Soup, Chowder & Stew

Beef and Barley

Chuck browned properly, barley added late so it does not drink the whole pot overnight.

8 bowls, about 4 litres
Makes
45 minutes
Hands on
3 hours
Start to finish

Two things decide how this turns out. The first is the browning: chuck cut in large cubes, dried, and seared in batches until genuinely dark, because a grey pot of stewed beef never recovers the flavour it did not build at the start. The second is when the barley goes in. Pearl barley absorbs about three times its volume in liquid and keeps absorbing for as long as it is in contact with it, so barley added at the beginning gives you a soup at dinner and a solid porridge by morning. Cook it separately, or add it in the last forty minutes and accept that leftovers need thinning.

What you need

Beef

  • 1.1 kg beef chuck, cut in 4 cm cubes
  • 2 teaspoons fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil

Pot

  • 2 onions, diced
  • 3 carrots, cut in 1 cm coins
  • 3 ribs celery, sliced
  • 250 g mushrooms, quartered
  • 4 cloves garlic, minced
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 180 ml red wine
  • 2.5 litres beef stock
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 4 sprigs thyme
  • 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce

Barley and finishing

  • 200 g pearl barley
  • 750 ml water, to cook the barley apart from the soup
  • 3 tablespoons chopped parsley
  • 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar
  • Fine salt and black pepper, to taste

How

  1. Season the beef and let it sit while the pot heats. Pat the cubes dry immediately before searing — surface moisture has to boil off before browning starts, and in a crowded pot it never does.

  2. Heat the oil in a heavy pot over medium-high until it shimmers. Sear the beef in three batches, without crowding, turning each cube only once it has released from the metal and shows a dark brown crust. Grey meat means the pan was too cool or too full. Set the seared beef aside.

  3. Lower the heat to medium and add the onion, carrot, celery and mushrooms with a pinch of salt. Cook until the onions are translucent and the mushrooms have given up their water and started to brown, about ten minutes, scraping the pot bottom as things loosen.

  4. Add the garlic, then the tomato paste, and cook until the paste has darkened from red to brick and smells sweet, about a minute.

  5. Pour in the wine and scrape until every brown patch has lifted off the bottom of the pot. Reduce until the pot looks almost dry and the raw alcohol smell has gone, three or four minutes.

  6. Return the beef with any juices, add the stock, bay, thyme and Worcestershire, and bring to the barest simmer — a bubble breaking every second or two. Cover with the lid slightly ajar.

  7. Cook for about two hours. It is done when a cube of chuck falls apart under light pressure from a spoon and a fork twists out of it with no resistance. This is a collagen conversion, not a temperature target: undercooked chuck is tough, and the fix is always more time, never more heat.

  8. While it cooks, simmer the barley separately in the water with a pinch of salt until the grains are tender with a slight chew, about thirty minutes, then drain. Cooking it apart is the difference between soup tomorrow and porridge tomorrow.

  9. Pull out the bay and thyme stems. Skim the fat off the surface, or chill the pot and lift the cap off cold. Stir in as much barley as you want in the pot now, keeping the rest aside for the leftovers.

  10. Finish with the parsley, vinegar, and salt and pepper to taste. Long-simmered beef soup goes flat without acid at the end; the vinegar will not read as vinegar, it will read as the soup tasting finished.

Notes

  • Cut the beef large. Small cubes look sensible and disintegrate into threads over two hours. Four centimetres is about right, and they will shrink by a third.

  • Oven method: after the stock goes in, put the covered pot in a 300°F (150°C) oven for the same length of time. The heat is more even and you cannot scorch the bottom.

  • Hulled barley, the whole grain version, takes about an hour and keeps more chew. Pearl barley is faster and releases more starch, which thickens the soup slightly.

Keeping it

Five days cold and clearly better on the second day. It freezes for four months; barley in the freezer goes a little soft but is entirely acceptable. Expect to add stock or water on reheating, because the barley will have taken up whatever liquid it could find.
