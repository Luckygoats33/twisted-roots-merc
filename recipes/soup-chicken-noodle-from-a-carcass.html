Soup, Chowder & Stew

Chicken Noodle from a Carcass

The whole point of roasting a chicken. Broth in the evening, soup the next day, nothing wasted.

6 bowls, about 3 litres
Makes
35 minutes
Hands on
4 hours, mostly unattended
Start to finish

A roasted chicken carcass is worth more than most of the meat that came off it. The bones are already browned, the skin and cartilage are loaded with collagen, and the roasting tray is coated in fond. Simmered for three hours it gives a broth with real body — one that sets to a soft jelly in the cold, which is the only reliable test. This is a two-evening job by design: broth on the night you roast the bird, soup the next day. Trying to do both in one go means either a rushed, thin broth or eating at ten at night.

What you need

Broth

  • 1 roasted chicken carcass, plus any wing tips, skin and pan drippings
  • 1 large onion, quartered, skin on
  • 2 carrots, cut in chunks
  • 3 ribs celery with leaves
  • 1 head garlic, halved crosswise
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 1 teaspoon black peppercorns
  • 6 stems parsley
  • 3 litres cold water

Soup

  • 2 tablespoons butter or schmaltz
  • 1 onion, diced
  • 3 carrots, sliced 5 mm thick
  • 3 ribs celery, sliced
  • 2 cloves garlic, minced
  • 1 teaspoon fresh thyme leaves
  • 300 g cooked chicken meat, pulled in large pieces
  • 180 g wide egg noodles
  • 2 tablespoons chopped dill or parsley
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • Fine salt and black pepper, to taste

How

  1. Break the carcass up with your hands so it packs down and stays submerged. Put it in a pot with the onion, carrot, celery, garlic, bay, peppercorns, parsley and any jellied drippings from the roasting tray, and cover with the cold water.

  2. Bring it up slowly and skim off the grey foam that rises as it approaches a simmer, until what comes up is clear rather than muddy.

  3. Drop the heat until the surface barely trembles, with a bubble breaking every couple of seconds, and hold it there for three hours. Never let it boil. A boiled broth is cloudy and greasy, and the fat that emulsifies into it cannot be skimmed back out.

  4. It is done when the broth tastes strongly of chicken rather than of water and the bones crumble under light pressure from tongs. Strain it, without pressing the solids, and cool it fast in shallow containers. Overnight in the cold it should set to a soft jelly with a cap of fat on top.

  5. The next day, lift off the fat and keep it — that is schmaltz, and it is the best fat to start the soup in. Warm 2 tablespoons of it in a heavy pot.

  6. Cook the onion, carrot and celery in the fat with a good pinch of salt until the onion is translucent and the carrot has begun to soften, about eight minutes. Add the garlic and thyme and cook until fragrant, thirty seconds.

  7. Add 2.5 litres of the broth and simmer until the carrots are tender all the way through, about ten minutes. Season it now, properly. Unsalted broth tastes like dishwater and people blame the recipe.

  8. Cook the noodles separately in salted water until just short of tender, then drain. Noodles cooked in the soup release starch that clouds the broth and, worse, keep swelling in the leftovers until there is no liquid left.

  9. Add the pulled chicken to the pot and bring back to a simmer until the meat is heated through. If any of your chicken is not already fully cooked, simmer it until the thickest piece reaches an internal temperature of 165°F (74°C) on an instant-read thermometer.

  10. Add the dill and lemon juice off the heat. Put a nest of noodles in each bowl and ladle the soup over. Taste one more time for salt — hot soup needs more than a cool spoonful suggests.

Notes

  • Broth that did not gel tastes fine but had less collagen than ideal. Add a couple of chicken feet or a wing or two next time; both are almost pure collagen and cost very little.

  • Freeze carcasses until you have two or three. A single bird makes a thin broth. Three make something you could stand a spoon in.

  • Skimming fat: the easy way is time. Cold broth wears a solid fat cap you lift off in one piece. Chasing fat around a hot pot with a ladle is a waste of an evening.

Keeping it

Broth: five days cold, six months frozen. Assembled soup without noodles: four days cold, three months frozen. Noodles: keep them separate, always. The soup is better on the second day and the noodles are not.
