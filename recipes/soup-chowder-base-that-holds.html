Soup, Chowder & Stew

A Chowder Base That Does Not Break

One base, four chowders. Built so it will sit on a low burner through a lunch rush without splitting.

About 3 litres of base, enough for 10 bowls
Makes
35 minutes
Hands on
50 minutes
Start to finish

A chowder splits for one of three reasons: it boiled after the dairy went in, the dairy was too lean, or there was not enough starch in the liquid to protect the proteins. This base addresses all three. It is thickened with a proper blond roux, which puts starch between the milk proteins and slows them clumping. It uses half and half rather than milk, because more fat means a more stable emulsion. And it is built to be held below a simmer, with the dairy stirred in at the end, so a pot standing on a low burner for two hours stays smooth. Add clams, salmon, corn or potatoes to finish.

What you need

Base

  • 150 g bacon, diced, or 100 g butter for a meatless base
  • 2 large onions, finely diced
  • 3 ribs celery, finely diced
  • 1 leek, white part only, split, washed and sliced
  • 3 cloves garlic, minced
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 2 teaspoons fresh thyme leaves
  • 80 g butter
  • 80 g all-purpose flour

Liquid

  • 1.5 litres light stock, clam liquor or a mix
  • 900 g Yukon gold potatoes, cut in 1.5 cm dice
  • 500 ml half and half, warmed
  • 2 teaspoons coarse black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon fine salt, to taste
  • 1 teaspoon cider vinegar

How

  1. Render the bacon in a heavy pot over medium heat until browned and the fat has run, then lift the pieces out. If you are going meatless, melt 100 g of butter instead.

  2. Cook the onion, celery and leek in the fat with the bay and thyme until entirely soft and translucent with no colour, eight to ten minutes. Colour here would turn a white soup grey, and any vegetable that is still firm now will still be firm in the bowl.

  3. Add the garlic and cook until fragrant, then add the 80 g of butter and let it melt.

  4. Sprinkle over the flour and cook, stirring constantly, until it smells of toasted crackers and turns pale straw, about two minutes. Equal weights of fat and flour, cooked blond — the ratio here is 80 g of each to 2 litres of total liquid, which gives a chowder that coats a spoon and still pours.

  5. Add the stock a ladle at a time, stirring smooth between additions. Every lump you fail to break up now is a lump in the finished soup.

  6. Add the potatoes and simmer until a knife enters a cube with no resistance and the smallest pieces have begun to break down and cloud the liquid, about fifteen minutes. That released potato starch adds body on top of the roux and is why this base holds so well.

  7. Take the pot off the heat and stir in the warmed half and half. Warming it first matters: cold dairy hitting a hot starchy base is the classic way to shock it into curdling.

  8. Return to the lowest heat and bring it back only until it steams and the surface shivers. From here it must never exceed about 180 degrees Fahrenheit (82°C) — the point where milk proteins tighten and the fat separates out. On a home stove, that means the lowest burner setting and an occasional stir.

  9. Season with the pepper, salt and vinegar. Now add whatever the chowder is going to be: cooked clams, flakes of hot-smoked salmon — smoked to an internal temperature of 145 degrees Fahrenheit (63°C), and so already cooked — corn and its scraped milk, or roasted mushrooms, folded in off the heat and warmed through for two or three minutes.

  10. If it does break — a grainy look with oil at the edges — take it off the heat immediately, add a splash of cold cream, and blend a portion smooth before stirring it back. That works sometimes. Not boiling it in the first place works always.

Notes

  • Holding it on a stove: a heat diffuser or a pot set inside a larger pan of barely simmering water will hold a chowder for hours without any risk. This is how it is done in a kitchen and it works at home.

  • Do not use skim or low-fat milk. Fat is what stabilises the emulsion, and a low-fat chowder is one that has been engineered to break.

  • Salt at the end, especially with clam liquor or bacon in the pot. Both bring a great deal of salt and the amount varies with everything.

Keeping it

The base holds four days cold and is arguably better on the second day. It does not freeze — potato and dairy both come out ruined. If you want a freezer version, make everything up to and including the stock and potatoes, freeze that, and add the dairy when you reheat.
