Soup, Chowder & Stew

Elk Stew

A lean animal in a wet climate. Low heat, long time, and fat added back because there is none in the meat.

6 bowls
Makes
45 minutes
Hands on
3 hours 30 minutes
Start to finish

Elk is not beef with a different label. A wild animal that walks over mountains all year has dense, close-grained muscle, a good deal of connective tissue, and almost no intramuscular fat, which means two adjustments. Fat has to come from somewhere else — bacon, butter or beef suet — or the finished stew is dry no matter how long it cooks. And the braise has to stay genuinely low, because lean muscle fibres squeeze out their moisture hard at a boil and you end up with tough shreds sitting in liquid. Shoulder, neck and shank are the right cuts. Backstrap in a stew is a waste of the best thing in the freezer.

What you need

Meat

  • 1.1 kg elk shoulder, neck or shank, cut in 4 cm cubes
  • 2 teaspoons fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 40 g all-purpose flour, for dusting
  • 120 g bacon, diced
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil

Braise

  • 2 onions, diced
  • 3 carrots, cut in thick coins
  • 3 ribs celery, sliced
  • 5 cloves garlic, smashed
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 250 ml dry red wine
  • 1.25 litres beef or game stock
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 4 sprigs thyme
  • 6 juniper berries, crushed
  • 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce

Late additions

  • 600 g Yukon gold potatoes, cut in 3 cm chunks
  • 250 g mushrooms, halved
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 3 tablespoons chopped parsley
  • 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar

How

  1. Render the bacon in a heavy pot over medium heat until browned and its fat has run out. Lift the pieces out and keep them. That fat is doing a job the elk cannot do for itself.

  2. Season the elk, dust it lightly with the flour and shake off the excess. Add the oil to the bacon fat and sear the meat in batches over medium-high, without crowding, until each piece has a dark crust and releases from the pot on its own. Set it aside.

  3. Cook the onion, carrot and celery in the remaining fat until softened and browning at the edges, about ten minutes, scraping the pot bottom. Add the garlic, then the tomato paste, and cook until the paste turns brick coloured and smells sweet.

  4. Pour in the wine and scrape hard until nothing is stuck to the pot. Reduce by half, until the sharp alcohol smell has gone.

  5. Return the meat and bacon, add the stock, bay, thyme, juniper and Worcestershire. The liquid should come about three quarters of the way up the meat, not cover it — this is a braise, not a boil.

  6. Bring it to the point where the surface barely moves, then cover and put it in a 300°F (150°C) oven, or hold it on the lowest burner setting. An oven is more forgiving because the heat comes from all sides and nothing scorches on the bottom.

  7. Cook for two to two and a half hours. It is done when a piece of elk gives no resistance to a fork and pulls apart in strands under gentle pressure. Lean game meat has a wider window than beef between tough and dry, and the only way through it is low heat and patience.

  8. Add the potatoes and mushrooms and cook another thirty to forty minutes, until a knife slides into a potato chunk without resistance and the mushrooms have shrunk and darkened.

  9. Stir in the butter off the heat, along with the parsley and vinegar. The butter gives the sauce body and gloss and, again, replaces fat the animal never had. Taste for salt.

  10. If the sauce is thin, lift the solids out and reduce the liquid over medium heat until it coats a spoon, then recombine. Adding more flour at this stage tastes raw and pasty.

Notes

  • Freezer-burnt meat from the bottom of the chest is still usable here — trim the grey papery surfaces off hard and accept a slightly less sweet result. A long braise hides a lot, but it does not hide rancid fat, so trim any fat that smells sharp.

  • Juniper and elk is not a decoration. Juniper cuts through the mineral, faintly liverish edge that some wild meat carries. Six berries in this size pot is enough to notice their absence and not enough to taste as gin.

  • Venison, moose and bear shoulder all behave the same way in this recipe. Bear needs to be cooked to well done for parasite safety — take it well past fork-tender and check with an instant-read thermometer that the thickest piece has passed 165°F (74°C).

Keeping it

Four days cold and noticeably better on the second day, once the fat has redistributed and the juniper has spread out. It freezes for six months, which makes it the right thing to cook when a shoulder comes out of the freezer whole. Freeze it without the potatoes if you can — potato goes grainy — and add them fresh on reheating.
