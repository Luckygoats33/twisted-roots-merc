Soup, Chowder & Stew

French Onion Soup

The onions take an hour. Anyone who tells you fifteen minutes has not made it and is not going to.

6 bowls
Makes
1 hour 15 minutes
Hands on
2 hours
Start to finish

Every shortcut version of this soup fails at the same place. Caramelising onions is not a browning step that takes fifteen minutes, it is a slow dehydration and sugar-browning process that takes the better part of an hour for four pounds of onion, and there is no way to compress it that does not either scorch them or leave you with soft onions that taste of nothing but onion. What you are after is a jammy, deep mahogany mass reduced to about a fifth of its starting volume. The stock matters too — a weak stock behind a great pile of caramelised onion tastes hollow, and this is the soup to spend a real bone stock on.

What you need

Onions

  • 1.8 kg yellow onions, about 6 large, halved and sliced thin
  • 60 g butter
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons fine salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon baking soda, optional

Soup

  • 180 ml dry sherry, dry vermouth or white wine
  • 1.75 litres good beef stock
  • 3 sprigs thyme
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 1 tablespoon sherry vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • Fine salt, to taste

The top

  • 6 thick slices baguette or sourdough
  • 250 g gruyere, grated
  • 50 g parmesan, grated
  • 1 clove garlic, halved, for rubbing the toast

How

  1. Melt the butter and oil in a wide heavy pot over medium heat. Add all the onions with the salt — they will fill the pot to the rim and look absurd — and stir to coat. They collapse to a third of that within fifteen minutes.

  2. Cook, stirring every few minutes, until the onions have released their water, gone completely limp and translucent, and the liquid in the bottom has cooked away. Twenty minutes or so. Nothing has browned yet.

  3. Now the long part. Turn the heat to medium-low, add the sugar, and keep cooking for forty-five minutes to an hour, stirring every five minutes and scraping the bottom. The onions go from pale to gold to amber to a deep mahogany jam, and a brown film builds on the pot which you scrape back in each time. If they are sticking hard or smell sharp and burnt, the heat is too high; add a splash of water and lower it.

  4. The baking soda, if you use it, raises the pH and speeds browning noticeably — expect to save fifteen minutes. It also softens the onions further, which some people dislike. A quarter teaspoon is the limit before they turn to mush and taste faintly metallic.

  5. They are ready when the mass is dark brown, glossy, sweet, reduced to about 500 ml in volume, and tastes of caramel more than of onion. This is not a stage you should guess at from a clock.

  6. Pour in the sherry and scrape the pot bottom clean while it bubbles. Let it reduce until the pot is nearly dry again and the alcohol smell has gone, two or three minutes.

  7. Add the stock, thyme and bay. Simmer gently, uncovered, for thirty minutes, until the broth has taken on the colour and sweetness of the onions and tastes like one thing rather than two. Add the vinegar, pepper, and salt to taste.

  8. Toast the bread until dry and firm all the way through — it has to survive being submerged — and rub one side with the cut garlic.

  9. Ladle the soup into ovenproof bowls, float a toast on each, and pile the gruyere and parmesan on top, taking it right out to the rim so it welds to the edge of the bowl and does not slide off.

  10. Set the bowls on a tray under a broiler until the cheese has melted, bubbled and browned in patches, two to four minutes. Watch it constantly — the distance between browned and burnt at this heat is about thirty seconds. Serve immediately, and warn people that the bowl is dangerously hot.

Notes

  • It tastes thin: the stock was weak. Caramelised onion cannot carry a soup on its own. If your stock is from a carton, reduce a litre of it by a third first and add a spoon of soy sauce.

  • It tastes burnt: the heat was too high during the long stage, or the fond on the pot bottom went black rather than brown. Once it is bitter there is no recovery.

  • No ovenproof bowls: broil the cheese on the toasts on a tray, then float them on the soup. Not the same theatre, same eating.

Keeping it

The soup base without bread or cheese keeps five days cold and freezes for four months, and it improves for the first two days. The caramelised onions alone freeze beautifully in portions and are worth making in a double batch, since the work is identical for twice the onion.
