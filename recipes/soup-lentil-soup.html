Soup, Chowder & Stew

Lentil Soup with Enough Going On

Brown lentils, a lot of aromatics, and acid at the end. Lentils are dull only when nobody seasons them.

6 bowls, about 3 litres
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
1 hour
Start to finish

Lentil soup has a reputation for being worthy and boring, which is a seasoning failure rather than an ingredient failure. Lentils are starchy, earthy and low in fat, and left alone they taste flat. Three things fix that. Real browning at the start, including tomato paste cooked until it darkens. Spice that goes into hot fat rather than into liquid, so it blooms instead of tasting dusty. And a serious amount of acid at the end — lemon or vinegar — which is the single biggest improvement available to any pot of legumes. Brown or green lentils hold their shape; red lentils dissolve entirely and make a different, smoother soup.

What you need

Base

  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 2 onions, diced
  • 3 carrots, diced
  • 3 ribs celery, diced
  • 5 cloves garlic, minced
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 2 teaspoons ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon ground coriander
  • 1 teaspoon smoked paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon red pepper flakes

The pot

  • 400 g brown or green lentils, rinsed and picked over
  • 1 tin diced tomatoes, about 400 g
  • 2 litres vegetable or light stock
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 1 teaspoon fine salt, plus more to taste
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

Finishing

  • 150 g spinach or chard, chopped
  • 3 tablespoons lemon juice, or 2 tablespoons red wine vinegar
  • 3 tablespoons chopped parsley or cilantro
  • 2 tablespoons good olive oil, for drizzling
  • A pinch of flaky salt

How

  1. Heat the olive oil in a heavy pot over medium-high and cook the onion, carrot and celery with a good pinch of salt until softened and browning at the edges, ten to twelve minutes. Take this further than you think you should — the brown at the bottom of the pot is where the soup gets its backbone.

  2. Add the garlic and cook until fragrant, about thirty seconds, then the tomato paste. Stir it around the hot pot until it turns from bright red to brick and smells sweet rather than sharp, a minute or so.

  3. Add the cumin, coriander, paprika and pepper flakes directly into the hot fat and stir for about thirty seconds, until they smell strongly toasted. Ground spices added to liquid taste raw and dusty; the same spices bloomed in fat taste like a different set of ingredients.

  4. Add the lentils, tomatoes, stock, bay, salt and pepper. Scrape the bottom of the pot clean. Bring to a boil, then reduce to a gentle simmer.

  5. Simmer, partly covered, for thirty-five to forty-five minutes, until the lentils are completely tender with no chalky centre when you bite one, and the soup has thickened as their starch releases. Older lentils take longer and some never soften; if they are still hard at an hour, they were too old.

  6. Stir in the greens and cook only until they have wilted and gone dark, a minute or two. Longer and they turn olive-drab and sulphurous.

  7. Off the heat, add the lemon juice and taste. Then taste again after adding more salt. A pot this size usually needs another teaspoon or more, and the difference between an under-seasoned and a correctly seasoned lentil soup is not subtle.

  8. Blend a ladleful or two and stir it back if you want a thicker, more cohesive soup. Serve with parsley, a hard drizzle of good olive oil and a pinch of flaky salt on each bowl.

Notes

  • Red lentils collapse completely in about twenty minutes and give a smooth, dal-like soup. They are not interchangeable with brown lentils here — use less stock and expect no texture from the lentils themselves.

  • Do not salt heavily at the start and do not add the tomatoes before the lentils have begun to soften if your lentils are old. Acid and salt both slow the softening of legume skins, and while the effect is smaller than folklore says, with tired lentils it is enough to matter.

  • A parmesan rind dropped in with the stock adds savoury depth and dissolves almost entirely. Keep the rinds in the freezer for exactly this.

Keeping it

Five days cold and better on the second day, once the spice has spread out. It thickens a lot; loosen with stock. It freezes for four months, though the greens go slack — add those fresh when reheating if you care.
