Soup, Chowder & Stew

Minestrone, Whatever Is In the Drawer

A method rather than a recipe. Beans, a hard cheese rind, whatever vegetables you have, in the right order.

8 bowls, about 4 litres
Makes
35 minutes
Hands on
1 hour 15 minutes
Start to finish

Minestrone is not a fixed recipe and never was; it is a structure for turning whatever vegetables are in the drawer into a substantial soup. The structure is what matters. A slowly cooked aromatic base, then hard vegetables that need time, then a starch, then soft vegetables and greens near the end, then acid and cheese off the heat. Follow that order and almost any combination works. Ignore it and you get mush and crunch in the same spoonful. The other constant is a hard cheese rind in the pot, which dissolves slowly and gives the broth a savoury weight that stock alone will not.

What you need

Base

  • 4 tablespoons olive oil
  • 120 g pancetta or bacon, diced, optional
  • 2 onions, diced
  • 3 carrots, diced
  • 3 ribs celery, diced
  • 5 cloves garlic, minced
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1/2 teaspoon red pepper flakes

The pot

  • 1 tin diced tomatoes, about 400 g
  • 2 litres vegetable or light stock
  • 1 parmesan rind, about 50 g
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 400 g cooked cannellini or borlotti beans, drained
  • 500 g hard vegetables — potato, squash, turnip, cut in 1.5 cm dice
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons fine salt

Late additions

  • 150 g small pasta, such as ditalini
  • 300 g soft vegetables — zucchini, green beans, peas
  • 150 g greens — kale, chard or spinach, chopped

Finishing

  • 3 tablespoons chopped basil or parsley
  • 2 tablespoons good olive oil
  • 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar
  • 60 g grated parmesan, to taste
  • Black pepper, to taste

How

  1. Heat the oil in a large heavy pot over medium and render the pancetta, if using, until browned and its fat has run. Add the onion, carrot and celery with a pinch of salt and cook until soft and lightly golden, about twelve minutes. Do not rush this; it is the base of everything else.

  2. Add the garlic, oregano and pepper flakes and cook until fragrant, thirty seconds. Add the tomato paste and cook, stirring, until it darkens to brick and smells sweet, about a minute.

  3. Add the tomatoes, stock, parmesan rind, bay leaves and salt. Bring to a simmer and scrape the bottom clean.

  4. Add the hard vegetables and the beans. Simmer for about twenty minutes, until the potato or squash gives no resistance to a knife but has not started falling apart.

  5. Add the pasta and cook until it is just short of tender, since it will keep softening in the hot soup. If you plan on leftovers, cook the pasta separately instead and add it by the bowl — pasta left in soup overnight swells into paste and drinks all the broth.

  6. Add the soft vegetables and cook until they are tender but still have some structure, about five minutes. Zucchini in particular goes from cooked to disintegrated very quickly.

  7. Add the greens last and cook only until wilted and dark, one to three minutes depending on whether it is spinach or kale.

  8. Pull out the bay leaves and whatever is left of the rind. Off the heat, stir in the basil, olive oil and vinegar, and taste for salt and pepper. The vinegar is what stops a vegetable soup tasting flat and muddy.

  9. Serve with grated parmesan and more olive oil. This soup should be thick enough that the spoon meets resistance; if it is thin, simmer uncovered a few minutes, and if it is stodgy, add stock.

Notes

  • The order is the recipe. Anything that takes twenty minutes goes in early, anything that takes five goes in late, anything that takes one goes in at the end. Substitute freely inside those groups.

  • Dried beans cooked from scratch give a better bean and a starchy cooking liquid worth using in place of some of the stock. Tinned beans are entirely acceptable; rinse them, since the liquid in the tin tastes tinny.

  • Keep rinds in the freezer. The stub end of a wedge of parmesan is worth as much in a pot as anything you would buy for the purpose.

Keeping it

Five days cold and better after one night. Freeze without the pasta and greens for four months. If the pasta is already in it, accept that the leftovers are a thick vegetable stew rather than a soup, and eat them as such.
