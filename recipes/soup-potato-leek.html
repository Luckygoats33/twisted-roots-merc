Soup, Chowder & Stew

Potato Leek Soup

Four ingredients and nowhere to hide. Wash the leeks properly and do not let a blender near it for too long.

6 bowls, about 2.5 litres
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
50 minutes
Start to finish

This soup is potatoes, leeks, stock and butter, which means every mistake shows. There are three worth knowing about. Grit, because leeks grow in sand and hold it between their layers, and one unwashed leek ruins the whole pot in a way nothing fixes. Browning, because leeks cooked hot go sweet and dark and turn what should be a pale, delicate soup into something muddy. And over-blending, because potato starch cells burst under a high-speed blade and release enough free starch to turn a smooth soup into wallpaper paste in about fifteen seconds.

What you need

Soup

  • 4 large leeks, about 900 g, white and pale green parts only
  • 60 g butter
  • 900 g Yukon gold or russet potatoes, peeled and cut in 2 cm chunks
  • 1.25 litres vegetable or light stock
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons fine salt, plus more to taste
  • 1/2 teaspoon white pepper

Finishing

  • 125 ml heavy cream or whole milk, warmed
  • 2 tablespoons chopped chives
  • 1 tablespoon good olive oil, for drizzling
  • A pinch of flaky salt

How

  1. Split the leeks lengthwise, then slice them. Drop the slices into a big bowl of cold water and swish them hard with your hands so the layers open and the grit falls to the bottom. Lift them out with your hands rather than pouring, which would tip the sand straight back over them. Repeat until the bottom of the bowl is clean.

  2. Melt the butter in a heavy pot over medium-low, add the leeks and a pinch of salt, and cover. Cook gently, stirring occasionally, until they have collapsed and are completely soft with no colour on them at all, twelve to fifteen minutes. If they start to brown, the heat is too high. Browned leeks make a soup that tastes fine and looks grey.

  3. Add the potatoes, stock, bay leaves, salt and white pepper. Bring to a simmer and cook until a knife goes into a potato chunk and it falls off the blade under its own weight, about twenty minutes.

  4. Pull out the bay leaves. Now blend carefully. Use an immersion blender in short bursts, or a food mill or a potato masher for a rustic version. If you use a high-speed upright blender, run it briefly and stop the moment it is smooth — thirty seconds too long and the soup turns gluey and stretchy, and there is no way back from that.

  5. Return it to low heat, stir in the warmed cream, and bring it back until it steams. Do not boil it after the dairy goes in.

  6. Taste for salt, and taste twice. A potato soup absorbs salt without seeming to change and then arrives all at once, so add it in small increments.

  7. Adjust the thickness with stock. It should pour smoothly off a ladle and coat the back of a spoon lightly. It will thicken considerably as it cools, so leave it slightly looser than you want it.

  8. Serve with chives, a drizzle of olive oil and a pinch of flaky salt.

Notes

  • Cold, this is vichyssoise. Blend it completely smooth, add an extra 125 ml of cream, chill it thoroughly, and taste for salt again — cold food needs noticeably more seasoning than hot food to taste the same.

  • Do not throw the dark green tops away. They are too tough for this soup and perfect in the stock pot.

  • Russets break down more and give a fluffier, thicker soup. Yukon golds hold together and give a silkier, more buttery one. Waxy red potatoes give a soup that never quite thickens.

Keeping it

Four days cold. It thickens hard in the cold and needs thinning with stock or milk. It does not freeze well — potato turns grainy and mealy on thawing, and the cream separates. If you must freeze it, do so before the cream goes in and accept a slightly grainy texture.
