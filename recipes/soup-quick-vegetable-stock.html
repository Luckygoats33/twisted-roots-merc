Soup, Chowder & Stew

Quick Vegetable Stock

Forty-five minutes, no bones, and better than what comes in a carton. Built from what you were going to throw out.

About 2 litres
Makes
15 minutes
Hands on
50 minutes
Start to finish

Vegetable stock is not a shorter version of meat stock, it is a different thing with different rules. There is no collagen to extract, so long cooking gains you nothing and actively costs you — after about forty-five minutes the vegetables start giving up bitter and sulphurous compounds and the stock turns muddy and cabbagey. So it is a fast job. The other rule is about what goes in. Onion, carrot, celery, leek, mushroom, tomato and herb stems are all good. Brassicas, potato peel, beetroot and anything from the cabbage family will dominate or cloud it, no matter how small the quantity looks.

What you need

Base

  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 2 large onions, roughly chopped, skins on for colour
  • 3 carrots, roughly chopped
  • 4 ribs celery with leaves, chopped
  • 1 leek, split and washed, all of it including the green
  • 200 g mushrooms or mushroom stems

Flavour

  • 1 head garlic, halved crosswise
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 1 teaspoon black peppercorns
  • 6 stems parsley
  • 4 sprigs thyme
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce, optional
  • 15 g dried mushrooms, optional

Liquid

  • 2.5 litres water

How

  1. Heat the oil in a large pot over medium-high and add the onion, carrot, celery, leek and mushrooms. Cook, stirring occasionally, until the onions are translucent and the pot bottom has picked up some brown, about ten minutes. This browning is where most of the depth comes from, so do not rush past it into a pale pot.

  2. Add the tomato paste and stir it around the hot pan until it darkens to brick and smells sweet instead of metallic, about a minute.

  3. Pour in the water, scraping the bottom of the pot until nothing brown is stuck to it. Add the garlic, bay, peppercorns, herbs, and the soy and dried mushrooms if you are using them.

  4. Bring to a boil, then drop to a gentle simmer with steady small bubbles. Vegetable stock does not need the delicate handling that bone stock needs — there is no fat to emulsify and no collagen to shred — so a real simmer is fine and faster.

  5. Cook for thirty to forty minutes, no longer. It is ready when the carrot has gone completely soft and pale, the liquid has taken on colour, and it tastes sweet and rounded rather than sharp and raw. Past three quarters of an hour it starts going bitter and there is no way back.

  6. Strain through a fine sieve without pressing the solids. Pressing gives you a cloudy stock full of vegetable particles that will cook into a grey sludge in whatever you use it for.

  7. Taste it. If it is thin, put it back on the heat and reduce it by a quarter, which concentrates rather than adds. Do not salt it — you will be seasoning the finished dish, and a salted stock that gets reduced becomes inedible.

  8. Cool quickly in shallow containers, then keep it cold or freeze it.

Notes

  • The scrap container: keep a bag in the freezer and add onion ends, carrot peel, celery butts, leek greens, mushroom stems, parsley stalks and corn cobs. When it is full, you have a stock. Leave out anything from the cabbage family, potato peel, and artichoke, all of which take over.

  • Corn cobs deserve a specific mention. Stripped cobs simmered in water give a sweet, faintly milky stock that is the best possible base for corn chowder, and they cost nothing.

  • Roasting first makes it darker and sweeter — spread the vegetables on a tray at 425°F (220°C) until browned at the edges, then proceed. It adds forty minutes and turns a light stock into something that will stand up in a stew.

Keeping it

Four days cold and three months frozen. It freezes well in flat bags, which stack. Reduce it by half before freezing if space is short, and add back the water when you use it.
