Soup, Chowder & Stew

Stock from Bones

Roast them dark, cover them cold, and never let it boil. Everything else in this category starts here.

About 4 litres
Makes
40 minutes
Hands on
8 hours, mostly unattended
Start to finish

Everything else on this page is only as good as what you built it on, and a stock is three decisions. Roast the bones or do not — roasted gives colour and roasted flavour, raw gives a cleaner, paler stock. Start in cold water so the proteins leach out slowly and rise as scum you lift off, rather than seizing and clouding the pot. And hold it below a simmer, because a rolling boil emulsifies fat into the liquid and shreds the connective tissue into cloudy particles that will never settle out. A stock at a bare tremble for eight hours gives you something that sets to jelly when cold, which is the only test that matters.

What you need

Bones

  • 2.5 kg beef or elk bones, a mix of marrow, knuckle and neck
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Aromatics

  • 2 large onions, halved, skins left on
  • 3 carrots, cut in thick lengths
  • 3 ribs celery, cut in thick lengths
  • 1 head garlic, halved crosswise
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 1 teaspoon black peppercorns
  • 6 sprigs thyme
  • 10 stems parsley

Liquid

  • 5 litres cold water, plus more as needed
  • 125 ml red wine or water, for deglazing

How

  1. Heat the oven to 450°F (230°C). Toss the bones with the oil, spread them on a heavy roasting tray in a single layer, and roast until they are deep brown and the fat has rendered out and gone golden in the bottom of the tray, about forty minutes. Pale bones give a weak, flat stock.

  2. Add the onions, carrots and celery to the tray, brush the bones with the tomato paste, and roast another twenty minutes, until the vegetables have browned at the edges and the tomato paste has darkened from red to brick.

  3. Move everything into a tall stockpot. Put the roasting tray over a burner, pour in the wine, and scrape hard with a wooden spoon until every brown patch has lifted off the metal and the liquid has reduced by half. Tip that into the pot. Leaving it behind throws away a third of your flavour.

  4. Cover with cold water by about 5 cm. Cold, not hot: it draws proteins out gradually and they coagulate at the surface where you skim them, instead of clouding the whole pot at once.

  5. Bring it up slowly. As it approaches a simmer a grey-brown foam will form. Skim it off with a ladle until what rises is white or clear rather than muddy grey, then add the garlic, bay, peppercorns and herbs.

  6. Now bring it down to where the surface barely trembles and a bubble breaks maybe once every two or three seconds. This is the single most important instruction here. If it is rolling, the stock will be cloudy and greasy and no amount of straining fixes it.

  7. Hold it there for six to eight hours, topping up with hot water if the bones ever break the surface. It is done when the liquid tastes of beef rather than of water, has a slight body to it on the tongue, and the knuckle bones have gone soft enough to break with tongs.

  8. Strain through a fine sieve into a clean pot. Do not press the solids, which forces particles through and clouds it. Discard the vegetables — they have given everything they had and taste of nothing.

  9. Cool it fast, which is a food safety matter as much as a quality one. Set the pot in a sink of ice water and stir, or divide into shallow containers, and get it cold within a couple of hours rather than leaving a warm stockpot on the stove overnight.

  10. Once cold, lift the solid cap of fat off the top and keep it for roasting potatoes. Good stock underneath will have set to a wobbling jelly. If it is liquid, it still tastes fine but it had less collagen than you wanted — use more knuckle and joint bones next time.

Notes

  • Stock versus broth: stock is made from bones and is about body and gelatin, seasoned later. Broth is made from meat and is seasoned to drink. This is stock, so it is deliberately unsalted — you will be reducing it in other dishes and salt concentrates.

  • Vinegar is sometimes added on the theory that it draws minerals from the bone. The effect on nutrition is negligible; the effect on flavour at more than a tablespoon is not. Leave it out.

  • Remouillage: the spent bones will give a second, weaker stock if you cover them again and go another four hours. It is not as good, but it is free, and it makes a fine base for a vegetable soup.

Keeping it

Five days cold, six months frozen. Freeze it in a way that suits how you cook: 500 ml containers for soup, an ice cube tray for pan sauces. Reduced hard to a syrup and frozen in cubes it takes a tenth of the space, and one cube plus 250 ml of water is roughly the original stock again.
