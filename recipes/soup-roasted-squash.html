Soup, Chowder & Stew

Roasted Squash Soup

Roast the squash instead of boiling it. Boiled squash soup tastes like a wet vegetable and no amount of cream fixes it.

6 bowls, about 2.5 litres
Makes
25 minutes
Hands on
1 hour 20 minutes
Start to finish

Squash is mostly water and sugar, and how you cook it decides which of those you end up tasting. Simmered in stock, it dilutes into something pale and vaguely sweet that people try to rescue with cream and nutmeg. Roasted, it loses water and its sugars caramelise into something concentrated and nutty that needs very little help. The extra step is forty minutes of oven time you spend doing something else. Butternut, kabocha and red kuri all work; kabocha is drier and gives the thickest soup, butternut is the easiest to peel and the most forgiving.

What you need

Roasting tray

  • 1.6 kg winter squash, halved and seeded
  • 2 onions, cut in thick wedges
  • 1 head garlic, top cut off
  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 2 teaspoons fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

Pot

  • 2 tablespoons butter or olive oil
  • 1 tablespoon grated fresh ginger
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground coriander
  • A pinch of cayenne
  • 1.25 litres vegetable or light stock
  • 1 tablespoon cider vinegar or lime juice
  • Fine salt, to taste

Finishing

  • 125 ml coconut milk or heavy cream
  • 3 tablespoons pumpkin seeds, toasted
  • 2 tablespoons good olive oil
  • 1 tablespoon chopped sage or parsley

How

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F (220°C). Rub the squash halves, onion wedges and the cut head of garlic with the olive oil and season them. Lay the squash cut side down on a heavy tray, with the onion and garlic alongside.

  2. Roast for forty to fifty minutes, until a knife pushes through the skin of the squash with no resistance, the cut faces have browned where they met the tray, and the onions have gone soft and caught colour at the tips. That browning on the cut face is where the flavour is, so do not pull it early because the flesh is technically soft.

  3. Let it cool enough to handle, then scoop the flesh from the skin and squeeze the roasted garlic cloves out of their skins.

  4. Warm the butter in a heavy pot over medium heat, add the ginger and the ground spices, and cook for about thirty seconds, until they smell strongly toasted. Ground spice added straight to liquid stays dusty and raw-tasting; bloomed in fat it does what it is supposed to.

  5. Add the squash flesh, roasted onions and garlic, and the stock. Break the squash up with a spoon and simmer for ten minutes, until everything is soft enough to fall apart against the side of the pot.

  6. Blend until completely smooth. Squash has no free starch to speak of, so unlike potato soup it will not turn gluey no matter how long you run the blender — take it as smooth as you like.

  7. Thin with stock to the consistency you want. Season with salt and add the vinegar or lime, which is doing more work than anything else on this list; a squash soup without acid tastes flat and cloying no matter how much salt goes in.

  8. Stir in the coconut milk or cream and warm through until it steams. Serve with toasted pumpkin seeds, a drizzle of oil and the herbs. The seeds matter — this is a completely smooth soup and it needs one crunchy thing.

Notes

  • Peeling raw squash is not necessary. Roast it in halves, skin on, and scoop. The only reason to peel and cube is if you want browned edges on every piece, which is a legitimate choice and takes twice the tray space.

  • Apple or pear, one, cored and roasted alongside, adds acidity and a little more sweetness. Do not add both apple and maple syrup; the soup ends up like dessert.

  • It tastes flat: almost always acid, occasionally salt, never more cream. Cream mutes an already mild soup.

Keeping it

Five days cold. This is one of the better freezers in this category — four months, with no starch or dairy issues if you freeze it before the cream goes in. It thickens in the cold; thin it with stock and re-season, since freezing dulls both the salt and the acid.
