Soup, Chowder & Stew

Smoked Salmon Chowder

Hot-smoked salmon, potato and dill. The smoke does most of the work, so the base stays quiet.

6 bowls, about 2.5 litres
Makes
35 minutes
Hands on
50 minutes
Start to finish

Smoked salmon chowder goes wrong in one direction almost every time: too much smoke, too much salt, and a base built as though the salmon needed help. It does not. Hot-smoked salmon is intensely flavoured and already salted through the cure, so the job here is to build a mild, slightly sweet potato and leek base and then fold the salmon in at the end, off the heat, where it warms without falling into threads. Dill and lemon keep it from going heavy. Use hot-smoked salmon, the flaky kind, not the cold-smoked slices, which dissolve into a soup and leave it tasting oddly of nothing.

What you need

Base

  • 3 tablespoons butter
  • 2 leeks, white and pale green only, split, washed and sliced
  • 2 ribs celery, diced
  • 1 fennel bulb, diced, optional
  • 3 cloves garlic, minced
  • 35 g all-purpose flour
  • 1 litre vegetable or light stock
  • 700 g Yukon gold potatoes, cut in 1.5 cm dice
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

To finish

  • 400 g hot-smoked salmon, skin removed, broken into large flakes
  • 300 ml half and half, warmed
  • 3 tablespoons chopped fresh dill
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • Fine salt, to taste
  • 60 g corn kernels, optional

How

  1. Melt the butter in a heavy pot over medium heat and add the leeks, celery and fennel with a pinch of salt. Cook until everything has collapsed and gone completely soft and sweet-smelling with no colour on it, eight to ten minutes. Leeks that still have bite will stay stringy in the finished soup.

  2. Add the garlic and cook until fragrant, about thirty seconds, then sprinkle over the flour and stir. Cook the roux until it smells like toasted crackers and turns pale straw, about two minutes, no darker. This is a light chowder and a browned roux would both colour it and weaken its thickening.

  3. Add the stock a ladle at a time, stirring each addition smooth before the next. Add the potatoes, bay leaves and pepper.

  4. Bring to a gentle simmer and cook until a knife enters a potato cube with no resistance and a few of the smaller pieces have started to break down and cloud the liquid, about fifteen minutes. That breakdown is doing half the thickening.

  5. Take the pot off the heat entirely. Stir in the warmed half and half. Keep the soup below a simmer from here — dairy that boils in a soup this thin will curdle into grains, and adding the salmon to a boiling pot would shred it.

  6. Fold in the salmon flakes, the corn if you are using it, the dill and the lemon juice. Set over low heat only until steam rises and the salmon is warmed through, two or three minutes.

  7. Taste before you add any salt. Between the smoked salmon and the stock there may already be enough. A properly hot-smoked salmon has been taken to an internal temperature of 145 degrees Fahrenheit (63°C) during smoking and is fully cooked, so all you are doing is warming it.

  8. Serve with more dill and a great deal of black pepper. It wants a heel of sourdough alongside more than it wants crackers.

Notes

  • If you are smoking the salmon yourself, hot-smoke it to an internal temperature of 145 degrees Fahrenheit (63°C) checked at the thickest part with an instant-read thermometer. Cold-smoked salmon is cured but not cooked and is the wrong ingredient for this soup on both counts.

  • It went stringy: the salmon was added too early or the pot was too hot. Fold it in off the heat and treat it as the last step, not an ingredient that cooks.

  • Salmon skin, if you have it, crisped hard in a dry pan and crumbled over the top, is the best garnish this soup will ever get.

Keeping it

Three days cold. Reheat over low heat, stirring, and stop when it steams. It does not freeze — the potato goes grainy and the dairy separates. Leftovers thin with a splash of milk, because it thickens considerably overnight.
