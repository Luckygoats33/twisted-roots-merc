Soup, Chowder & Stew

Tomato Soup for Grilled Cheese

Roasted tinned tomatoes, a little butter, no cream to speak of. Built to be dipped into.

6 bowls, about 2 litres
Makes
20 minutes
Hands on
1 hour 15 minutes
Start to finish

A tomato soup that gets dipped into has different requirements from one eaten alone. It needs to be thick enough to cling to a corner of toasted bread, salty enough to stand up to a mouthful of cheese, and acidic enough to cut it. That rules out the very creamy versions, which turn the whole plate into one soft note. Roasting good tinned tomatoes concentrates them and adds browning that raw tomatoes never provide, and a little butter stirred in at the end gives richness without diluting anything. Tinned tomatoes are the right choice for eight months of the year on this coast, and no apology is required.

What you need

Roasting tray

  • 2 tins whole peeled tomatoes, about 800 g each, drained, juice kept
  • 2 large onions, cut in thick wedges
  • 1 head garlic, cloves peeled
  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar
  • 2 teaspoons fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

Pot

  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 tablespoon tomato paste
  • 750 ml vegetable stock
  • The reserved tomato juice, about 250 ml
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1 bay leaf
  • A small handful of basil, about 10 g

Finishing

  • 45 g cold butter, cubed
  • 60 ml heavy cream, optional
  • 1 teaspoon sherry vinegar or red wine vinegar
  • Fine salt and black pepper, to taste

How

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F (220°C). Spread the drained tomatoes, onion wedges and garlic cloves on a heavy rimmed tray, toss with the olive oil, brown sugar, salt and pepper, and arrange the tomatoes cut side up.

  2. Roast for forty to fifty minutes, until the tomatoes have collapsed and darkened with charred patches at the edges, the onions are browned and soft, and the tray has a sticky brown residue on it. Those charred patches are the whole reason to do this rather than opening a tin into a pot.

  3. Warm the olive oil and tomato paste in a heavy pot over medium heat and cook until the paste darkens to brick and smells sweet, about a minute. Scrape in everything from the roasting tray, using a little of the stock to lift the sticky residue off the metal.

  4. Add the rest of the stock, the reserved tomato juice, oregano and bay leaf. Simmer, uncovered, for twenty minutes, until the flavour has pulled together and the soup has reduced enough to coat a spoon thinly.

  5. Pull the bay leaf out and add the basil. Blend until completely smooth with an immersion blender, or in batches in an upright blender. If you use an upright, fill it no more than halfway and hold the lid down with a towel — hot soup expands violently and will paint the ceiling.

  6. For a very smooth soup, push it through a fine sieve. Tinned tomatoes are usually smooth enough that this is optional; if you used fresh, the skins and seeds make it worth doing.

  7. Return it to low heat and whisk in the cold butter a cube at a time, letting each melt before the next. This gives body and gloss without the flatness that a lot of cream brings. Add the cream too if you want it milder.

  8. Finish with the vinegar and taste hard for salt. Tomato soup is almost always under-salted, and it needs enough acid that it tastes bright rather than jammy. Serve it hot enough to steam, alongside a grilled cheese cut on the diagonal.

Notes

  • The grilled cheese it belongs with: three cheeses on sourdough, buttered on the outside and cooked slowly in a covered pan over medium-low until the cheese has fully melted and the bread is deep golden. Rushing it gives you burnt bread around unmelted cheese.

  • Fresh tomatoes only make this better for about six weeks of the year. Use 2 kg of ripe ones, halved, roasted the same way, and expect to sieve out the skins.

  • Brown sugar is not for sweetness, it is for browning. A tablespoon across two tins is not detectable as sugar in the finished soup, but the caramelisation it enables in the oven is.

Keeping it

Five days cold and genuinely better on the second day. It freezes well for four months, which is rare in this category, because there is very little dairy in it — freeze it before the butter goes in if you want it perfect. Reheat gently and re-season, since freezing dulls the salt and the acid.
