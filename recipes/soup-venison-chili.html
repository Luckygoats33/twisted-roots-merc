Soup, Chowder & Stew

Venison Chili

Ground venison, real dried chiles, and fat added back. A lean grind makes a thin, dry chili on its own.

8 bowls, about 3.5 litres
Makes
45 minutes
Hands on
2 hours 30 minutes
Start to finish

Most venison ground at a processor comes in around ninety-five percent lean, which is why so much venison chili tastes dry and grainy. The meat has nothing to carry flavour and nothing to keep the crumbles tender, so this recipe adds fat back in the form of bacon and a little beef fat. The other change worth making is the chile. Powder from a tin is mostly filler and stale; whole dried chiles toasted and blended into a paste give a chili with actual depth, colour and a low warm heat rather than a flat dusty burn. Neither change is difficult and together they make it a different dish.

What you need

Chile paste

  • 4 dried ancho chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 dried guajillo chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 1 dried chipotle, stemmed
  • 500 ml boiling water
  • 1 tablespoon cumin seed
  • 1 teaspoon coriander seed
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano

Meat

  • 1.1 kg ground venison
  • 180 g bacon, diced
  • 2 tablespoons beef fat or neutral oil
  • 2 teaspoons fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

Pot

  • 2 large onions, diced
  • 6 cloves garlic, minced
  • 1 tin diced tomatoes, about 800 g
  • 750 ml beef or game stock
  • 1 tablespoon cocoa powder
  • 1 tablespoon cider vinegar
  • 2 teaspoons fine salt, to taste
  • 800 g cooked pinto or kidney beans, drained, optional

The table

  • 150 g grated sharp cheddar
  • 120 ml sour cream
  • 4 tablespoons sliced green onion
  • 1 lime, cut in wedges

How

  1. Toast the dried chiles in a dry skillet over medium heat, pressing them flat for a few seconds a side, until they smell fragrant and toasty and have softened slightly. Watch closely — a chile that goes past toasted to scorched turns bitter and will ruin the pot.

  2. Cover the toasted chiles with the boiling water and leave them until fully soft and pliable, about twenty minutes. Meanwhile toast the cumin and coriander seed in the same dry pan until they smell strongly nutty and start to pop, then grind them.

  3. Blend the soaked chiles with about 250 ml of their soaking liquid, the ground spices and the oregano until it is a smooth paste. Taste the soaking water first; if it is bitter, use plain water instead.

  4. Render the bacon in a large heavy pot until browned and its fat has run. Lift it out and keep it.

  5. Add the beef fat and brown the venison in two batches over medium-high, pressing it flat and leaving it alone until a dark crust forms before breaking it up. A lean grind will try to steam; crowding it guarantees grey meat and no flavour. Ground meat must reach an internal temperature of 160°F (71°C), so check the thickest part with an instant-read thermometer rather than relying on colour, which is unreliable in game.

  6. Lower the heat, add the onions to the pot and cook until translucent and starting to brown, about eight minutes. Add the garlic and cook until fragrant.

  7. Add the chile paste and cook it in the hot fat, stirring, until it darkens a shade and smells roasted rather than raw, three or four minutes. This step is what separates a chili that tastes cooked from one that tastes stirred together.

  8. Return the meat and bacon and add the tomatoes, stock and cocoa powder. Bring to a simmer, then hold it at the barest bubble, uncovered, for at least ninety minutes.

  9. It is ready when the fat has separated and pooled dark red at the surface, the liquid has reduced enough that a spoon dragged through leaves a track for a moment, and the raw tomato edge has gone. If you are adding beans, put them in for the last twenty minutes so they warm without collapsing.

  10. Off the heat, add the vinegar and salt to taste. Serve with cheddar, sour cream, green onion and a lime wedge, and let people build their own bowl.

Notes

  • Beans or no beans is a regional argument with no correct answer. This pot works either way; without them, reduce the stock by 250 ml so it does not come out thin.

  • Cocoa powder is not there to make it taste of chocolate. A tablespoon in a pot this size adds bitterness and colour that reads as depth, in the same way a small amount of coffee does.

  • If it tastes flat after all that, it needs salt and acid, in that order, not more chile. Chili is very easy to over-spice and nearly impossible to un-spice.

Keeping it

Five days cold and substantially better on the second and third days — this is the clearest example in this whole category of a dish that improves overnight. It freezes for six months and reheats without complaint. Make double.
